Her family had money, and money was the problem. Robert Cassatt was a stockbroker and land speculator, her mother came from banking, and the household took long educational tours of Europe the way other families took the air. A daughter who painted was charming. A daughter who intended to earn a living at it was something else. When she announced she meant to go to Paris and train seriously, her father is said to have told her he would almost rather see her dead. He relented, then spent years paying for her lodging and food while refusing to pay for her paints.

She had already done four years at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts, where the women were kept away from live models and drew plaster casts instead, and where the male students and teachers treated the whole female contingent as a hobby class. "There was no teaching," she said later. She left without a degree — none was given — and sailed for Paris in 1866 at twenty-two. The École des Beaux-Arts did not admit women, so she paid for private lessons from its professors, got the permit to copy in the Louvre, and worked. Respectable women could not sit in the cafés where the avant-garde argued, so she missed those arguments entirely and had them at dinner parties instead.

For a decade she played the Salon's game and grew steadily more contemptuous of it. In 1875 a canvas was refused, then accepted the following year after she darkened the background. In 1877 both her entries were thrown out. At that low point Degas asked her to show with the Impressionists, and she said yes with something like relief — "we are carrying on a despairing fight," she wrote, "& need all our forces." She was the only American in the group. She had first seen Degas' pastels in a dealer's window two years earlier: "I used to go and flatten my nose against that window... It changed my life."

What she did with the freedom was paint women: reading, drinking tea, at the theatre, holding children. Not as decoration but as people thinking. It was the half of modern life the men could not get at, because they could not get into those rooms.

She never married. She advised the Havemeyers on the collection that became the Metropolitan's Impressionist core, campaigned for the vote, and lent eighteen works to a 1915 suffrage exhibition that Philadelphia society, including her own sister-in-law, boycotted. Diabetes and cataracts took her sight; after about 1914 she could not paint at all. She died in 1926, blind, in a château north-west of Paris.
