His real name was Alessandro Filipepi, and the nickname that stuck — Botticelli, "little barrel" — probably came from a portly older brother. Born in Florence around 1445, the son of a tanner, he trained under Fra Filippo Lippi, a painter-monk notorious for running off with a nun, and inherited from him a line of unearthly grace. By his mid-twenties Sandro had his own workshop and, more valuable than any commission, the friendship of the Medici, the bankers who ruled Florence in all but name. For them and their brilliant, doomed circle of poets and philosophers he painted the pictures the world remembers: goddesses stepping from the sea, spring gardens crowded with nymphs, a paganism reborn under a Christian sky.

Florence in those years was a city that could turn on you overnight. In 1478 the Pazzi family tried to murder the Medici brothers at Mass in the cathedral; Giuliano died on the floor, Lorenzo escaped, and the conspirators were hanged from the windows of the government palace. Botticelli was hired to paint their dangling corpses on the wall as a warning — a reminder that the man who gave us the Birth of Venus also worked as the state's executioner-in-effigy. His fame carried him to Rome in 1481, where he helped fresco the walls of the newly built Sistine Chapel, beneath the ceiling Michelangelo had not yet dreamed.

Then the mood of the city curdled. In the 1490s a Dominican friar named Girolamo Savonarola began thundering from the pulpit that Florence's beauty was rottenness, its art vanity, its rulers corrupt. When the Medici were driven out in 1494, Savonarola's followers built "bonfires of the vanities" and fed them with mirrors, books, and paintings. Botticelli, by many accounts, was swept up in it. His late work grows severe and grief-stricken, the sensuous line hardening into anguish.

Savonarola himself was burned in 1498, but the old joy never came back to Botticelli's brush. Fashion moved on to Leonardo and the young Michelangelo, and the painter of Venus slid into poverty and neglect. He died in 1510 and was buried in his parish church, half forgotten. For nearly four centuries the world let him lie there — until the Victorians, hunting for lost beauty, rediscovered him and made his goddesses famous all over again.
