His father was a provincial jack-of-all-trades — miniatures, sculpture, decorative work — who taught the boy to draw and to play the violin, and both stuck. Ingres was in Paris by seventeen, studying under Jacques-Louis David, the most powerful painter in France, and by twenty-one he had won the Prix de Rome. Then the state ran out of money and he sat in Paris for five years waiting for a scholarship it could not afford to pay.

When he finally reached Rome in 1806 he found that his Paris reviews had been savage, and he reacted by digging in: he would stay in Italy, he announced, and reform French painting from a distance. He stayed eighteen years. He painted the pictures the Academy had asked for and got told the nudes were not idealised enough. He sent work to the Salon and watched it get taken apart — most famously in 1819, when the critic Kératry complained that the *Grande Odalisque* had three vertebrae too many, and another wrote that the figure had no bones, no muscles, no blood, no life. They were not wrong about the anatomy. He simply did not care; the line was worth more to him than the skeleton.

He married in 1813 without having met his bride, having courted Madeleine Chapelle entirely by letter. It worked. They were together thirty-six years and were devoted; their only child was stillborn.

Then in 1824 *The Vow of Louis XIII* was a hit, and everything reversed. He came home a celebrity, took the Légion d'honneur, entered the Académie, and became the official champion of drawing, order, and Raphael against Delacroix's colour and chaos — the great artistic feud of the century. He called Delacroix the apostle of ugliness. When his own *Martyrdom of Saint Symphorian* was attacked in 1834 he took it personally, refused public commissions, and left for Rome again to run the French Academy there.

He was, by all accounts, vain, thin-skinned, and difficult, and he was also the finest draughtsman France produced. He played his violin well enough that "violon d'Ingres" entered the language as the phrase for a serious hobby. Widowed in 1849, he married again at seventy-one and was by his own account rejuvenated by it. He worked to the end — signing an enormous canvas of naked women at eighty-two — and died of pneumonia in January 1867, leaving four thousand drawings and the violin to his home town.
