Rembrandt Harmenszoon van Rijn was born in Leiden in 1606, the son of a miller wealthy enough to send him to Latin school and briefly to university — an education the boy abandoned to paint. By his mid-twenties he had learned everything the city could teach him and moved to Amsterdam, the richest boomtown in Europe, where his talent for catching a living face in paint made him an instant success. Commissions poured in. In 1634 he married Saskia van Uylenburgh, the cultured cousin of his art dealer, and painted her again and again — laughing, dressed as a goddess, pulled onto his lap. For a decade he had everything: fame, a beautiful wife, money, and a swagger that shows in the self-portraits of these years.

Then the losses began, and they never stopped. Three of his children with Saskia died in infancy; only their son Titus, born in 1641, survived. Saskia herself died in 1642, the year Rembrandt finished The Night Watch, at just twenty-nine. He took the boy's nurse as a lover, then cast her off cruelly and had her locked away when she sued him. He found real companionship at last with Hendrickje Stoffels, his young housekeeper, who bore him a daughter and stood by him — even when the Church publicly shamed her for living with him unmarried.

Money was his undoing. He spent lavishly, bought art and curiosities compulsively, and above all bought a magnificent house he could not pay for. When the loans came due in the 1650s he was forced into insolvency; in 1657 and 1658 his collection, his prints, his house, everything, was sold at auction. To keep working, Hendrickje and Titus formed a company that employed him as their servant, so his earnings could not be seized by creditors.

He kept painting, and the late work is the greatest of his life — looser, darker, unbearably honest. But the people around him kept dying. Hendrickje in 1663. Titus, newly married, in 1668. Rembrandt followed in October 1669, buried in a rented grave in the Westerkerk. He had painted his own face perhaps forty times across his life, watching himself rise, prosper, and lose it all — the most complete record of one human being ever made in oil.
