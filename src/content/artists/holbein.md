Hans Holbein was born in Augsburg around 1497 into a painter's shop — his father, Hans Holbein the Elder, ran a busy altarpiece business, and the boy learned the trade at his elbow. By 1515 he had walked south to Basel, a Swiss city of printers, humanists and money, and started at the bottom: woodcut designs, title pages, painted signboards, decorations for a schoolmaster's shopfront. He drew marginal jokes in a copy of Erasmus's *In Praise of Folly*. In 1519 he joined the guild, bought citizenship, and married Elsbeth Binsenstock, a tanner's widow with a small son. Work came steadily — town-hall murals, a terrifying panel of the dead Christ, and in 1523 the portraits of Erasmus that carried his name across Europe.

Then the Reformation arrived and took his living with it. Basel turned Protestant, altarpieces became idolatry, and by the later 1520s the commissions had dried up. Erasmus wrote him a letter of introduction and told a friend, bluntly, that "the arts are freezing" in that part of the world. In 1526 Holbein sailed for England, where Thomas More took him in and reported back to Erasmus that "your painter, dearest Erasmus, is a wonderful artist." He stayed two years, went home, found Basel busy smashing the statues out of its churches, and in 1532 left for London again. This time he did not come back. Elsbeth and the children stayed behind; passing through Basel in 1538 he was given a banquet by the city council and a pension to lure him home, and still he did not stay.

In England he rebuilt from nothing a second time. More had fallen from power, so Holbein worked instead for the German merchants of the Steelyard, then for Anne Boleyn's circle, then for Thomas Cromwell, and by 1536 for the king himself at thirty pounds a year. He painted the vast Whitehall mural of Henry VIII that burned in 1698 and survives only in copies, and he was sent across the Channel as a kind of diplomatic camera, bringing home the likenesses of women Henry might marry.

It was dangerous employment. Cromwell went to the block in 1540, partly over the Anne of Cleves marriage that Holbein's portrait had helped arrange; the painter kept his post but lost his patron. He fathered two children in England, whose names are unknown. He made his will on 7 October 1543 and was dead within weeks, probably of plague, at about forty-five. Nobody recorded where he was buried. His own face survives in a small chalk drawing, later enlarged and reworked by another hand, inscribed with his name and his age: forty-five.
