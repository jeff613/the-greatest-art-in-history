He was born in Paris in June 1848, a few weeks before the barricades went up, into a family whose politics made France unsafe. They fled for Peru when he was an infant, and his father died of a heart attack somewhere on the voyage. So Gauguin's first memories were of Lima — servants, sunlight, a wealthy Peruvian great-uncle's house — and then, at six, of being taken back to grey provincial France. The pattern of his whole life was already set: paradise is always the place you have just been removed from.

He went to sea as a young man, first in the merchant marine and then two years in the French navy, before coming ashore in 1871 and doing the most conventional thing imaginable. He became a Paris stockbroker. He was good at it. By the end of the decade he was earning some thirty thousand francs a year, had married a Danish woman named Mette-Sophie Gad, and was filling a comfortable apartment with five children and a growing collection of Impressionist canvases. Painting was his weekend hobby, pursued seriously enough that Pissarro took him on and Degas came to respect him.

Then in 1882 the Paris market crashed, the art trade contracted with it, and the safe life evaporated. Gauguin's response was not to find another job. He decided to paint full time. He moved the family to Mette's Copenhagen, failed spectacularly as a tarpaulin salesman, and in 1885 went back to Paris alone. He never really lived with his wife and children again. Whatever else he was, he was a man who had done the arithmetic and concluded that his family cost more than his freedom was worth.

What followed was fifteen years of running further out. Brittany, where he found the Breton villages primitive enough to feed him. Martinique. Nine legendary, disastrous weeks in Arles with van Gogh that ended with a razor and a severed ear. And in 1891, Tahiti — a French colony he imagined as untouched Eden and found already Christianised, taxed, and dying of imported disease. He painted the Eden regardless. He also took a thirteen-year-old girl, Teha'amana, as his *vahine*, and others after her; it is the part of his story that no honest account leaves out.

He died at Atuona in the Marquesas in May 1903, fifty-four, sick and broke, under a three-month prison sentence he was still appealing. Within a decade the paintings he could not sell were changing what European art was.
