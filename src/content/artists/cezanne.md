Paul Cézanne was born in 1839 in Aix-en-Provence, the son of a hat-maker who had turned himself into a prosperous banker. His father, Louis-Auguste, co-founded the only bank in town and expected his boy to study law and inherit a respectable career. Paul dutifully enrolled — and hated every minute of it. His deepest bond was with a schoolmate named Émile Zola, a poor, fatherless boy who would grow up to become France's most famous novelist. The two roamed the hills around Aix reciting poetry and swimming in the river, dreaming of glory in Paris.

In 1861 Cézanne gave up the law and followed Zola to the capital. What awaited him was two decades of failure. The official Salon rejected his dark, thickly painted canvases again and again. Critics who saw his work in the first Impressionist exhibition of 1874 treated him as the group's clown, the crude provincial who could not draw. He was awkward, quick to rage, and painfully unsure of himself. Only Camille Pissarro, the gentle elder of the Impressionists, took him seriously, coaxing him out of doors near Pontoise to lighten his palette and paint directly from the landscape.

Slowly Cézanne stopped chasing Paris and retreated to Provence, where the light was hard and clear and no one bothered him. He painted the same subjects obsessively: bathers, bowls of fruit, the men who played cards in the local café, and above all Mont Sainte-Victoire, the limestone mountain outside Aix that he returned to for the last twenty years of his life. He worked with agonising slowness, laying patch beside patch of colour, trying to make the fleeting sensations of Impressionism "something solid and durable, like the art of the museums."

The world caught up late. His father's death in 1886 left him rich and free, and in 1895 the young dealer Ambroise Vollard gave him a solo show that stunned a new generation — Matisse and Picasso would later call him "the father of us all." Cézanne barely noticed the acclaim. In October 1906, still working outdoors, he was caught in a storm, collapsed, and died a few days later at sixty-seven, painting to the very end.
