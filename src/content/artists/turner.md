Joseph Mallord William Turner was born in 1775 above a barber's shop in the grubby heart of Covent Garden, the son of a wig-maker and a mother who would end her life confined in an asylum. He had almost no formal education and spoke with a rough London accent his whole life. What he had instead was a freakish, precocious eye. At fourteen he entered the Royal Academy schools; his father proudly displayed the boy's drawings in the shop window with prices chalked beside them. By twenty-seven Turner was a full Academician — one of the youngest ever elected — and rich enough to open his own gallery.

He devoured the world by sketchbook, tramping thousands of miles through Britain, the Alps, and Italy, filling hundreds of notebooks with rivers, ruins, and weather. And weather was his true subject. Where other landscapists painted solid places, Turner painted the atmosphere between things — mist, spray, blinding sun, the smoke of the new machines. Over his long career his canvases grew steadily less about objects and more about light, until ships, bridges, and mountains seemed to be dissolving into radiant storms of paint. Critics were baffled; one dismissed a late seascape as "soapsuds and whitewash."

He needed no one's approval. Turner was famously secretive, miserly, and solitary, obsessed with his own reputation and the fate of his work. He never married, fathered two daughters he barely acknowledged, and in old age lived semi-incognito in a Chelsea cottage under the false name "Mr Booth," so unrecognizable that neighbors took the great painter for a retired admiral.

His champion was the young critic John Ruskin, who declared Turner the supreme painter of nature and defended him in volumes of passionate prose. When Turner died in 1851, he left an astonishing hoard — some three hundred oil paintings and around thirty thousand works on paper — to the British nation, on the condition they be kept together. He was buried, as he wished, in St Paul's Cathedral, among the country's heroes. Half a century later, his blazing, half-abstract late canvases would look uncannily like prophecies of modern art.
