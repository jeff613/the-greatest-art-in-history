For centuries people believed Jan van Eyck had invented oil painting. He hadn't — painters had mixed pigment with oil long before him — but the legend tells you how astonishing his pictures looked to the people who first saw them. Where others painted, van Eyck seemed to trap light itself: a brass chandelier, a fur trim, a single pearl, a face reflected in a convex mirror, all rendered with a clarity so uncanny that rivals were convinced he had discovered some secret substance. The truth was patience. He built his panels from dozens of translucent glazes, each layer thin as breath, until the surface glowed from within.

He was born around 1390, probably in Maaseik, and he first surfaces in the records not as a struggling artist but as a man of standing. By 1422 he was court painter to a Dutch count; by 1425 he had joined the household of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, one of the richest and most powerful men in Europe. Van Eyck was no ordinary craftsman there. He held the title valet de chambre, a mark of personal trust, and Philip stood godfather to one of his children. The duke paid him handsomely and, tellingly, kept paying him even in years when no paintings were delivered.

That is because van Eyck's job was not only to paint. He was a courtier and a diplomat, dispatched on missions the ducal accounts describe only as "secret," for which he received sums many times his annual wage. In 1428 he sailed to Portugal as part of an embassy sent to arrange Philip's marriage to Isabella of Portugal — and painted the bride from life so the duke could inspect his intended before committing. A man who could both keep confidences and record a face exactly was doubly useful.

From 1429 he lived in Bruges, the humming Flemish port where Italian bankers and Baltic merchants crossed paths, and there he made the works that changed European art: the towering Ghent Altarpiece, the enigmatic Arnolfini double portrait, the piercing bust often taken for his own likeness. He signed and dated his paintings — a rare, almost swaggering habit for the age — and stamped many with a motto that reads as a quiet challenge: *Als Ich Can*, "as I can." He died in Bruges in 1441, mourned by a duke who paid his widow a full year's salary.
