Diego Velázquez was born in Seville in 1599, in a Spain that was still the richest empire on earth and already beginning to fray at the edges. He trained under Francisco Pacheco, a learned if stiff local master, married Pacheco's daughter Juana, and set up as a painter of sober religious scenes and kitchen still lifes lit with an almost startling realism. He might have stayed a provincial star. Instead, in 1623, letters of introduction and a single trial portrait carried him to Madrid and the young King Philip IV — who was so taken with the likeness that he declared no other painter would ever portray him again.

That royal favor shaped the rest of Velázquez's life. He spent nearly four decades inside the court, painting the king, the queen, the infantas, the dwarves and jesters who lived at the palace, and the occasional god or ancient hero. Twice he escaped to Italy, first in 1629 to drink in Titian and the Venetians, and again in 1649, ostensibly to buy pictures and sculpture for the crown but also to paint, in Rome, the ferocious portrait of Pope Innocent X that alone would have made him immortal.

What set him apart was less what he painted than how honestly he looked. He gave a court dwarf the same grave dignity he gave a king. He painted surfaces — silk, silver, a wet eye, the bloom on a cheek — with loose, almost careless strokes that dissolve into pure paint up close and snap into breathing life at a distance. Later painters treated him as a kind of secret founder of modern art; Manet called him "the painter of painters."

Yet Velázquez himself hungered for something his brush could not give him: rank. He climbed the palace hierarchy, taking on tedious court offices, and spent years lobbying for a knighthood in the Order of Santiago, an honor reserved for men of pure noble blood who had never worked with their hands — which a painter, by definition, had. Only royal pressure and a papal dispensation finally won it for him in 1659. He wore the honor for barely a year. In the summer of 1660, worn down by court duties, he fell ill and died; Juana followed him within days.
