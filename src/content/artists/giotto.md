No one is quite sure when or where Giotto was born, or what he looked like — the portrait that survives was painted more than a century after his death, one head in a row of famous Florentines. Tradition places his birth around 1267 in the Mugello hills north of Florence, and tells a story too good to check: that the painter Cimabue, riding through the countryside, came upon a shepherd boy scratching a lifelike sheep onto a flat rock, and carried him back to Florence to learn the trade. Scholars now suspect his family was comfortably off and simply apprenticed him in the city. But the legend stuck because it fit the man. Giotto really did make figures that looked as if they had walked in from the fields — solid, heavy, breathing people, where a thousand years of painting had offered only flat gold saints.

That was the revolution. For a millennium the Byzantine icon had been the correct way to show the holy: weightless, symmetrical, gazing out of a goldground eternity. Giotto gave his people bodies that took up space, robes that fell under their own weight, and above all faces that felt — grief, tenderness, fury, doubt. His contemporaries knew they were watching something new. The poet Dante, who may have been his friend, wrote in the Purgatorio that Cimabue had once held the field in painting but now "Giotto has the cry," his master's fame gone dim. Boccaccio said flatly that Giotto had brought back to light an art that had been buried for centuries.

He became the most sought-after painter in Italy, working in Assisi, Rome, Padua, Florence, and Naples, where King Robert of Anjou took him into the royal household. He grew rich, leased out looms, and by all accounts was small, plain, and famously quick-witted. In 1334 Florence made him master of works of its cathedral, and he designed the soaring bell tower that still bears his name, though he lived to see only its base. He died in the city in January 1337. He had not invented perspective or oil paint or anatomy — those would come later. What he had done was simpler and larger: he taught painting how to feel, and it never forgot.
