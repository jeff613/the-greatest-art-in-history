Almost nobody who knew Georges Seurat felt they knew him. He was born in Paris in December 1859, the son of a property speculator so peculiar that he lived apart from the family and came home once a week to dine, and the boy seems to have inherited the habit of withholding himself. Friends described a tall, quiet, immaculately dressed man who listened more than he spoke, worked with monastic regularity, and told them nothing about his life.

He trained the orthodox way — drawing school, then the École des Beaux-Arts in 1878 under Henri Lehmann, who had studied with Ingres — and got the standard diet of casts, life class, and copying. What actually formed him was reading. He went at the science of vision like a scholar, working through Charles Blanc on colour, Chevreul's laws of simultaneous contrast, and the American physicist Ogden Rood, and emerged convinced that a painter could put pure unmixed colours side by side and let the eye do the blending. Impressionism had been improvisation; Seurat wanted a method that could be written down.

He tested it at enormous scale. *Bathers at Asnières*, three metres wide, was rejected by the Salon in 1884, so he helped found a society that had no jury at all. Then he began *A Sunday on La Grande Jatte* and spent two years on it, producing dozens of drawings and oil studies and covering the final canvas in a fabric of small separated touches. When it appeared in 1886 the critic Félix Fénéon gave the new manner a name — Neo-Impressionism — and Seurat, at twenty-six, had a movement, disciples, and Pissarro himself painting in dots.

He also had a talent for secrecy that his friends found wounding in retrospect. He had taken a model, Madeleine Knobloch, into his studio; she bore him a son in February 1890; he introduced her to almost no one and told his mother only in the last days of his life.

In March 1891 he hung his unfinished *Circus* at the Indépendants and fell ill during the exhibition. He died on the twenty-ninth, at thirty-one, of something the doctors never agreed on — meningitis, pneumonia, diphtheria. His infant son caught the same illness and died two weeks after him. Madeleine, pregnant again, lost that child too. Seurat had been a working artist for roughly a decade, and he had changed the direction of European painting.
