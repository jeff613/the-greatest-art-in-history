Edgar Degas was born in 1834 into a comfortable Parisian banking family — his father ran the French branch of a Neapolitan bank, his mother came from Creole New Orleans — and he never entirely shed the manner of the wealthy, conservative gentleman he was raised to be. He was christened De Gas, a spelling with social pretensions, and only later flattened it to the plainer Degas. He trained the old-fashioned way, copying masters at the Louvre and spending three formative years in Italy, and he began as a painter of history and portraits, an admirer of Ingres who prized drawing above all. He was, in temperament, the least likely rebel imaginable.

Yet he threw in his lot with the young radicals. Degas helped organize the Impressionist exhibitions and showed in nearly all of them, and to the public he was one of that scandalous band. But he rejected the label furiously. He had no interest in sunlight on water or easels set up in fields; he thought painting from nature was foolish and that art should be made from memory and calculation in the studio. "No art was ever less spontaneous than mine," he said. He preferred to be called a Realist or an Independent, and what obsessed him was modern Paris itself — dancers, laundresses, milliners, jockeys, café drinkers, women at their baths — observed with a cool, unsentimental, almost clinical eye.

Around 1874 his father died and the family bank was found to be drowning in debt. Degas, a man of means, suddenly had to sell his own art collection and live by his work, which sharpened both his output and his bitterness. He never married, grew famously prickly and reclusive, and revealed an ugly, vicious antisemitism during the Dreyfus affair that cost him old friendships.

The cruelest turn was his sight. His eyes had troubled him since his thirties, and over decades a creeping maculopathy narrowed his vision until, in his final years, he was nearly blind — a man who had built everything on the precision of his eye, groping through the streets of a city he could barely see. He died in 1917, in the middle of a war, largely alone.
