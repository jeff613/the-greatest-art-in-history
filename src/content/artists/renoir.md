Pierre-Auguste Renoir was born in Limoges in 1841, the sixth child of a tailor, and grew up poor in a cramped apartment in central Paris, close to the Louvre. He had a lovely singing voice — a choirmaster at Saint-Eustache, said to have been the composer Charles Gounod, wanted to train it — but the family needed money, and at thirteen the boy was apprenticed to a porcelain factory. There he spent his days painting sprays of flowers and, on the finer pieces, the face of Marie Antoinette onto cups, vases, and plates. He was fast and gifted; the older workmen nicknamed him "Monsieur Rubens," and because he was paid by the piece he earned enough to help his parents. The prettiness and lightness of that decorative work never really left him.

When machines arrived to print designs onto china, Renoir lost the trade, and he scraped by painting fans, window blinds, and café walls until he had saved enough to study seriously. In 1861 he entered the studio of Charles Gleyre and fell in with a band of restless young painters — Claude Monet, Alfred Sisley, Frédéric Bazille — who preferred working outdoors, in daylight, to copying plaster casts. Out of that friendship came Impressionism, and in 1874 Renoir was among the rebels who mounted the first independent exhibition that gave the movement its mocking name.

He was, from the start, its painter of pleasure. While others chased weather and water, Renoir painted people — dancers, lovers, lunching friends, children, women in dappled sunlight — insisting that a picture ought to be likable and joyful, that there was already enough ugliness in the world. The late 1870s and early 1880s brought his warmest crowd scenes. In 1890 he married Aline Charigot, the young seamstress who had modeled for him; they had three sons, one of whom, Jean, became one of France's greatest film directors.

Then his body betrayed him. From the 1890s rheumatoid arthritis slowly crippled his hands; a fall from a bicycle and, later, a stroke left him frail and eventually confined to a wheelchair. He moved south to the warmth of Cagnes-sur-Mer, where — his curled fingers bandaged so the nails would not cut his palms, a brush set into his grip by an assistant — he kept painting almost to the end. He died there in 1919, having spent half a century painting happiness.
