Almost nothing certain is known about Pieter Bruegel's early life — not the year he was born, not even the town. He arrived around 1525, probably somewhere near Breda, and the first solid fact about him is professional: by 1551 he was a master in the painters' guild of Antwerp, the boom city of northern Europe, where he had trained under Pieter Coecke van Aelst, a cultivated artist with connections to the imperial court. This is the crucial thing to understand about Bruegel, because his reputation has always pulled the other way. He was not a rustic. He was an educated, well-connected urban man who counted humanists and mapmakers among his friends.

Soon after qualifying he made the long journey over the Alps to Italy, down as far as Rome and Sicily, and what stayed with him was not the marble antiquity that dazzled everyone else but the landscape itself — the mountains, the vast weather, the sense of human beings as small figures under an enormous sky. Back in Antwerp he spent years designing prints for the publisher Hieronymus Cock, learning to pack a single image with dozens of tiny, legible incidents. That crowded, panoramic instinct never left him.

Then came the paintings that made him immortal, and the odd double life behind them. His first biographer, Karel van Mander, writing in 1604, tells how Bruegel and a merchant friend, Hans Franckert, would put on peasant clothes and slip out to village fairs and weddings, passing themselves off as relatives of the bride or groom, eating and drinking and watching the dancing — then Bruegel would go home and paint it all. Whether or not the story is literally true, it captures something real: here was a sophisticate who chose to look hard at ordinary working people, at harvests and hunts and proverbs and games, and to take them seriously as subjects.

In 1563 he married his old master's daughter, Mayken Coecke, and moved to Brussels, where his patrons included the financier Nicolaes Jonghelinck and even Cardinal Granvelle, the Habsburg chief minister. He had barely six years left. He died in 1569, still young, leaving two infant sons who would carry the name into a painting dynasty — and leaving a handful of panels that turned the whole world of ordinary life into high art.
