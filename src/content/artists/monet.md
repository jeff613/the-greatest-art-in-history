Claude Monet spent the first half of his life broke and the second half building the most famous garden on earth. He was raised in the Channel port of Le Havre, a boy who drew mocking caricatures of local worthies until a gruff landscape painter named Eugène Boudin dragged him outdoors and showed him that the real subject was light on water, changing by the minute. Monet never got over it. He would chase that changing light for the next seventy years.

In Paris he fell in with Renoir, Sisley, and Bazille, a band of young painters the official Salon repeatedly turned away. Rejected by the establishment, they staged their own show in 1874, and a sneering critic seized on the title of one Monet canvas — Impression, Sunrise — to christen the whole group "Impressionists." The insult stuck, and so did the mission: painting quickly, outdoors, catching the fleeting moment before it changed.

The triumph took decades to arrive, and the years in between were brutal. Monet begged bread money from friends, dodged creditors, and moved his family from town to town one step ahead of the rent. His wife Camille, the model for so many of his sunlit figures, died in 1879 at thirty-two, worn down by illness and hardship; Monet, half-mad with grief, found himself compulsively painting the changing colors on her dead face and hated himself for it. He was already, by then, living alongside Alice Hoschedé and her children, the family he would keep for the rest of his life.

Fame and money came at last in the 1890s, and Monet did something singular with them. Settling at Giverny, he bought land, diverted a river, and dug a water garden with a Japanese bridge and a pond of lilies — then spent his final thirty years painting that pond obsessively, in every light, as his eyes clouded with cataracts and the world dissolved into color. He painted the huge Water Lilies murals nearly blind, as a gift to a France shattered by the First World War. When he died in 1926, the mocked young radical had become a national monument — the man who taught the modern eye how to see.
