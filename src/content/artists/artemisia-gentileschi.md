She was born in Rome in July 1593, the eldest of Orazio Gentileschi's children and the only one of them who could paint. Her mother died when she was twelve. After that she kept her father's house and learned his trade in his workshop, because there was no other door open — the academies did not take women, the life class did not take women, and no woman could sign a contract for a commission. By seventeen she had produced a picture better than most men in Rome could manage.

In May 1611 Agostino Tassi raped her. Orazio had hired him, a colleague from a Roman commission, to come to the house and teach his daughter perspective. In March 1612 Orazio brought a suit — under the law of the time the wrong was done to the family, not to her — and the case ran seven months. Artemisia testified in detail and in public. To test whether she was lying, the court applied the *sibille*: cords wound round her fingers and drawn tight. The transcripts survive, and what she said as they tightened was that she had told the truth and would go on telling it. At one point she turned to Tassi and said, this is the ring you give me, and these are your promises. It also emerged in court that he had been accused of raping his sister-in-law and of hiring men to murder his wife. He was convicted. The sentence was annulled, and he was at liberty in Rome within a year.

A month after the verdict she was married off to a Florentine painter, Pierantonio Stiattesi, and left the city.

Florence made her. She learned to read and write there, sold to the Medici, painted a ceiling figure for Michelangelo Buonarroti the Younger — later draped over by another artist to spare the modesty of the women of the house — became friendly with Galileo, and in 1616 became the first woman ever admitted to the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno, which meant she could finally trade under her own name. She also ran up debts, and took a lover, and by 1620 had to go.

What followed was thirty more years of work: Rome, Venice, and from 1630 Naples, where she ran a proper workshop with assistants and took altarpieces and cathedral commissions. In 1638 she crossed to London to work at the court of Charles I alongside her father, who died there the next February. Then Naples again, and letters — bargaining, complaining about money, refusing to be underpaid.

She died in Naples, and nobody wrote it down. The year usually given is 1653; documents surfaced since put her still in the city, still paying her taxes, in 1654, and she may have been among the dead of the plague that emptied Naples two years after that.
