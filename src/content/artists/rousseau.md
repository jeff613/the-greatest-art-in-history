He came from a tinsmith's house in Laval and never entirely got out of it. School was a struggle in everything except drawing and music, and by his late teens he was a clerk in a lawyer's office in Angers, where in 1863 he was accused of taking twenty francs from his employer. He enlisted in the army, which did not save him — he served a month in prison the following year — and he spent roughly four years in uniform without ever leaving France. That is worth stating plainly, because he spent the rest of his life letting people believe otherwise. He liked to imply he had been sent to Mexico with the French expedition. Historians have found no evidence he went anywhere; the likeliest source of his Mexican stories is men in his regiment who had.

His father died and he came to Paris to support his mother, and in 1871 he got the job that named him. He was a clerk of the octroi, the municipal toll service that taxed wine, meat, timber and everything else that entered the city. He sat in a booth at a gate and wrote down what went past. Friends called him *le Douanier*, the customs man, which was a joke and a slight misnomer: the customs service was a different, grander thing. He worked at it for more than twenty years, and started painting seriously in his forties.

From 1886 he showed every year at the Salon des Indépendants, which had no jury and no prizes and would hang anyone who paid. Critics used him as comic relief. Visitors laughed in front of his canvases. He kept going, retired in 1893 on a pension too small to live on, taught violin and drawing to make up the difference, and painted lions.

He was, by every account, credulous. In 1907 an acquaintance drew him into a bank fraud; his advocate reportedly held up one of his pictures in the courtroom and asked whether a man who painted like that could possibly have understood a cheque. He got a suspended sentence.

Meanwhile the young were arriving. Picasso bought one of his canvases off a junk dealer and threw him a banquet in 1908; Delaunay, Brâncuși and Apollinaire took him seriously when almost nobody else did. He died in September 1910 of a clot after a gangrenous leg, and seven people came to the grave.
