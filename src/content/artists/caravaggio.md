He was named Michelangelo, after the greatest artist of the age, and he spent his short life proving that genius and disgrace could share a single body. Born in Milan in 1571, Michelangelo Merisi lost his father to plague at six and his mother by thirteen. At fourteen he was apprenticed to a competent Milanese painter, learned his craft, sold the small inheritance his family left him, and by his early twenties had drifted to Rome with nothing. He slept rough, fell ill, and painted fruit and flowers into the corners of other men's canvases for a few coins.

What he did next changed European painting. He threw out the graceful, idealized figures everyone else was making and painted people as they actually looked — dirty feet, weathered faces, torn sleeves — lit by a hard raking light that plunged everything else into darkness. He used prostitutes as Madonnas and street boys as angels. Critics were scandalized; cardinals were transfixed. When his Calling of Saint Matthew went up in a French church in 1600, Rome had never seen anything like it, and every ambitious young painter in the city began imitating him.

But the same violence that charged his art ran loose in his life. The Roman police archives are thick with Caravaggio: sword fights, thrown stones, a plate of artichokes hurled at a waiter, a libel suit, a night in the cells. In May 1606, in a brawl over a wager and a woman, he killed a man named Ranuccio Tomassoni. This time there was no smoothing it over. The pope issued a bando capitale — a death sentence that let anyone in the Papal States kill him on sight — and Caravaggio ran.

The last four years are a fugitive's blur: Naples, Malta, Sicily, Naples again, painting masterpieces in every port while powerful friends lobbied Rome for a pardon. He was ambushed and slashed across the face outside a tavern. In the summer of 1610 he loaded paintings meant as bribes for the pope's nephew onto a boat heading north, only to be detained, separated from his cargo, and left to die of fever on the beach at Porto Ercole. He was thirty-eight. The pardon, it is thought, was already being drawn up.
