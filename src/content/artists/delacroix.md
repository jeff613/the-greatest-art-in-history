Eugène Delacroix was a paradox in a frock coat: a fastidious, aristocratic Parisian dandy who filled his canvases with slaughter, sensuality, and roaring color. Born in 1798 to a high official of the French Republic — though rumor whispered his real father was the sly diplomat Talleyrand — he was orphaned young and grew up cultured, bookish, and ambitious. He read Dante, Shakespeare, and Byron, played the violin, and moved through Parisian salons with cool refinement. On canvas, all that restraint detonated.

He trained in the studio of Pierre-Narcisse Guérin but learned far more from two obsessions: the fleshy warmth of Rubens and the raw modern nerve of his friend Théodore Géricault, whose Raft of the Medusa showed him that contemporary catastrophe could be high art. Delacroix's own debut, The Barque of Dante, announced a talent unafraid of horror. Two years later The Massacre at Chios turned a real atrocity of the Greek war into a Salon sensation, and in 1827 The Death of Sardanapalus — a swirling orgy of murder and jewels — scandalized critics so badly that the government warned him his career was in danger.

Then came 1830. When the July Revolution swept Charles X off the throne in three violent days on the Paris barricades, Delacroix, who watched more than he fought, channeled the upheaval into Liberty Leading the People — the image that would become the visual anthem of French freedom. He was, almost against his will, crowned leader of the Romantic movement, locked in a career-long duel with the icy classicist Ingres over the soul of French painting: color versus line, passion versus order.

A journey to Morocco in 1832 gave him a lifetime of subjects and a sun-struck new palette. His later decades were spent partly on vast murals for Paris's public buildings and churches, and partly on the extraordinary private Journal he kept for thirty years — one of the great diaries in art. Frequently ill, endlessly working, he died in 1863 and was buried at Père Lachaise, having taught the next generation that a painting could burn.
