Raphael was born in Urbino in 1483, in one of the most cultivated small courts in Italy, and he seemed to arrive already knowing how to please. His father, Giovanni Santi, was the duke's court painter, so the boy grew up amid pictures, poetry, and the polished manners of a Renaissance palace. It was an inheritance that shaped him twice over: he learned to paint, and he learned to charm. But it was also a childhood of loss. His mother died when he was eight, his father when he was eleven, and Raphael was left an orphan with a workshop to his name and a precocious gift he would spend the rest of his short life spending.

He trained in Umbria under Perugino, whose sweet, balanced compositions he absorbed so completely that early works are hard to tell apart from the master's. Then, around 1504, he went to Florence and everything sped up. Leonardo and Michelangelo were both in the city, and Raphael — younger, humbler, endlessly observant — studied them like a scholar, quietly stealing Leonardo's soft modeling and Michelangelo's muscular power and fusing them into something graceful and wholly his own. Where the other two were difficult, secretive, tormented, he was easy company, and it served him.

In 1508 that talent for both painting and people carried him to Rome and the court of Pope Julius II, who set him to fresco the papal apartments while Michelangelo, a few rooms away, sweated over the Sistine ceiling. The rivalry defined the age. Raphael won the papal heart. Under Julius and then the pleasure-loving Leo X he became the most sought-after artist in Europe, so buried in commissions — frescoes, altarpieces, portraits, tapestry designs, even the rebuilding of St. Peter's and a survey of ancient Rome — that he ran a workshop of some fifty assistants, an art factory unlike anything before it.

He lived like a prince, well dressed and well loved, and by legend it was love that killed him: Vasari claimed a fever brought on by too many nights with his mistress. On Good Friday, 6 April 1520, he died at thirty-seven — the same day, it was believed, on which he had been born. Rome mourned as if a ruler had fallen, and he was buried, as he wished, inside the Pantheon.
