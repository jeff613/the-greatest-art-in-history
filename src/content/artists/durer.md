Albrecht Dürer was born in Nuremberg in 1471, the third of eighteen children of a goldsmith who had emigrated from Hungary. He began in his father's workshop, learning to push a graver through metal — a skill that would later make him the greatest engraver who ever lived — before he talked his way into an apprenticeship with the painter Michael Wolgemut, whose shop also churned out woodcut illustrations for the city's booming printing trade. Nuremberg was a free imperial city thick with merchants, humanists, and presses, and the young Dürer grew up understanding that a picture could be a product, sold in quantity, carried across borders.

That insight made him rich and famous in a way no northern artist had been before. When he crossed the Alps to Venice in the 1490s he saw what Italians were doing with proportion, perspective, and the dignity of the artist, and he came home determined to fuse their theory with German precision. His 1498 Apocalypse — fifteen thunderous woodcuts he designed, published, and marketed himself — made his name from Lisbon to Kraków. He turned the humble printed sheet, until then a cheap devotional trinket, into a collector's object worth owning for its own sake, and he defended it fiercely, going to court in Venice over pirated copies of his monogram.

He was, above all, obsessed with himself and with the status of his craft. He drew one of the earliest surviving self-portraits in European art at thirteen, and kept looking, most famously in the frontal, Christ-like panel of 1500. He befriended humanists like Willibald Pirckheimer, corresponded across Europe, and wrote theoretical treatises on measurement and human proportion, insisting the artist was a learned man, not a tradesman.

His later years were shadowed. He entered the service of Emperor Maximilian I, but when the emperor died the pension lapsed, and in 1520 Dürer made an arduous journey to the Netherlands to have it renewed by the young Charles V — a trip he recorded in a candid diary, and on which he seems to have caught the illness that dogged him for the rest of his life. Drawn to the ideas of Martin Luther, he died in Nuremberg in 1528, mourned as the man who had made German art the equal of Italy's.
