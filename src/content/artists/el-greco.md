He was born Domenikos Theotokopoulos on Crete in 1541, when the island belonged to Venice, and he learned his trade the medieval way — painting icons in the post-Byzantine manner of the Cretan school, gold grounds and flat sacred figures whose poses had been fixed for centuries. By twenty-two he was documented as a master with his own workshop. Had he stayed, he would be a footnote in the history of icon painting.

Instead he went to Venice around 1567, into the orbit of Titian and Tintoretto, and learned to build a picture out of colour and light. Then to Rome in 1570, where the miniaturist Giulio Clovio got him lodgings in the Farnese palace. And in Rome he said the thing that follows him around to this day. Michelangelo's *Last Judgment* was under attack for its nudity, and El Greco reportedly offered to paint the whole wall again, better and more decently. He is also said to have remarked that Michelangelo was a good man who did not know how to paint. Rome, which worshipped Michelangelo, did not forgive him.

So in 1577, at thirty-six, he tried Spain — specifically Toledo, the old imperial capital, thick with clergy and money. It should have been a stepping stone to the court. King Philip II gave him two commissions, looked at the *Martyrdom of St Maurice*, disliked it, and never asked again. There would be no royal career. El Greco stayed in Toledo for the remaining thirty-seven years of his life.

What he built there was extraordinary and entirely his own: figures stretched to impossible lengths, colours that belong to no daylight, bodies flickering like candle flame in a space with no reliable floor. Where this came from is still argued. Ophthalmologists once proposed he had astigmatism; another physician blamed hashish; scholars now generally accept the duller and better answer, which is that he painted this way on purpose, fusing Byzantine icon logic with Venetian colour.

He lived well when he could, renting rooms in a nobleman's palace and hiring musicians to play while he ate, and he was ferociously litigious — he took clients to court repeatedly over what his paintings were worth. He never married Jerónima de las Cuevas, the mother of his son Jorge Manuel, who became his assistant. He died in April 1614, seventy-two and in debt.

Then Spain decided he was ridiculous, and for two hundred years he was. It took the nineteenth century to rediscover him, and Cézanne and Picasso to prove he had been three hundred years early.
