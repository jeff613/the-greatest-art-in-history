Francisco Goya climbed higher than almost any painter of his age and then walked, deliberately, into the dark. Born in 1746 to a modest family in rural Aragon, he clawed his way up through the Spanish art world by sheer ambition — apprenticed in Zaragoza, married into the well-connected Bayeu family of painters, and talked his way into the Royal Tapestry Factory, designing cheerful scenes of picnics and parasols for the walls of palaces. By 1786 he was a painter to the king; by 1799 he was First Court Painter, the most powerful artist in Spain, portraitist to a royal family he depicted with unnervingly honest, almost cruel, clarity.

Then, in the winter of 1792–93, a mysterious illness nearly killed him and left him stone deaf for the rest of his life. He was forty-six. Cut off inside a wall of silence, Goya's art turned inward and grew teeth. He began the Caprichos, prints skewering superstition, vanity, and the clergy — one plate famously captioned "The sleep of reason produces monsters." The court painter had become a private visionary who happened to keep his day job.

History then handed him horror. In 1808 Napoleon's troops occupied Spain, and the Peninsular War unleashed six years of atrocity, famine, and guerrilla slaughter. Goya recorded it in the savage etchings of The Disasters of War — made for no patron, shown to no public — and, once the French were gone, in the towering Third of May 1808. He had survived under both the French and the restored Spanish crown, painting whoever held power, but something in him had broken open.

In 1819 he bought a house outside Madrid nicknamed the Quinta del Sordo — the House of the Deaf Man — and, alone and ageing, painted directly onto its plaster walls the fourteen images we now call the Black Paintings: a god devouring his child, a coven of witches, drowning pilgrims, faces twisted by fear. He never titled them, never exhibited them, never explained. In 1824, weary of the absolutist tyranny of Ferdinand VII, he went into exile in France, and died in Bordeaux in 1828, still drawing, at eighty-two.
