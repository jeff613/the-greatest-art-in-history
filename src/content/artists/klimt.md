Gustav Klimt was born in 1862 in Baumgarten, a poor suburb of Vienna, the second of seven children of a gold engraver from Bohemia who never made much money. The trade in his father's hands — beating gold into thin, luminous leaf — would one day become the signature of the most famous paintings his son ever made. At fourteen the boy won a place at Vienna's School of Applied Arts, and by his early twenties he, his brother Ernst, and their friend Franz Matsch were a going concern, decorating theaters and museums across the empire in a polished, conventional style. In 1888 the young Klimt was so acceptable to the establishment that the Emperor awarded him the Golden Order of Merit for his ceiling paintings in the Burgtheater.

Then, in a single year, everything that anchored him fell away. In 1892 his father and his beloved brother Ernst both died, and the safe, salaried Klimt died with them. What emerged over the following decade was stranger, more erotic, and far more dangerous to a conservative city: gold-drenched women, frank sexuality, allegories that refused to flatter. In 1897 he and a group of rebels walked out of Vienna's official art world to found the Secession, and Klimt became its first president, the reluctant figurehead of Austrian modern art.

The rebellion cost him. His Faculty Paintings for the University of Vienna, commissioned to celebrate human knowledge, instead showed suffering, sex, and death; eighty-seven professors signed a petition against them, and the fight reached parliament. Wounded, Klimt eventually bought the pictures back with his own money and swore off public commissions forever. He retreated into private portraits of Vienna's wealthy Jewish families and into the intimate, glittering world of his "Golden Phase" — Judith, The Kiss, the two portraits of Adele Bloch-Bauer.

He was a private, wordless man. He lived with his mother and sisters, took his lifelong companion Emilie Flöge on summer holidays but almost certainly never married, and fathered at least fourteen children by various models. He hated explaining his work; when asked for a self-portrait he replied that he had never painted one and had no interest in himself as a subject, only in other people. In January 1918 a stroke paralyzed his right side, and on 6 February, felled by pneumonia amid the great influenza pandemic, he died at fifty-five, leaving a studio full of unfinished canvases.
