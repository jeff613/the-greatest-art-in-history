Edvard Munch was born in 1863 into a household that death seemed to visit on a schedule. His mother died of tuberculosis when he was five; his adored older sister Sophie died of the same disease at fifteen, while he watched. His father, an army doctor of morbid, punishing piety, met each loss by reading his children ghost stories and warnings of hell, and Munch grew up sickly and terrified, convinced that he had "inherited two of mankind's most frightful enemies — the heritage of consumption and insanity." A younger sister was committed to an asylum. "Illness, madness and death," he later wrote, "were the black angels that stood at my cradle."

He trained briefly as an engineer, then abandoned it for painting and fell in with the Kristiania Bohème, a circle of anarchist writers led by Hans Jæger who preached free love, atheism, and the duty to "write your own life." Their creed — that an artist should paint his own raw experience — set the course of everything Munch made. In 1886 he exhibited The Sick Child, a searing memory of Sophie's deathbed, and the Norwegian public recoiled in disgust. It was the first of many scandals.

A state scholarship took him to Paris, where he absorbed the Impressionists and van Gogh, and then to Berlin, where in 1892 the authorities closed his exhibition after a week. Munch was delighted: the uproar made him famous across Europe. Through the 1890s he poured himself into the Frieze of Life, an interlocking series about love, jealousy, anxiety, and death that includes The Scream, Madonna, and Vampire — pictures that turned private dread into universal symbols.

The strain of hard drinking, bruising love affairs, and a self-inflicted gunshot wound to his hand caught up with him in 1908, when he collapsed and spent eight months in a Copenhagen nerve clinic. He came out steadier and, for the first time, calmer. The last decades at his estate outside Oslo were productive and increasingly solitary. He died in 1944, aged eighty, and left his enormous hoard of paintings, prints, and drawings to the city — the man who had painted his fear finally outliving it.
