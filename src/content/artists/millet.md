Jean-François Millet was the real thing. Born in 1814 in Gruchy, a granite hamlet above the sea on the Normandy coast, he was the eldest son of a farming family and spent his youth plowing, mowing, and spreading manure — the labor he would later paint. His people were pious and serious; his grandmother, who named him for Saint Francis, told him to paint for eternity. The village noticed the boy could draw, and at eighteen he was sent to Cherbourg to train, then on to Paris in 1837 with a small stipend and an accent thick enough to cut.

Paris was a humiliation. In the polished studio of Paul Delaroche the big Norman was mocked as "the wild man of the woods," and he soon went his own way, scraping a living from portraits and pretty pastel nudes he privately despised. The 1840s nearly broke him: rejected at the Salon, widowed at thirty when his first wife died of tuberculosis, chronically poor. In 1849, with cholera raging in Paris and revolution barely a year cold, he loaded his family into a cart and moved to Barbizon, a farming village on the edge of the Fontainebleau forest. He stayed for the rest of his life.

There Millet found his subject: the peasant, painted not as comic rustic or picturesque prop but as a monumental, almost sacred figure. The Sower strode across the Salon of 1850 like a prophet; The Gleaners gave three stooping women the gravity of antique sculpture; The Angelus caught two laborers praying in a darkening field. Conservative critics accused him of socialism — of smuggling 1848 onto canvas — and Millet, a fatalist far more biblical than political, wearily denied it. He had ten years of poverty ahead of him regardless, once trading paintings for shoes and rent.

Fame arrived late but completely. After a triumphant showing at the 1867 Exposition Universelle he was decorated, collected, and finally solvent, and by the 1880s the dead Millet was among the most beloved painters on earth, The Angelus the most reproduced image in France. His truest heir never met him: Vincent van Gogh discovered Millet in reproductions, called him "father Millet," and copied him over and over — twenty-one painted "translations" in the asylum at Saint-Rémy alone. The farmer's son who painted work as prayer became, for Van Gogh and generations after, something very close to scripture.
