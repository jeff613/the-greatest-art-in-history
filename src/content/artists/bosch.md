Start with what is actually known, because it fits on a postcard. He was born around 1450 in 's-Hertogenbosch, a walled market town in Brabant, into a family of painters called van Aken — his grandfather painted, four of his uncles painted, his father advised the town's grandest confraternity on artistic matters. He first surfaces in the municipal record in 1474. He married a woman with money. He joined the Brotherhood of Our Lady in the mid-1480s and stayed a member for thirty years. He died in 1516, and the Brotherhood paid for his funeral mass. That is close to the whole documentary life of the strangest painter in Europe. No letters, no notebooks, no reported conversation, not one sentence explaining a single thing he painted.

Everything else has to be inferred, and the inferences have been wild. Because the pictures are full of demons, sects and hallucinations, people have proposed that he was a heretic, a member of an underground group of free spirits, an alchemist, or a man eating bad rye bread. There is no evidence for any of it. What the record shows is a prosperous, pious, well-connected burgher living on the market square of a town he seems never to have left, in a house he acquired by marriage, painting for patrons rich enough to reach him from Brussels and beyond.

Some things did happen to him. When he was about thirteen a fire burned down much of his town in a single night, and the childhood memory of a city on fire recurs on the horizon of picture after picture. He grew up under the shadow of the enormous church of Saint John, in a world of processions, mystery plays and pulpit warnings about what waits for the greedy. The Brotherhood he joined ran a chapel, a choir and a great deal of the town's ceremonial life, and put him in regular company with its wealthiest men.

He signed his panels not with his family name but with the name of the town: Jheronimus Bosch. It worked. Within a generation of his death, Antwerp dealers were selling Bosch-like devils by the crate, imitators were signing his name, and Philip II of Spain was buying the real thing for the Escorial. Roughly twenty-five paintings are still accepted as his own hand, and even that number keeps moving as technical research reassigns panels to his workshop and back.

The face on this page is not a life portrait. No certain likeness of Bosch exists. This drawing — an old man in a cap, mouth set, eyes hooded — was made in red chalk and charcoal around 1550, more than thirty years after he died, for an album of copied portraits now in Arras, and is generally thought to record a lost original. It is the closest thing we have.
