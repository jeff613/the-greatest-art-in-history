Édouard Manet was born in 1832 into exactly the kind of respectable Parisian family that his paintings would later scandalize. His father was a senior official in the Ministry of Justice, and the household expected the boy to follow into law or the navy. He failed the naval entrance exam twice, sailed as far as Rio de Janeiro on a training ship, and came home no more suited to a conventional career than when he left. Only then did his family relent and let him paint, and in 1850 he entered the studio of the fashionable academic master Thomas Couture, where he spent six restless years half-learning, half-arguing.

What set Manet apart was never bohemianism. He was a witty, elegant, faultlessly dressed man about town, a fixture of the boulevards and cafés, and above all he craved the approval of the official art establishment — the medals, the Salon, the ribbon of the Légion d'honneur. The paradox of his life is that this deeply conventional man could not stop making pictures that horrified the very people he wanted to please. When he took the old themes of the museums — the reclining nude, the picnic of gods and nymphs — and repainted them with real, contemporary Parisians, the public read insolence and even obscenity where he had intended homage.

The uproar over Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe in 1863 and, far worse, Olympia in 1865 made him notorious almost overnight. Younger painters — Monet, Renoir, Degas, and his friend and sister-in-law Berthe Morisot — gathered around him as the reluctant leader of a new way of seeing. Yet Manet never once exhibited with the Impressionists, refusing their breakaway shows because he still believed the Salon was the only arena that mattered.

Recognition came at last, and cruelly late. In 1881 he received a Salon medal and the Légion d'honneur; by then his health was failing badly, his legs weakening from a disease that left him in constant pain. He painted A Bar at the Folies-Bergère, propped up and exhausted, in 1882. The following spring his gangrenous left leg was amputated, and he died days later, in April 1883, aged only fifty-one — honored at the finish by the establishment he had spent a lifetime unsettling.
