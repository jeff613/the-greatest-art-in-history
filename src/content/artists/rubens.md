Peter Paul Rubens was born in 1577 in Siegen, a German town where his family had fled after his father, a Protestant-leaning lawyer, disgraced himself in a scandalous affair with the wife of a prince. It was an unpromising start for a man who would become the most celebrated painter in Europe. After his father's death the family crept back to Catholic Antwerp, where the boy served briefly as a page in a noble household — learning the manners, languages, and courtly ease that would later make him as useful to kings as any ambassador.

He trained under local masters and registered as an independent painter in 1598, but his real education came in Italy. For eight years he studied Titian, Michelangelo, and the ancients, worked for the Duke of Mantua, and absorbed everything the south could teach him about color, muscle, and grandeur. He came home in 1608 a fully formed genius and took Antwerp by storm, opening a studio that ran like a factory. Assistants and specialists — including the young Anthony van Dyck — turned his rapid oil sketches into vast canvases, and commissions poured in from every church and court on the continent.

But Rubens was never only a painter. Fluent in six languages, discreet, and endlessly charming, he was trusted with secret diplomacy by the Spanish crown that governed his homeland. In the late 1620s he shuttled between Madrid and London on a mission to make peace between Spain and England, painting portraits by day and negotiating by night. Both kings rewarded him with knighthoods — Philip IV of Spain and Charles I of England — an almost unheard-of honor for a man who worked with his hands.

His private life ran alongside the public one. His first marriage, to Isabella Brant, was happy until she died of plague in 1626. Four years later, at fifty-three, he married the sixteen-year-old Helena Fourment, whose ample beauty fills his late paintings. In his final years he withdrew to a country estate, painted landscapes and his young family for his own pleasure, and suffered agonies of gout that eventually stilled his hand. He died in 1640, rich, ennobled, and mourned across a continent that had never seen a painter live quite so grandly.
