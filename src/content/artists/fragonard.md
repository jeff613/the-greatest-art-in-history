Jean-Honoré Fragonard was born in Grasse, the Provençal town of perfume, in 1732, the son of a glover. The family moved to Paris, and the boy's talent was obvious enough that he was placed for a short while with Jean-Baptiste-Siméon Chardin, the great painter of quiet still lifes, before passing into the studio of François Boucher, the reigning master of Rococo charm. From Boucher he absorbed a whole world of tumbling cupids, rosy flesh, and silken pleasure. In 1752, remarkably, he won the coveted Prix de Rome without even being a pupil of the Royal Academy's own school, and a few years later sailed for Italy.

The five years in Rome should have made him a history painter — the grandest and most respectable calling an artist could have. When he came home and was received into the Academy in 1765, it was with a vast, serious mythological canvas, and everyone expected a brilliant official career. Instead Fragonard walked away from it. He had discovered that he preferred the private world of wealthy pleasure-seekers to the public world of Salons and royal commissions. He would paint swings and stolen kisses, garden trysts and bedroom games, for a small circle of aristocrats and financiers who paid well and asked no questions.

For two decades he was the most delicious painter in France, turning out shimmering, fast, feather-light pictures of a society devoted to amusement. He married Marie-Anne Gérard in 1769, and his household — later joined by his sister-in-law Marguerite — became a little workshop of talent. But he had tied his fortune entirely to the taste of a doomed class. Even before the catastrophe, the wind was shifting: the cool, moralizing severity of Neoclassicism was rising, and Fragonard's whipped-cream sensuality began to look like a relic.

Then came 1789. The Revolution swept away the patrons, the parties, and the entire way of life his art had celebrated. His pictures were suddenly worse than unfashionable — they were the emblem of everything the new republic despised. He was saved from destitution by an unlikely rescuer: Jacques-Louis David, the stern painter of the Revolution, who remembered the older man's kindness and had him appointed to help build the new national museum in the Louvre. Fragonard spent his last years as an administrator among the pictures, a ghost from a vanished age. He lost the post under Napoleon, and when he died in 1806 almost no one noticed. It took nearly a century for the world to remember how good he had been.
