Michelangelo Buonarroti was born in 1475 in the Tuscan village of Caprese and grew up in Florence, the son of a minor official who beat him for wanting to be an artist rather than something respectable. The boy won anyway. By thirteen he was apprenticed to the painter Domenico Ghirlandaio; by fifteen he had been drawn into the household of Lorenzo de' Medici, Florence's uncrowned ruler, where he lived among ancient statues and learned to carve stone as if it were breathing flesh. He always insisted that sculpture, not painting, was his true art — that a figure already lived inside a block of marble and his only job was to free it.

He proved it young. The Pietà, finished when he was twenty-four, is so tender and so technically impossible that Romans refused to believe a Florentine unknown had made it; he sneaked back at night to carve his name across the Virgin's sash, the only signature of his life. Then came the David, seventeen feet of coiled marble nerve, and Florence had a new hero. He was famous, difficult, and proud, with a broken nose from a boyhood brawl and a temperament that made enemies as fast as it made masterpieces.

His fame is the reason his life became a tug-of-war between his chisel and other men's ambitions. Pope Julius II dragged him to Rome to build a tomb, then abandoned it to send him up a scaffold to paint the Sistine ceiling — a job Michelangelo believed a rival had engineered to see him fail. He did not fail. He spent four years craning backward under wet plaster, paint dripping into his eyes, cursing in a sonnet that he had grown a goiter and could no longer stand straight. When the ceiling was unveiled in 1512 it silenced everyone.

He would keep working for another half-century, outliving popes and rivals, returning to the same chapel decades later to cover its altar wall with The Last Judgment. In old age he turned to architecture and poured himself into St. Peter's, designing the dome that still crowns Rome's skyline. He died in 1564, nearly eighty-nine, unmarried, devout, and endlessly dissatisfied, having convinced the world of exactly the thing he denied — that he was the greatest painter of his age as well as its greatest sculptor.
