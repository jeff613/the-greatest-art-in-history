Wassily Kandinsky did not take up the brush in earnest until he was thirty, and for most of his early life nothing suggested he would become the man who taught European painting to let go of the visible world. He was born in Moscow in 1866 into a comfortable tea-merchant family, raised partly in Odessa after his parents separated, and groomed for a respectable career. He studied law and economics at the University of Moscow, did well enough to be kept on as a lecturer, and by his early thirties was offered a professorship of Roman law at the University of Dorpat. He turned it down.

Two experiences in the mid-1890s had unsettled him past the point of return. At a French exhibition in Moscow he stood before one of Claude Monet's stacks of wheat — the same series this museum hangs in its Impressionist rooms — and could not tell what it showed. "That it was a haystack the catalogue informed me," he wrote; "I could not recognize it." The picture had seized him without his being able to name its subject. Around the same time he heard Wagner's *Lohengrin* at the Bolshoi and felt, he said, as though he could see the music moving in front of him as color. The heresy he drew from these moments was simple: that color and form might carry a painting's whole power, with no object at all.

He gave up the law, moved to Munich in 1896, and started over as a beginner, learning to draw from scratch at a private academy. For years he painted Russian fairy-tale scenes and glowing Bavarian landscapes before edging toward pure abstraction around 1910. In 1911 he and Franz Marc founded Der Blaue Reiter — the Blue Rider — and he published *Concerning the Spiritual in Art*, arguing that painting should work on the soul the way music does. His breakthrough canvases, the spontaneous *Improvisations* and the carefully built *Compositions*, poured out in the years before the First World War scattered everything.

The war sent him back to Russia; the uneasy peace brought him to the Bauhaus in 1922, where his art turned crisp, cool, and geometric. When the Nazis closed the school in 1933 and branded his work "degenerate," he fled to France, and there, in a suburb of Paris, he died in 1944. He had travelled almost the entire arc of modern art, from realism to the first deliberate abstractions — and he always insisted the journey began with a haystack he could not read.
