Almost everything anyone says about Cimabue comes from two sources, and one of them is a poem. The documents give us a handful of dates: a man called Cenni di Pepo, nicknamed Cimabue, present in Rome in 1272; a contract in Pisa in 1301 for a mosaic of Saint John the Evangelist in the cathedral; and his death, at Pisa, the following year. That mosaic is the only surviving work anyone can prove he made. Everything else — the crucifixes, the great enthroned Virgins, the wrecked frescoes at Assisi — is attributed to him on grounds of style and old tradition.

The poem is Dante's. In the *Purgatorio*, written within a generation of Cimabue's death, Dante uses him as an example of how fast worldly fame rots: Cimabue believed he held the field in painting, and now the cry is all for Giotto, and the older man's name has gone dim. It is a brutal thing to be remembered for, and it fixed Cimabue's role permanently — the man who was the best until someone better arrived.

Two and a half centuries later Vasari expanded the demotion into a story. Cimabue, riding through the countryside, came upon a shepherd boy scratching a picture of his sheep onto a flat rock, recognised genius, and carried him off to Florence to be trained. The boy was Giotto. It is a wonderful anecdote and most scholars now discount it, pointing to earlier sources that tell it differently. Even Vasari's Cimabue is really a device for introducing Giotto.

What survives argues for something less tidy and more interesting. Cimabue was working at the end of a tradition that had run for centuries: the Byzantine manner, with its gold grounds, its fixed poses, its faces built from formula rather than observation. He did not abandon it. He pushed at it — enlarging the figures, giving the Virgin's throne a plausible three-dimensional structure, letting a body have weight under its drapery — until the old system began to strain. Giotto walked through the gap he made.

He also painted very large, at Assisi and in Florence and Pisa, and worked in mosaic, and evidently ran a substantial operation. He died in Pisa in 1302, before Giotto's greatest work existed and long before anyone decided the Renaissance had begun.

No portrait of Cimabue drawn from life exists. The likeness used here is the woodcut Vasari printed at the head of the life in 1568 — an invention made more than two and a half centuries after the man died, captioned with a first name, Giovanni, that Vasari appears to have given him and that the documents do not support. Like the collection's Giotto and Duccio, it is a stand-in, not a record of a face.
