Pablo Picasso was born in Málaga in 1881 to a father who taught drawing and painted pigeons for a living, and the boy's gift was so absurd that the family legend has Don José handing his own brushes to his teenage son and giving up painting forever. By fifteen Picasso could turn out flawless academic canvases in the manner of the old masters; the problem, for the next seventy-five years, was what to do with a skill he had already exhausted before he was grown. He would later say it took him four years to paint like Raphael but a lifetime to paint like a child.

The family moved to Barcelona, where he fell in with the bohemians of the Els Quatre Gats café, and in 1900 he made the pilgrimage every ambitious young artist made — to Paris. The city was cheap, cold, and merciless. When his closest friend Carles Casagemas shot himself over a woman in 1901, Picasso sank into the melancholy Blue Period, painting beggars, blind men, and the destitute in chilly monochrome. "It was thinking of Casagemas," he admitted, "that got me started painting in blue." Warmth crept back with the Rose Period and its wistful acrobats, and then, in a Montmartre studio in 1907, he blew the whole tradition apart with Les Demoiselles d'Avignon and, alongside Georges Braque, built Cubism plank by plank.

What followed was the longest, most restless career in modern art. Picasso never settled into a style; he devoured them — classicism, Surrealism, sculpture, ceramics, printmaking — reinventing himself so often that historians carve his life into "periods" the way geologists read rock. In 1937 the German bombing of a Basque town gave him Guernica, the century's great howl against war. He kept working almost until the day he died in 1973, at ninety-one, rich and world-famous, a communist who lived like a king and treated the people around him with genius's careless cruelty.

This museum keeps only his early rooms. The collection stops in 1929, where living copyright begins — the later giants, Guernica among them, still belong to his estate and must wait for the public domain before they can hang on these walls.
