He was born Tiziano Vecellio in Pieve di Cadore, a mountain town in the Dolomites north of Venice, sometime around 1488 — the exact year was already uncertain in his own lifetime, and he was happy to let it stay that way, later padding his age to suit a patron. Sent as a boy to Venice, he trained in the workshops of the brothers Gentile and Giovanni Bellini and fell under the spell of the short-lived, mysterious Giorgione, beside whom he painted frescoes on the walls of the German merchants' warehouse. When Giorgione died young of plague in 1510 and old Giovanni Bellini died in 1516, the field was suddenly, spectacularly clear. Titian stepped into it and did not leave for sixty years.

His breakthrough was public and enormous: the flame-red, upward-surging *Assumption of the Virgin*, installed over the high altar of the Frari church in 1518, a picture so full of motion and color that it seemed to lift the whole building. From then on he was the painter of Venice, and soon of far more than Venice. The Este dukes of Ferrara, the della Rovere of Urbino, the Farnese pope in Rome — all wanted him. Above all, the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V wanted him, and in 1533 made him a Count Palatine and Knight of the Golden Spur, a nobleman's rank for a man who ground pigment for a living. Legend says the emperor once bent to retrieve a brush Titian had dropped, telling his scandalized court that even a king could serve so great an artist.

He became the most sought-after portraitist in Europe and, for Charles's son Philip II of Spain, the painter of sensuous mythologies he called *poesie* — poems in paint. His style loosened as he aged, the tight finish of his youth dissolving into smoky, broken brushwork laid on, a contemporary marveled, "with fingers rather than a brush." He grew rich, kept a grand house on the northern edge of Venice, and shrewdly guarded his fame. He died in 1576, in his late eighties, as plague swept the city — still, by every account, painting to the end.
