No confirmed portrait of Johannes Vermeer exists, which is fitting for a painter who left almost no trace of himself. He was born in Delft in 1632, the son of a man who wove silk, ran an inn, and dealt in pictures on the side — so the boy grew up around paintings as merchandise. In 1653 he married Catharina Bolnes, a Catholic woman from a wealthier family, converted to her faith, and registered as a master in the local painters' guild. The couple moved in with Catharina's formidable mother, Maria Thins, whose money kept them afloat, and there Vermeer settled into a life of near-total obscurity outside his home town.

He worked with almost unimaginable slowness. Where a busy Dutch master might turn out dozens of canvases a year, Vermeer produced perhaps two or three, grinding costly pigments — real ultramarine ground from lapis lazuli — and building each picture up in patient, luminous layers. Only about thirty-five paintings by him survive, and it is likely he made only a handful more. He was not, in the ordinary sense, a professional churning out product; he was a perfectionist who painted quiet rooms lit by a single window, women reading letters, pouring milk, weighing pearls, caught in a stillness no one else could touch.

To feed a household that eventually held eleven living children, he leaned on a single loyal patron, the Delft collector Pieter van Ruijven, and dealt in other artists' work to make ends meet. His fellow painters respected him enough to elect him a head of their guild. But he was always one bad year from disaster, and in 1672 that year came. The French army invaded the Dutch Republic, the economy seized, the art trade evaporated, and Vermeer — a dealer as well as a painter — was ruined.

He died suddenly in 1675, only forty-three, worn down by stress and debt. His widow, drowning in creditors, told the authorities that the collapse of the country had killed him. She was forced to hand over paintings to settle bakery bills. Then the world simply forgot him: for two centuries his pictures hung under other men's names, until a French critic rediscovered him in the 1860s and the silent painter of Delft became one of the most beloved artists who ever lived.
