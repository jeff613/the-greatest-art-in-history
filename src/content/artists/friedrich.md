He learned early what the world could take away. He was born in Greifswald in September 1774, one of ten children of a candle-maker and soap-boiler. His mother died when he was seven. A sister died the year after that. In 1787 his younger brother Johann Christoffer drowned in the ice; the account passed down since the nineteenth century, and repeated in most books about him, is that the boy went in trying to pull Caspar David out after he broke through first, though the documentation for that version is thin and it is better told as a story people have always told than as a fact. Another sister died of typhus in 1791. He was seventeen and had buried four of them.

He studied at the Academy in Copenhagen from 1794, then moved to Dresden in 1798 and never left. He did not paint outdoors at the easel. He walked the Baltic coast and the sandstone country above the Elbe, drew what he found, and then assembled pictures in a studio so bare that visitors commented on it — his friend Georg Friedrich Kersting painted the room twice and could find almost nothing in it to include. Close the bodily eye, Friedrich told a student, so that you may first see your picture with the spiritual eye.

The public arrived in 1808, in the form of an argument: a critic attacked him for putting a mountain crucifix in an altarpiece, and Friedrich wrote the only detailed explanation of his own work he ever produced. Two years later the Prussian king bought two paintings straight out of the Berlin exhibition and the Academy elected him. In 1818, at forty-three, he married Caroline Bommer, twenty-five, and had three children with her; his palette warmed noticeably, and quiet women in long dresses began appearing in his landscapes.

Then the tide went out. Taste moved toward brighter, plainer naturalism; his silent allegories began to look like a fashion that had passed. Illness came, sales stopped, and in June 1835 a stroke took most of what was left. He managed small sepias afterwards. Old friends kept him going — the Russian poet Vasily Zhukovsky, an admirer with a place at court, helped find money for him. He died in Dresden in May 1840, and his widow died in poverty seven years later.

He was rescued in 1906, when the great Berlin centenary exhibition of German art hung thirty-two of his paintings and a generation raised on Impressionism recognised something startlingly modern in them. The recovery was then badly damaged from an unexpected direction: in the 1930s the Nazis annexed his lonely German landscapes for their own myth of blood and soil, and the stain kept serious attention away from him for decades after the war. It took scholars like Werner Hofmann, in the 1970s, to prise him loose again.
