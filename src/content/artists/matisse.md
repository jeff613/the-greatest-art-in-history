Henri Matisse was born in 1869 in Le Cateau-Cambrésis, a weaving town in the grey industrial north of France, the son of a prosperous grain merchant who expected the boy to make something respectable of himself. He did as he was told. He went to Paris, studied law, and came home to a clerk's desk in a Saint-Quentin office, copying out other men's documents. He was twenty and destined, it seemed, for a small, orderly life.

Then, in 1889, an attack of appendicitis put him in bed for weeks, and his mother brought him a box of paints to pass the time. The effect was volcanic. "From the moment I held the box of colours in my hands," he later said, "I knew this was my life." Painting was, he found, "a kind of paradise" — and he walked out of the law forever, over his father's furious objections, to become an art student.

He was a late, deliberate starter who worked his way through the Paris studios, copying old masters in the Louvre and studying under the generous Symbolist Gustave Moreau. For years he was poor, married to the steadfast Amélie Parayre, who ran a hat shop to help keep them afloat. Then, in the autumn of 1905, he and a band of young painters hung their canvases at the Salon d'Automne in colours so raw and arbitrary that a critic dubbed them *fauves* — wild beasts. Fauvism made Matisse notorious overnight, and it made him, for a moment, the most advanced painter in Paris.

Notoriety brought patrons. The American Stein family and, above all, the Russian textile magnate Sergei Shchukin bought his boldest work, freeing him to paint enormous decorations like *Dance*. His lifelong duel with Pablo Picasso — "as different," Matisse said, "as the North Pole is from the South Pole" — drove both men to greater daring. In 1917 he moved south to Nice, chasing the Mediterranean light, and spent the 1920s painting languorous "odalisques" in sun-flooded rooms.

Old age did not slow him. Bedridden after cancer surgery, he reinvented himself yet again, "drawing with scissors" to make the radiant paper cut-outs of his final years and designing a whole chapel at Vence. He died in Nice in 1954, at eighty-four, having spent sixty-five years proving that the clerk who once copied documents had been right to trust a box of paints.
