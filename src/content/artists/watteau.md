He was baptised in Valenciennes in October 1684, in a town that had belonged to the Spanish Netherlands until Louis XIV's armies took it six years earlier. His father was a roofer, second generation, and by local report a man handy with his fists. The boy drew the charlatans who sold cure-alls in the market square. In 1702 he went to Paris with nothing arranged, and the first years there were the kind that break people: scene-painting, poor health, and finally a workshop on the Pont Notre-Dame where he turned out copies of Flemish genre pictures by the dozen, paid by the piece. He learned to paint fast because he was paid to paint fast.

What rescued him was his drawings. Claude Gillot, a painter of theatre subjects, saw them and took him on around 1705, and in Gillot's studio Watteau met the thing that would occupy him for the rest of his life: the commedia dell'arte, the Italian players with their masks and stock lovers and their sad white clown. The two men quarrelled — nobody recorded over what — and Watteau moved to the decorator Claude Audran III, who happened to be keeper of the Luxembourg Palace. There, more or less for free, he could stand in front of Rubens's enormous Marie de' Medici cycle whenever he liked.

He came second in the Prix de Rome in 1709 and never got to Italy, which he seems not to have minded much once the financier Pierre Crozat opened his collection to him — hundreds of paintings and thousands of drawings, Titian and Veronese and more Rubens. Watteau lodged with Crozat for a while. He lodged with a great many people. He had no household, no wife, no fixed address for long, and friends complained that he was touchy, restless, and blankly indifferent to his own future, as though he already knew how much of it there was.

The Academy approved him in 1712 and then waited. It took him five years, and several official reprimands, to hand over the reception piece he owed, and when it finally arrived the clerk could not classify it: he wrote down the title, struck it out, and put "a fête galante" instead. Watteau had a genre named after him because a bureaucrat needed a box to tick.

His buyers were never courtiers. They were bankers, dealers, collectors — Crozat, Jean de Jullienne, the dealer Gersaint — the new Paris money of the Regency. In late 1719 he sailed to London to put himself in the hands of Dr Richard Mead, the most fashionable physician in England, and came home in the summer no better. He died at Nogent-sur-Marne on 18 July 1721, thirty-six years old. The abbé who sat with him said he was mute at the end, holding a brush, painting pictures in the air.
