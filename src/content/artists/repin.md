He was born in August 1844 in Chuguev, a garrison town in the Kharkov Governorate — today Chuhuiv, in Ukraine — into the caste of military settlers, peasant-soldiers bound to the army for life. His father had ridden three campaigns with the Chuguev uhlans and traded horses between them. Nothing about that household suggested a boy would ever get out of it.

What got him out was icons. At sixteen he was taken into a local icon-painting workshop, and from there into a travelling artel that moved between villages painting church walls for twenty-five roubles a month. Before he was anything else he was a jobbing religious craftsman, and he never quite lost the habit of painting a human face as though something eternal depended on getting it right.

In 1863 he arrived in St Petersburg with almost nothing and the Imperial Academy of Arts turned him down. He took evening classes instead under Ivan Kramskoi and was admitted the following January. The timing was remarkable: weeks before Repin reached the city, Kramskoi had walked fourteen of the Academy's best students out of the building rather than paint the mythological subject they had been assigned. Repin's teacher was the man who had led the revolt. In 1878 he joined the movement that grew out of it — the Peredvizhniki, the Wanderers, who refused to let painting stay in the capital and dragged their exhibitions out to the provinces.

By then he was already the most talked-about painter in Russia. *Barge Haulers on the Volga* had done that when he was twenty-nine. The 1880s belong to him almost entirely: the murdered tsarevich, the Cossacks howling with laughter, the exile walking back into his own living room. From 1894 he taught at the Academy that had once rejected him, and Serov, Kustodiev, Grabar and Malyavin came through his studio.

In 1899 he bought pine woods at Kuokkala, up the Gulf of Finland from St Petersburg, and built a house he named Penaty after the Roman gods of the household. Then in 1917 Finland broke from Russia, the border closed a short walk from his gate, and Repin's house was suddenly abroad. Soviet emissaries came in 1925 and 1926 — one of them a former student of his, another carrying a letter from the People's Commissar Kliment Voroshilov — and he did not go. In old age his right hand failed him; he hung the palette from his neck and taught himself to paint left-handed. He died at Penaty on 29 September 1930 and was buried in the garden.

He called himself a Russian born in Little Russia. Since 2023 the Metropolitan Museum and Finland's Ateneum have relabelled him a Ukrainian artist, citing church records that place his father's and grandfather's birthplaces on what is now Ukrainian soil. Both countries claim him, and the argument is not settled.
