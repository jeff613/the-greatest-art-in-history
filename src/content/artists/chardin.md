He never left Paris, never saw Italy, never learned the mythology every serious painter of his century was expected to have at his fingertips. His father made billiard tables and rose to head his guild; the son grew up in a workshop, among tools and finished surfaces, with no literary education to speak of. That closed off gods, emperors and saints before he had begun. What was left was the room in front of him.

In 1728, aged twenty-eight, he presented the Académie royale with a canvas of a gutted skate hanging over a kitchen table. They took him the same day — and entered him on the books in the talent of animals and fruit, the lowest category the institution had. There was no mechanism for moving up. For the next half-century France's most admired painter of everyday life carried an official label that meant, roughly, decorator.

He responded by ignoring the ladder entirely. Around 1732, reportedly needled by a portraitist friend who joked that he would work cheap if a portrait were as easy to paint as a sausage, he began putting people into the pictures: a boy blowing a bubble, a kitchen maid stopped mid-thought over a turnip, a mother waiting for a small child to finish saying grace. He worked slowly, a handful of canvases a year, and repeated the ones that sold. Engravers reproduced everything, and it was the prints, more than the paintings, that made him famous — he is generally said to have earned more from them than from the originals.

The Académie that had capped him also came to depend on him. He was its treasurer from 1755 and, from 1761, its *tapissier* — the man who hung the Salon. For over a decade every ambitious painter in France had to live with where Chardin decided to put their picture on the wall. Diderot, the century's fiercest critic, wrote about him as though he were a category of his own: not paint on a palette but the substance of things.

The private accounting was harsher. His first wife died in 1735, a daughter in 1737. He married again in 1744, comfortably. And his son Pierre-Jean, a painter who had won the Academy's Rome prize in 1754, drowned in Venice — the Louvre's records date it to 1768, other accounts push it later, and nearly all of them treat it as suicide. In his last years Chardin's eyes gave out and oil paint had become hard to work with, so he took up pastel and drew himself: an old man in a knotted white headscarf under a blue eyeshade, round spectacles on his nose, looking back over his shoulder without apology.

He died in 1779 in his apartment at the Louvre, and was thoroughly forgotten within a generation. It took Realist critics and the Goncourt brothers, in the 1860s, to dig him out — after which Manet, Cézanne and most of modern French painting quietly claimed him.
