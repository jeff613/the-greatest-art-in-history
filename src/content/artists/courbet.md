Gustave Courbet was born in 1819 in Ornans, a small town in the mountainous Franche-Comté near the Swiss border, into a family of prosperous farmers and vineyard owners. He never lost the swagger of that rooted, provincial world — the confidence of a man who owned land and answered to nobody. Sent to Paris in 1839 to study law, he abandoned it almost at once for painting, and taught himself largely by copying the Spanish, Dutch, and Venetian masters in the Louvre rather than submitting to an academy. From the start he was loud, physically imposing, and monumentally sure of himself.

By the late 1840s he had found his subject: the real, unidealized life around him. He painted stonebreakers by the roadside, peasants at a village funeral, provincial burghers — and he painted them on the vast canvases the academy reserved for gods, saints, and history. This was a provocation, and it was meant to be. When the word "Realism" was flung at him as an insult, he seized it and made it a banner. He is supposed to have answered a request to paint angels for a church with the line that became his motto: he had never seen an angel, and when someone showed him one, he would paint it.

Courbet was also a political animal. A friend of the anarchist Proudhon and a scourge of the Second Empire, he refused the Legion of Honour and threw himself into the Paris Commune of 1871. That commitment destroyed him. As head of the Commune's arts commission he was blamed for the toppling of the Vendôme Column, Napoleon's monument to military glory. He was jailed, then, in 1873, handed a staggering bill — 323,000 francs — to rebuild it in yearly installments. Rather than be stripped of everything, he fled across the border to Switzerland.

There he spent his last years, drinking heavily, painting saleable landscapes to chip at an impossible debt, watching France from the wrong side of the frontier. His health broke down, his body swelling with dropsy and liver disease. He died at La Tour-de-Peilz on the last day of 1877, aged fifty-eight — reportedly the very day before the first payment on the column came due. The proudest man in France died a debtor and an exile, but he had already changed what a painting was allowed to be about.
