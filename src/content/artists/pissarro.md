He was born above a hardware store in Charlotte Amalie, the harbour town of St Thomas, then a Danish colony in the Caribbean. His father, a Sephardic Jew from Bordeaux, had sailed out to settle a dead uncle's business and married the uncle's widow — his own aunt by marriage — a match the island's small synagogue refused to bless for years. The Pissarro children were treated as illegitimate and shut out of the Jewish school, so they were sent instead to the island's primary school for Black children. He began life as an outsider twice over and never entirely stopped being one.

At twelve he was shipped to a boarding school outside Paris. At seventeen he was back behind the counter, drawing the harbour instead of counting stock. In 1852 he simply left, sailing for Venezuela with a Danish painter named Fritz Melbye and spending two years sketching markets and washerwomen in Caracas. His parents gave up arguing. In 1855 he arrived in Paris for good.

What followed was half a century of poverty conducted with unusual calm. He took up with Julie Vellay, his mother's maid and a vine-grower's daughter; his family disapproved; they had eight children anyway. The Franco-Prussian War drove them to London in 1870, where they finally married, and when he returned to his rented house at Louveciennes he found that the soldiers billeted there had used his canvases as duckboards and butcher's aprons. Of roughly 1,500 works left behind, some forty survived.

He rebuilt at Pontoise and then at Éragny-sur-Epte, and became the fixed point of a movement full of prima donnas. He was the only painter to hang in all eight Impressionist exhibitions. Cézanne stood at his shoulder in the fields outside Pontoise and later called him "humble and colossal" — "a father for me," he said, "a man to consult and a little like the good Lord." Gauguin, still a stockbroker, brought him weekend canvases for correction.

He was also a convinced anarchist, a reader of Kropotkin who quietly paid off the debts of Jean Grave's newspapers and, in 1889, drew twenty-eight pen sketches called *Turpitudes sociales* to explain modern capitalism to his nieces in London. At fifty-five he threw over his own style for Seurat's dots and, four years later, threw that over too. In the 1890s a chronic eye infection ended outdoor painting and drove him to hotel windows above the Paris streets, where he made the last and most modern pictures of his life. When the Dreyfus affair split France, Degas and Renoir stopped greeting him in the street. He went on painting until weeks before he died.
