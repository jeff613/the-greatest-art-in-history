No one knows what Duccio di Buoninsegna looked like — no portrait of him survives, and the face used here is the Madonna from his own great altarpiece, standing in for a man who left only his paintings and a long paper trail of trouble. Even his dates are approximate. He was born in Siena around 1255, and he first steps into the light of the record in 1278, being paid to paint the wooden covers of the city's account books — humble municipal work for a painter who would end his life as the most celebrated artist in Tuscany.

What the Sienese archives preserve, alongside his commissions, is a running list of his misbehaviour. From the 1270s onward he is fined again and again: for debts left unpaid, for breaking the night curfew, for refusing to swear loyalty to the city's captain, for dodging military service, even, on one occasion, for something the clerk logged as sorcery. In 1280 the commune hit him with a fine of a hundred lire, a punishing sum, for an offence no longer named. He seems to have been a man who could conjure the divine on a panel yet could not, or would not, keep the ordinary rules of his town.

None of it dimmed his reputation. In 1285 a Florentine confraternity crossed into rival territory to commission from him the enormous Rucellai Madonna, and the contract they drew up is the earliest surviving Italian document of its kind. He absorbed the gold-ground grandeur of the Byzantine tradition and the softness of French Gothic, then warmed it with a tenderness all his own — Virgins who incline their heads toward their children, drapery that falls in gentle, living folds. Where his great contemporary Giotto gave figures weight and gravity, Duccio gave them grace and feeling, and between them the two men founded the two rival schools, Sienese and Florentine, that would shape Italian painting for a century.

His masterpiece, the Maestà, crowned everything. It earned him a fortune — and still he died, around 1319, so far in debt that his own children refused the inheritance. Siena carried his heaven through the streets; it could never quite balance his books.
