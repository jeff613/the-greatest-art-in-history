He was rich, he was reckless, and he was in a hurry, though nobody yet knew why.

Théodore Géricault was born in Rouen in 1791 and raised in Paris on family money — a lawyer father with an interest in the tobacco trade. It meant he never had to take a commission he did not want, which turned out to matter enormously. He trained first with Carle Vernet, a painter of horses and hunts whose son Horace became a lifelong friend, then with the strict classicist Pierre-Narcisse Guérin. His real education happened elsewhere: in the Louvre, copying Rubens and Titian and Velázquez, and in the imperial stables, learning what a horse's leg actually does.

In 1812, at twenty-one, he sent his first painting to the Salon — an officer of Napoleon's Guard on a rearing horse, done in about five weeks — and walked out with a gold medal. Two years later he sent its opposite, a beaten cuirassier backing away from the fighting, and the critics tore it apart. He stopped painting, enlisted, and served a spell in the garrison at Versailles.

Then he went to Italy, as young French painters did, though he also went to get away from a woman. Alexandrine-Modeste Caruel was the young wife of his mother's brother, and she had become his lover. Distance did not fix it. In August 1818 she bore him a son, who was registered without his parents' names and handed to a wet-nurse in Normandy. Neither of them raised him.

That November Géricault shaved his head, so that he would be unfit to be seen in society, and shut himself into a studio opposite a hospital to paint a raft. He was twenty-seven. The Raft of the Medusa took him eight months, indicted the restored monarchy, won a gold medal, sold to nobody, and made him famous.

He carried it to England in 1820 and stayed nearly two years, drawing racehorses, jockeys, and the poor of London. He came home into an illness that never really lifted. In his last years the pictures got smaller and stranger — horses, severed limbs, and ten portraits of asylum patients painted with such straight-faced dignity that they still stop people.

Riding killed him, more or less. A fall onto a pile of stones injured his spine; he kept riding; an abscess formed on his back and burst, and tuberculosis finished what the horses had started. He wasted for months and died in Paris on 26 January 1824, thirty-two years old. The Louvre bought the Raft from his heirs the same year. He lies at Père Lachaise beneath a bronze of himself, reclining, brush in hand, with the raft carved in relief below.
