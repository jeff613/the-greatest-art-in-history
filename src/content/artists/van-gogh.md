Vincent van Gogh failed at everything before he failed at staying alive. The son of a Dutch pastor, he was pushed out of art dealing, gave up on teaching, and flamed out as a missionary among the coal miners of Belgium's Borinage — he took the Gospel so literally, giving away his clothes and sleeping on straw, that the church committee dismissed him for undermining the dignity of the profession. He was twenty-seven, penniless, and estranged from nearly everyone when he made the one decision that mattered: he would draw.

What followed was a single decade — that is all he got — of the most furious self-education in the history of art. He copied drawing manuals in freezing rooms, drew weavers and diggers, and in 1885 produced his first masterpiece, The Potato Eaters, a dark, knuckled painting of peasants who looked like the earth they worked. Then Paris happened to him. Living with his brother Theo, the art dealer who bankrolled and believed in him his whole career, Vincent saw the Impressionists and the Japanese prints flooding the city, and his palette detonated: grays became yellows, browns became cobalt and vermilion.

In 1888 he moved south to Arles, dreaming of founding a "studio of the south" with Paul Gauguin. The dream lasted nine weeks. The two painters argued viciously about art; on 23 December 1888, exhausted and unraveling, Vincent cut off part of his own left ear and delivered it to a woman at the local brothel. Gauguin fled. The neighbors petitioned to have Vincent locked away, and in May 1889 he committed himself voluntarily to the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole at Saint-Rémy — where, between devastating attacks, he painted some 150 canvases, The Starry Night among them.

He shot himself in a wheat field at Auvers-sur-Oise in July 1890 and died two days later with Theo at his bedside. He had sold almost nothing — one documented painting sale, The Red Vineyard, for 400 francs. Theo, broken, died six months after him. It was Theo's widow, Jo van Gogh-Bonger, who spent the next three decades publishing Vincent's letters and lending his canvases to exhibitions, patiently building the fame he never saw. She succeeded beyond all reason: the man who could not sell a painting became the most loved painter on earth.
