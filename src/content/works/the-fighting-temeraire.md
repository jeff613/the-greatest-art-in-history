## The Story

A great pale warship glides across still golden water, her masts bare, her hull glowing almost translucent in the evening light. Ahead, dragging her along, chugs a small, dark, ugly steam tug, belching fire and black smoke from its funnel. Behind them a molten sun sinks into the Thames. It is one of the most quietly heartbreaking pictures in British art: the old being pulled to its death by the new.

The ship is HMS Temeraire, a ninety-eight-gun ship of the line that had covered herself in glory at the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805, coming to the rescue of Nelson's flagship in the thick of the fight. For decades afterward she rotted in reserve, and in 1838 the navy sold her hull for scrap. To reach the breaker's yard at Rotherhithe, the once-mighty vessel was towed up the Thames by steam tugs — stripped of her masts and rigging, a hollow relic. Turner may have seen the tow, or heard of it; either way it lodged in him as a symbol.

He was not interested in documentary accuracy. In life the Temeraire would have been a dismasted hulk, her tall masts long gone; Turner painted them back, ghostly and complete, so the ship could stand tall one last time. He set the sun in the wrong part of the sky for a westward journey, because he wanted the blaze of sunset to fall like a requiem. The tug is small and grimy on purpose; the warship, though doomed, is luminous and noble. Turner was painting the passing of an entire era — the world of wind, sail, and wooden heroism giving way to coal, iron, and steam.

He exhibited it at the Royal Academy in 1839 and treasured it for the rest of his life, calling it his "darling" and refusing every offer to buy it. It has been loved almost from the moment it was shown. In 2005 the British public voted it the greatest painting in any British gallery.

## The World Behind It

Turner painted the Temeraire on the cusp of the Victorian age, as Britain reinvented itself through industry. The steam engine was conquering everything — the railways were spreading across the country, steamships were crossing oceans, and factory chimneys were rising over the old green landscape. To many it was thrilling progress; to some it was the death of an older, slower, more heroic Britain.

The Temeraire herself belonged to that vanishing world. She was a survivor of the Napoleonic Wars, the long naval struggle in which Britain's wooden walls had made her the mistress of the seas. By 1838 that struggle was a generation gone, the great sailing warships obsolete, and a proud nation was selling its Trafalgar veterans for firewood. Turner, then in his sixties, felt the change keenly. His painting is not a protest against steam — he was fascinated by machines — so much as an elegy, a lingering, golden goodbye to the age that had made him.

## The Artist at This Moment

Turner was sixty-four in 1839, at the height of his fame and powers but increasingly out of step with his critics, who found his later work wild and unfinished. The Fighting Temeraire is, unusually for late Turner, both radical and instantly loved — a picture whose meaning everyone could feel. It sits at a turning point in his career, just as his handling of paint was loosening toward the near-abstract storms of light of his final decade.

The subject also touched something personal. Turner was a patriot who had painted Trafalgar and the navy before, and he was a man acutely aware of time, endings, and his own mortality. An ageing artist, watching an ageing ship hauled off to be destroyed, could hardly miss the parallel. He kept the canvas by him and would not part with it, as though unwilling to let the old ship go a second time. It passed, with his vast bequest, to the nation he had served.

## Interesting Facts

- In 2005 a public poll run by the BBC and the National Gallery voted The Fighting Temeraire the greatest painting in any British collection.
- Turner altered the facts for effect: the real ship was stripped of her masts before being towed, but he painted her fully rigged and ghostly-tall.
- The sunset is on the "wrong" side of the sky for a ship travelling upriver to the west — Turner wanted the dying light as pure symbolism.
- He called the painting his "darling" and refused for the rest of his life to sell it, leaving it instead to the nation.
- Since 2020 the painting has appeared on the Bank of England's £20 note, alongside a portrait of Turner himself.
