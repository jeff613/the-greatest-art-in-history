## The Story

The sofa is the joke and the key. A woman lies naked on a buttoned red velvet divan, the kind of thing that belongs in a Paris apartment, and around her the jungle has closed in: lotus flowers, orange trees, a serpent, elephant ears, two lions staring straight out of the leaves, and a dark figure in a striped skirt playing a pipe. A full moon sits in the top corner. The canvas is enormous — nearly three metres across, the largest thing he ever made.

Rousseau finished it early in 1910 and hung it at the Salon des Indépendants that March. He seems to have known people would find it ridiculous, and he took the precaution of writing a poem to go with it, a short verse explaining that Yadwigha, having fallen gently asleep in a beautiful dream, was hearing the sounds of a pipe played by a kindly charmer. Pressed further by the critic André Dupont, he offered a plainer reading: the woman is on her couch in Paris, and she is dreaming that she is listening to a flute player in the jungle. The sofa is not in the forest. The forest is in her sleep.

Yadwigha, he said, was a Polish woman he had loved as a young man. There is no independent record of her, and the story may be as much a construction as his Mexican campaigns.

What happened at the Indépendants was, for once, not laughter. Guillaume Apollinaire, then the most listened-to voice in the Paris avant-garde, wrote that the picture radiated beauty and that nobody, this year, was going to laugh. Two years earlier Picasso had thrown a banquet in Rousseau's honour at the Bateau-Lavoir, a night that has been retold so many times that the retellings no longer agree: some who were there described a sincere tribute, others insisted the whole evening was an elaborate joke at his expense, and the argument continued for decades. Whatever it was, the young painters were now looking at him hard, and *The Dream* is what they were looking at.

He was dead by September. Nelson Rockefeller gave the painting to the Museum of Modern Art in 1954, and Rousseau's last jungle became the picture the twentieth century used to prove he had been serious all along.

## The World Behind It

Paris opened 1910 underwater. In January the Seine rose until it swamped whole quarters of the city and people crossed the boulevards on planks — a wet, disrupted, memorable winter, and the backdrop to the months Rousseau spent finishing the largest canvas of his life in a rented room.

Above the flood line, the city was in the middle of the fastest artistic upheaval in its history. Cubism was two or three years old, Matisse and the Fauves had already detonated, and a generation was hunting for anything that got behind trained European habits of seeing. Rousseau, who had no training to unlearn, arrived at the perfect moment: an artist making pictures that were flat, frontal, dreamlike and completely uninterested in the rules the academies had spent three centuries perfecting. The Salon des Indépendants, still without a jury and still open to anyone who paid the fee, was where all of them came together every spring — the serious, the fashionable and the ridiculous, hung side by side.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was sixty-five when he hung *The Dream*, and turned sixty-six that May. He was living in a small flat and studio, teaching, playing violin, painting, and short of money as always. In his last year he was courting a shop worker named Léonie, and was reportedly refused; friends thought the rejection had knocked something out of him.

He cut his leg. The wound went bad, gangrene set in, and he was taken to the Necker hospital in Paris, where he died on 2 September 1910 of a blood clot. He was sixty-six. He was buried in a rented grave at Bagneux with seven people present: the painters Paul Signac and Manuel Ortiz de Zárate, Robert and Sonia Delaunay, the sculptor Constantin Brâncuși, his landlord Armand Queval, and Apollinaire, who wrote the epitaph that Brâncuși later carved onto the stone — a greeting to gentle Rousseau, telling him they could still be heard. In 1947 his remains were moved to Laval, the town that had turned down his painting.

## Interesting Facts

- At roughly two by three metres, *The Dream* is the largest canvas Rousseau ever painted, finished months before his death.
- He wrote a poem to hang with it, explaining that Yadwigha had fallen asleep and was hearing a charmer's pipe.
- Asked what the sofa was doing in a jungle, he explained the woman is asleep on her couch in Paris and dreaming the forest.
- Apollinaire's review of the 1910 Indépendants declared the painting indisputably beautiful and predicted that this year nobody would laugh.
- Picasso's 1908 banquet for Rousseau at the Bateau-Lavoir is real, but accounts of it flatly contradict each other — sincere homage or elaborate prank, depending on who was telling it.
- Nelson Rockefeller gave the painting to the Museum of Modern Art in 1954, for the museum's twenty-fifth anniversary.
- Rousseau was buried in a rented grave with seven mourners; his remains were moved to his home town of Laval in 1947.
