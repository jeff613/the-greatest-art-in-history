## The Story

In the summer of 1517, a year after Bosch died, an Italian churchman named Antonio de Beatis walked through the Brussels palace of the counts of Nassau in the retinue of a cardinal, and stopped in front of a painting. He wrote in his diary that it showed men and women, black and white, in every kind of pose, and birds and beasts of all sorts, and that the whole thing was so bizarre and so delightful that it could not be described to anyone who had not seen it. That is the earliest record we have of this picture, and it is worth holding onto: the first witness did not say he was frightened. He said he was charmed.

Closed, the triptych is not colour at all. It is a grey-green globe, the world on the third day of creation, plants without animals, God very small in the upper corner with a book, and above him a line from the Psalms: for he spoke, and it was done. Then the shutters open and the noise begins.

On the left, Eden — God joining the hands of Adam and Eve, a pink fountain rising out of a black pool, an elephant and a giraffe and a cat trotting off with a mouse in its jaws. In the centre, a wide green park with hundreds of naked people, none of them ashamed, riding stags and boars and unicorns in a circle around a pond of bathing women, feeding one another blackberries the size of boulders, crawling in and out of shells and eggs. On the right, everything goes black. A city burns on the horizon. A pair of enormous ears advances with a blade between them. A bird-headed devil on a high chair swallows the damned one after another. A man crucified on a harp; a choir singing from a score written across another man's buttocks; and at the centre a hollow eggshell body on two rotting tree-trunk legs, whose face turns and looks straight out at you.

Nobody knows what it is for. Nobody knows who ordered it, though the Nassau family are the likeliest candidates. It hung in a palace rather than a church, which means it was a wonder shown to guests, not an altarpiece to kneel before — a five-hundred-year-old argument that has never been settled.

## The World Behind It

Bosch's town, 's-Hertogenbosch, sat in Brabant, in the Burgundian and then Habsburg Netherlands: a prosperous market town of some twenty thousand people, dominated by the huge church of Saint John, thick with confraternities, processions and preaching. This was a culture that thought hard and constantly about the Last Judgement, and that had recently acquired the printing press, which meant devotional images and moral satires were now cheap and everywhere.

It was also a world whose edges were coming apart. Columbus made landfall in 1492; Portuguese ships were bringing back animals and fruits nobody in Brabant had names for. The exotic beasts and unplaceable plants that crowd the left panel are not only fantasy — they are what a curious man in a trading region heard about and half-imagined.

One thing this painting is not is Protestant. Luther posted his theses in 1517, the year after Bosch was buried. Whatever quarrel Bosch had with monks, nuns and the appetites of his neighbours, he was having it entirely inside the old church.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was somewhere between forty and sixty when he painted it, and by then a settled man. He had married a wealthy woman, Aleyt Goyaerts van den Meervenne, some years his senior; he lived on the market square of the town he never seems to have left; he had been a sworn member of the Brotherhood of Our Lady, the town's most exclusive religious confraternity, since the mid-1480s, which put him in the same rooms as the men with money.

His reputation had travelled ahead of him. In 1504 Philip the Handsome, Duke of Burgundy, put down thirty-six livres toward a Last Judgement from the hand of "Jeronimus van Aeken" — one of the few hard documents of Bosch's working life, and for a painting now lost.

What survives of the man himself is almost nothing. No letters, no theory, no recorded remark. He signed his pictures with the name of his town — Jheronimus Bosch — and left the explaining to everyone else.

## Interesting Facts

- The earliest known description, by the traveller Antonio de Beatis in 1517, calls the painting delightful and admits it cannot be described to anyone who has not seen it.
- Bosch gave it no title. Sixteenth-century Spanish inventories listed it simply as *La Lujuria* — Lust — and a 1605 account called it the strawberry painting.
- Shut, the triptych is a grisaille globe showing the world on the third day of creation, inscribed with Psalm 33:9.
- In 2014 an American undergraduate transcribed the notation painted across a damned soul's buttocks in the hell panel and put a recording online as the "500-year-old butt song from hell"; it went viral, though the reading involves a good deal of guesswork.
- Dendrochronology dates the oak to trees felled between 1460 and 1466; the painting itself is usually placed between 1490 and 1510.
- The Duke of Alba's men confiscated it in 1568; Philip II of Spain bought it at auction in 1591 and sent it to the monastery of El Escorial, where the monk José de Sigüenza defended it in 1605 against the charge of heresy, calling it a painted satire on the sins and follies of man.
- Wilhelm Fraenger argued in 1947 that Bosch belonged to a heretical sect and that the central panel shows paradise regained rather than sin. It is an influential theory and most scholars regard it as unsupported.
