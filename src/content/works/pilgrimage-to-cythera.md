## The Story

The Royal Academy approved Antoine Watteau as a candidate in the summer of 1712 and then settled in to wait. A candidate owed the Academy a reception piece, a painting that would prove what he was and fix where he belonged in the hierarchy — history at the top, portraits below, landscape and low life at the bottom. Watteau owed his for five years. He was reprimanded more than once. He appears to have painted the thing at last in about eight months and delivered it on 28 August 1717.

What he handed over was a hillside at the end of an afternoon, in a light that has already started to go golden and cool. On the right, at the top of the slope, stands a stone herm of Venus wound with pink roses, and beneath it a couple who have not moved at all — the man still talking, the woman still listening, her fan open in her hand while a small cupid pulls at her skirt to get her going. Below them a second man is drawing his companion up onto her feet. Below them again a third pair are walking away downhill, and she is looking back over her shoulder at the place they are leaving. Then the ground drops to the shore, where a gilded boat is waiting, a rigged mast of drapery above it, and a swarm of small cupids circling in the haze over the water. Every figure in the picture is at a different point along the same short journey, from sitting still to going. It reads like one couple filmed across ten minutes, printed as a single frame.

The title, then and now, has caused trouble. Cythera was the Aegean island off which Venus was said to have risen from the sea, and eighteenth-century Paris knew it from the stage — scholars have long linked the picture to a line in a 1700 comedy inviting the audience to embark for the isle. But nobody arrives here. The couples have already paired off, the garlands are already hung, and in 1961 the art historian Michael Levey argued flatly that the picture shows them leaving, not setting out. The party is over. That is why the woman looks back.

Confronted with it, the Academy's clerk wrote down the title he had been given — *Le pèlerinage à l'isle de Cithère* — struck it out, and entered instead "a fête galante." A genre had just been invented to hold one painting.

## The World Behind It

Louis XIV had died two years earlier, after seventy-two years on the throne and decades of war, taxation, and Versailles piety. His heir was five. France was run by the boy's cousin Philippe, duc d'Orléans, and the Regent moved the whole apparatus of government back to Paris, out of the great cold palace and into the city's private houses. The tone changed overnight. Suppers, theatre, gambling, and a great deal of open scandal replaced the late king's grim devotion.

Money changed too. The Scottish financier John Law had opened a bank in Paris in 1716 and in 1717 launched the trading company whose shares would, within three years, make and then annihilate a generation of Parisian fortunes. The men buying Watteau's pictures were exactly these people: bankers, dealers, speculators, collectors with new money and no chateau. He never painted for the court. He painted for the city, in a decade when the city suddenly believed that pleasure was a reasonable way to organise a life.

## The Artist at This Moment

Watteau was thirty-two and already coughing. He had spent the previous years living partly in the house of the financier Pierre Crozat, whose collection ran to hundreds of paintings and thousands of drawings by the Venetians and by Rubens, and who let him look at all of it. He had no wife, no household, no fixed lodging he kept for long, and a reputation among his friends for being prickly, restless, and strangely careless about his own future.

The reception piece made him, officially, a member of the Academy. It did not make him a history painter, which was the only rank that carried real prestige, and the new label the clerk had improvised guaranteed he never would be. He seems not to have argued. He rarely argued about anything to do with his career; the men who wrote about him afterwards described someone who could be caustic about a brushstroke and completely blank about his own prospects.

He had four years left, and he spent them painting actors, shop girls, guitar players and departures.

## Interesting Facts

- The Academy's minutes for 28 August 1717 show the original title crossed out and replaced with "une feste galante" — the first time the phrase was used to classify a painting.
- Watteau painted a second, richer version of the subject, now in Schloss Charlottenburg in Berlin, with a ship added at the shore and far more cupids in the air; an earlier and much smaller treatment hangs in the Städel in Frankfurt.
- The Louvre canvas is 129 by 194 centimetres — modest for a reception piece, in a period when ambition was usually measured in square metres.
- Nobody in the picture is arriving. The couples are already paired, the statue is already garlanded, and one woman is looking back at what she is leaving.
- The stone figure on the right is a herm of Venus, the goddess whose island this is, watching her own pilgrims pack up and go.
- The canvas stayed in the Academy's own rooms for seventy-six years and only entered the Louvre in 1793, when the Revolution turned the palace into a public museum.
- A story repeated since the nineteenth century holds that art students in the building used it for target practice in those years, flicking bread pellets at it — Rococo was by then held in open contempt.
