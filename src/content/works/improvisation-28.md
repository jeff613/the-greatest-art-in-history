## The Story

For Kandinsky the word *Improvisation* meant something precise. He sorted his most ambitious pictures into three kinds: *Impressions*, drawn from nature; *Compositions*, planned slowly and deliberately over months; and *Improvisations*, which he described as largely unconscious, spontaneous expressions of inner feeling — the visual equivalent of a musician improvising at the keyboard. *Improvisation 28* is one of the boldest of the series, and the "second version" in its title means exactly that: he had painted a first attempt at the same idea and then reworked it into this larger, more resolved canvas in 1912.

Look closely and half-buried images swim up out of the storm of line and color. Scholars have long read the left side as a scene of destruction — a tossing boat, surging waves, the black strokes of what may be cannon — and the right side as its opposite, a vision of salvation: an embracing couple, a glowing sun, the flames of candles. It is the same drama of apocalypse and paradise that obsessed Kandinsky in these years, dissolved almost, but not quite, into abstraction.

The picture belongs to the moment Kandinsky committed fully to that dissolving. He liked to tell the story of its origin: returning to his Munich studio one evening at dusk, he was stopped by a painting of "indescribable beauty," glowing with an inner radiance, whose subject he could not make out at all. Only after a moment did he realize it was one of his own canvases, leaning against the wall on its side. The next morning, in daylight and the right way up, the magic was gone — he could see the objects again, and they seemed to him to spoil the picture. The lesson stuck: the recognizable thing, he decided, was getting in the way. Works like *Improvisation 28* are what he made once he set it aside.

This is also a founding object of one of the world's great modern collections. The American mining magnate Solomon R. Guggenheim, guided by the artist and adviser Hilla Rebay, became a passionate collector of Kandinsky, and *Improvisation 28* entered the holdings that would open to the public as the Guggenheim Museum — which still owns more of his work than almost anywhere on earth.

## The World Behind It

Munich in 1912 was one of the crucibles of modern art, and Kandinsky sat at its center. The year before, he and Franz Marc had broken away to found Der Blaue Reiter; in 1912 the group published its famous almanac and Kandinsky issued *Concerning the Spiritual in Art*, the little book in which he argued that color and form could speak directly to the soul, as music does, without needing to describe the world.

That argument was fed by the mystical currents swirling through pre-war Europe — Theosophy, talk of a coming spiritual age, a widespread sense that the material nineteenth century was exhausted and something vast was about to break. Kandinsky's veiled scenes of flood and deliverance are the pictorial side of that mood: he believed art had a duty to prepare humanity for a great renewal, and he was trying to paint the feeling of it rather than the fact. It is no accident that the same disaster-and-salvation drama surfaces again in his *Compositions* of these years.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1912 Kandinsky was forty-six, a Russian who had spent sixteen years turning himself from a Moscow lawyer into the theorist-in-chief of the German avant-garde. He was living and working with Gabriele Münter, moving between Munich and their house at Murnau, and he had just published the book that made his name as a thinker as much as a painter.

He was, in these months, taking the final step toward the pure abstraction that would define him. The *Improvisations* were his laboratory for it — pictures in which he let feeling lead and pushed recognizable subjects to the vanishing point, trusting that a viewer would respond to color and line the way a listener responds to music. It was risky, uncharted work, and he was making it faster and more freely than at any other time in his life. He had two productive years left in this German idyll before the war of 1914 tore it apart, ended his life with Münter, and sent him home to Russia.

## Interesting Facts

- Kandinsky divided his major works into *Impressions*, *Compositions*, and *Improvisations*; the *Improvisations* were meant to be spontaneous and largely unconscious.
- The "second version" in the title is literal: he reworked an earlier canvas of the same subject into this larger one.
- Hidden in the abstraction are opposed scenes — destruction on the left (a boat, waves, cannon) and salvation on the right (a couple, sun, and candles).
- Kandinsky traced his turn to abstraction to an evening when he failed to recognize one of his own paintings, standing on its side in the dusk.
- The painting was collected by Solomon R. Guggenheim on the advice of Hilla Rebay and became part of the founding collection of his museum.
- The Guggenheim holds one of the largest collections of Kandinsky's work anywhere in the world.
