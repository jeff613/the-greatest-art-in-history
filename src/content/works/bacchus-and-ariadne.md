## The Story

The god of wine has just seen her, and he cannot stop himself. Bacchus, riding into the scene on a chariot pulled by cheetahs, catches sight of Ariadne alone on the shore and vaults straight out of the moving car toward her, his rose-pink cloak whirling behind him, one leg still trailing in the air. Titian caught him at the single instant of the leap — the whole painting hangs on that suspended, impossible moment of a god falling in love in midair. Ariadne, startled, twists away even as she turns to look, her blue and red drapery snapping with the same violence as his cloak.

Her story is misery turning to fortune. The Cretan princess had given the hero Theseus the thread that led him out of the Minotaur's labyrinth, then fled Crete with him — only to be abandoned, asleep, on the island of Naxos. On the far left of the picture you can just make out Theseus's ship, a pale sail slipping over the horizon, sailing away and leaving her. Into that fresh grief crashes Bacchus and his riotous, drunken train: a follower wrapped in writhing snakes, a fat Silenus lolling on a donkey, satyrs and maenads clashing cymbals and waving the severed leg of a calf. Titian stitched the scene together from two ancient poets, taking the abandoned heroine and the wild rout from Catullus and the sudden divine meeting from Ovid.

And there is a promise written in the sky. Just above Ariadne's head hangs a small ring of eight stars — the constellation Corona Borealis, the Northern Crown. Bacchus will marry her, make her immortal, and fling her crown into the heavens as those very stars. The whole composition, one great diagonal of blue and green sweeping down to the lovers, drives the eye from her earthly desertion to her place among the constellations. It is a picture about the instant a life pivots from despair to glory.

## The World Behind It

The painting was made for one of the most sophisticated art lovers of the age, Alfonso I d'Este, Duke of Ferrara, for a small private study he called the *camerino d'alabastro* — the little alabaster room. Alfonso wanted a suite of pictures on ancient themes of wine, love, and revelry, drawn from classical texts, to line this intimate space. He first turned to Raphael for a *Triumph of Bacchus*, but Raphael died in 1520 leaving only a drawing, and the commission passed to Titian, who delivered this thunderous canvas by 1523.

It was a competitive arena. The room already held works by the aging Venetian master Giovanni Bellini and the Ferrarese painter Dosso Dossi, and Titian was determined to outdo them all. Alfonso was a demanding, deep-pocketed patron who badgered his artists for delivery, and the *camerino* became one of the great showcases of Renaissance mythological painting — a private gallery of pagan pleasure for a duke steeped in humanist learning.

## The Artist at This Moment

Titian was only in his early thirties, and this was the work that proved the young Venetian could stand with anyone in Italy. Bellini and Giorgione were dead; Raphael had just died; Michelangelo and the Roman school were the standard against which everything was measured. With *Bacchus and Ariadne* Titian answered them with sheer color and energy — a picture no Roman could have painted, drenched in a light and a blue that only Venice, with its trade routes to the East, could supply.

That blue is the tell of his ambition. The vast sky is laid in with ultramarine, ground from lapis lazuli imported across the world from the mountains of Afghanistan and worth more by weight than gold. Titian spread it across one of the largest expanses of the pigment in all of Renaissance art, on the sky, the sea, and Ariadne's sash — a young painter spending a fortune in pigment to make an entrance no one would forget. If you look closely at the brass urn near Ariadne's feet, he signed it plainly: *TICIANVS F.* — Titian made this.

## Interesting Facts

- Titian froze Bacchus at the exact instant of leaping from his chariot — a daring bit of arrested motion that makes a god's coup de foudre feel physically real.
- The ring of eight stars above Ariadne is the constellation Corona Borealis; the myth ends with Bacchus casting her crown into the sky to make her immortal.
- The pale ship vanishing on the left horizon is Theseus's — the lover who has just abandoned Ariadne asleep on Naxos.
- Titian braided two ancient sources together: the wild Bacchic procession from Catullus and the moment of meeting from Ovid's *Metamorphoses*.
- The sky is one of the broadest uses of ultramarine — crushed lapis lazuli, costlier than gold — of any painting of the era.
- Raphael held the commission first but died in 1520 with only a drawing done, so it fell to Titian, who signed his triumph on the brass urn: *TICIANVS F.*
