## The Story

Two lovers kneel at the edge of a flowering cliff, wrapped in a single cloak of gold. The man, crowned with ivy, bends the woman's face up to kiss her cheek; she surrenders with closed eyes, her fingers curling. Around them there is no room, no landscape, no world — only a shimmering gold void and, beneath their feet, a narrow meadow of flowers that seems to drop away into nothing. It is the most reproduced image of desire in Western art, and Klimt painted it at the height of what admirers call his Golden Phase, laying real gold and silver leaf over the oil the way his father had once beaten gold for a living.

The circumstances of its debut were extraordinary. When The Kiss went on show at the 1908 Kunstschau — a vast exhibition Klimt and his circle mounted to mark the sixtieth anniversary of the Emperor's reign — it was still unfinished. Yet the Austrian state, which had spent years attacking Klimt over his Faculty Paintings, bought it on the spot, straight off the wall of the exhibition, for its Moderne Galerie. A painter the establishment had denounced as a pornographer was suddenly, and permanently, national property. Klimt took the canvas back afterward to make his final changes before delivering it.

Who are the lovers? Klimt, as always, refused to say. The most romantic guess is that they are Klimt himself and Emilie Flöge, the fashion designer who was his companion for a quarter century; others see one of his many models. He left no note, no title beyond "Lovers," no explanation of any kind — a wordless man who let the gold do the talking. What he made was not a portrait of two people but an icon of the act itself, a couple dissolving into a single golden shape in the instant before the world drops away.

## The World Behind It

The Kiss belongs to Vienna at its most brilliant and most brittle. The Habsburg capital in 1908 was a city of coffee houses and chandeliers, of Freud mapping the unconscious a few streets from the palace, of Mahler at the opera and Schnitzler dramatizing the sexual hypocrisy of the middle class. Beneath the imperial glitter ran an obsession with eros and death, and Klimt gave that obsession its most seductive face. Gold, in his hands, was not just decoration but a deliberate throwback — a nod to the Byzantine mosaics he had seen in Ravenna, whose sacred, timeless surfaces he borrowed for an entirely worldly subject.

That his golden lovers were bought by the state is one of the sweeter ironies in the history of art. Only a few years earlier the same establishment had dragged Klimt through a national scandal over the Faculty Paintings. The Kunstschau of 1908 was in part his answer: a self-financed festival of the new, staged on the emperor's jubilee, entirely on his own terms. Vienna was learning, slowly and against its will, that its most modern artist was also its greatest — even as the empire that produced him had barely a decade left to live.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1907 Klimt was in his mid-forties and at the absolute peak of his powers and reputation. The Golden Phase was pouring out of him: in the same stretch he finished the dazzling Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I and began The Kiss, handling gold with a confidence no other painter of his time could match. He was wealthy, sought after by Vienna's richest families, and utterly set in his private routines — living with his mother and sisters, working in a garden studio, wearing a long painter's smock with nothing underneath, and vanishing each summer to the Attersee lake with Emilie Flöge.

He had also made peace with being an outsider inside his own establishment. The Faculty Paintings wound had hardened into a permanent refusal of public commissions; he answered only to private patrons and to himself. The Kiss is the work of a man who has stopped arguing with his critics and simply out-dazzled them. It would prove to be the summit. Within a decade the war would gut Vienna, the empire would fall, and Klimt would be dead — but in these two golden years he was exactly where he wanted to be.

## Interesting Facts

- The Austrian state bought The Kiss in 1908 while it was still unfinished, straight off the wall of the Kunstschau — from the same establishment that had branded Klimt a pornographer over his Faculty Paintings.
- Klimt used real gold and silver leaf applied over oil paint; his father had been a gold engraver, and the medium was, quite literally, in the family's hands.
- The lovers have never been identified. Popular tradition names Klimt and his companion Emilie Flöge, but Klimt left no clue, calling the picture simply "Lovers."
- The canvas is a near-perfect square, about 180 by 180 centimeters, and its glimmering surfaces borrow directly from the Byzantine mosaics Klimt admired in Ravenna.
- It has never left Austria's national collection since 1908 and now hangs, endlessly photographed, in the Belvedere in Vienna.
