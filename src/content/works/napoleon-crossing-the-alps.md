## The Story

A young general reins in a rearing white stallion on a windswept mountainside, his cloak boiling around him like flame, one gloved hand thrown up toward the summit while his whole army toils invisibly below. This is Napoleon Bonaparte as he wished to be seen: not a man but a force of nature, serene in the middle of the storm. He had just led an army over the Alps in May 1800 to fall on the Austrians in Italy, and he commissioned David to fix the legend in paint.

Napoleon gave clear instructions. He refused to sit — "nobody knows if the portraits of great men resemble them," he said, "it is enough that their genius lives there" — and told David to show him "calm on a fiery horse." So David worked mostly from a uniform, a borrowed pose, and his own imagination. Into the rocks in the foreground he carved three names — BONAPARTE, HANNIBAL, and KAROLVS MAGNVS, Charlemagne — placing the general in the company of the only two commanders who had famously crossed the same mountains before him.

The reality was less operatic. Napoleon made the actual crossing not on a spirited charger but on a sure-footed mule, bundled in a grey coat, led over the ice by a local guide who knew the passes. David's picture is not a lie so much as a myth — propaganda of the highest order, designed to turn a hard slog through the snow into an image of destiny.

It was so successful that David painted it five times. The first version, with its orange cloak and piebald horse, is the one now at Malmaison, Josephine's country house; the others went to palaces across Europe. Half a century later the painter Paul Delaroche answered David's fantasy with the truth: in 1850 he painted the same crossing with Napoleon hunched and cold on his mule, a tired man on a tired animal picking his way through grey rock. The two pictures make a perfect pair — the myth and the fact, hung across the history of a single mountain road.

## The World Behind It

By 1800 the French Revolution had burned itself out and a soldier had stepped into the vacuum. Napoleon Bonaparte, a general made famous by his campaigns in Italy and Egypt, had seized power in the coup of November 1799 and made himself First Consul, the effective ruler of France. He was not yet emperor, but he was already building the image that would carry him there.

The crossing of the Alps was a genuine feat — moving an army with its artillery over the Great St Bernard Pass to surprise the Austrians — and the ensuing victory at Marengo secured his grip on power. Napoleon understood, better than almost any ruler before him, that images make rulers, and he set the machinery of art to work glorifying himself. David's equestrian portrait was a keystone of that campaign. It borrowed the age-old language of the hero on horseback and updated it for a new kind of leader, one who claimed to embody the will of a whole nation. The picture helped sell the idea that France's turmoil had found its resolution in one extraordinary man — the founding myth of the coming Empire.

## The Artist at This Moment

David had come through the Revolution alive, but only just. Imprisoned twice after the fall of Robespierre, the former artistic dictator of the Republic had been lucky to keep his head, and he emerged looking for a new patron and a new cause. He found both in Napoleon, in whom he saw a hero worthy of his brush.

The commission for Napoleon Crossing the Alps marked the beginning of a partnership that would define David's final creative decade. He became First Painter to the Emperor and produced the enormous state pictures — above all the Coronation — that fixed the image of the Napoleonic regime for all time. It was a startling reinvention: the man who had painted the martyrs of the Republic and voted to kill a king now bent his genius to glorifying a new monarch. Critics have called it opportunism, and it was; but David seems genuinely to have believed in Napoleon as the strong man who could redeem the Revolution's chaos. The faith would cost him again. When Napoleon fell, David fell with him, into the Brussels exile where he would die.

## Interesting Facts

- Napoleon refused to pose, telling David that a portrait needed to capture a great man's genius, not his exact likeness, and asked only to be shown "calm on a fiery horse."
- He actually crossed the Alps on a mule, wrapped against the cold and led by a local guide — a far cry from the rearing stallion in the painting.
- David painted five versions between 1801 and 1805; the earliest now hangs at Malmaison, and the others are in Berlin, Vienna, and Versailles.
- The names BONAPARTE, HANNIBAL, and CAROLVS MAGNVS (Charlemagne) are carved into the rocks at the horse's feet, ranking Napoleon with the great Alpine conquerors of history.
- In 1850 the artist Paul Delaroche painted a deliberately truthful counter-version, showing a cold, weary Napoleon slumped on a plodding mule.
- The Malmaison version was first commissioned not by Napoleon but by the King of Spain, as a diplomatic gift celebrating the new Franco-Spanish friendship.
