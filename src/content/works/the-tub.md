## The Story

A woman kneels in a shallow tin tub, one hand braced on the floor, the other sponging the back of her neck. We look down on her from above and to the side, so steeply that the floor tilts up and a narrow shelf of toilet articles — a jug, a comb, brushes — crowds in from the right. She is entirely absorbed in an ordinary act, and entirely unaware of us. That is the whole point, and it was the whole scandal.

For centuries the nude in Western art had posed. Venus reclined, goddesses turned to display themselves, and the naked body faced the viewer as if aware of being admired. Degas threw all of that out. "Until now," he reportedly said, "the nude has always been represented in poses which presuppose an audience. But my women are simple, honest people who are concerned with nothing beyond their physical occupations. It is as if you looked through a keyhole." His bathers scrub, stoop, dry their feet, and comb their hair in poses no one would ever strike for an artist — awkward, unlovely, real.

This pastel was one of a suite of bathing women Degas unveiled at the eighth and final Impressionist exhibition in 1886. The group was among the most talked-about things in the show, and the reaction split sharply. Many critics admired the daring and the beauty of the pastel handling; others were disturbed. To some, the steep, spying viewpoint made the viewer into a voyeur peering at a woman who had no idea she was watched, and they read cruelty or contempt into these unflattering, animal poses. Whether Degas meant tenderness or coldness has been argued ever since. What is certain is that he permanently changed what a nude could be: not an offering to the eye, but a private moment stolen from a life.

## The World Behind It

The 1886 exhibition was the last gasp of the Impressionist experiment. The original group had fractured; Monet and Renoir stayed away, the young Georges Seurat arrived with his revolutionary pointillism, and the movement that had begun a dozen years earlier was dissolving into what would come next. Into this fractious final show Degas dropped his bathers, and they felt like a manifesto for a new kind of realism.

They also reflected changing habits. Private washing at home, in a basin or portable tub, was becoming more common among ordinary Parisians, and Degas seized on it as a thoroughly modern subject — the body treated not as myth but as domestic fact. He brought tubs and basins into his studio and had his models wash as they would at home, then drew them absorbed in the task. Nothing could have been further from the polished Salon nudes still winning official prizes, and that was exactly his intention.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1886 Degas was in his early fifties and increasingly turning from oil to pastel — a medium that let him draw and color at once, in the direct, tactile way he loved, and that grew still more suited to him as his eyesight declined. His vision, troubled since his thirties, was worsening, and pastel's bold strokes and strong contrasts may have been easier for his failing eyes than the fine detail of painting.

He was also hardening into the solitary, difficult man of his later legend. Never married, sharp-tongued, and fiercely private, he was withdrawing from the group life of his younger years even as he dominated its final exhibition. The bathers suited his temperament perfectly: intimate yet detached, made not from a live encounter but built up in the studio through relentless observation. He would keep drawing these women for years, chasing the same handful of humble gestures, long after the Impressionist adventure that this show brought to a close.

## Interesting Facts

- Degas described his bathers as seen "as if you looked through a keyhole" — a line that fueled accusations of voyeurism.
- The pastel was shown in a suite of bathing women at the eighth and final Impressionist exhibition in 1886.
- He broke a centuries-old convention by painting a nude who is unaware of the viewer, absorbed in an everyday task rather than posing.
- Critics divided sharply: some praised the pictures' beauty and daring, others read cruelty or contempt in the ungainly poses.
- Degas brought real tubs and basins into his studio and had models wash as they would at home.
- In his later years he increasingly favored pastel over oil, partly because its bold, direct handling suited his deteriorating eyesight.
