## The Story

A gaunt old man sits crumpled on the ground, his body bent into an angular knot, cradling a large brown guitar that is the only warm thing in the picture. Everything else is blue — the walls, the shadows, the man's skin, his rags, the very air around him. His eyes are shut, his head lolls, and he seems less to be playing the instrument than clinging to it, as if music were the last thing keeping him alive. Picasso painted him in Barcelona over the winter of 1903–1904, at the frozen heart of what we now call his Blue Period, when nearly everything he made was drenched in that one cold color and populated by the people society had thrown away: beggars, drunks, the blind, the starving.

That obsession with the wretched was not an abstract pose. Picasso was barely into his twenties and desperately poor himself, shuttling between Paris and Barcelona, sleeping in unheated rooms, sometimes burning his own drawings for warmth. The color blue, he later said, came out of grief — the suicide of his close friend Carles Casagemas in 1901 had cracked something open in him. "It was thinking of Casagemas," he told a critic, "that got me started painting in blue." The old guitarist is the type made flesh: an outcast, blind, and utterly alone, yet dignified by the single act of making music.

And there is a secret buried in the panel. In raking light you can just make out the faint outline of a woman's face floating near the guitarist's neck — a ghost bleeding up through the blue. Because Picasso could not afford fresh materials, he painted this masterpiece straight over earlier pictures. When the Art Institute of Chicago X-rayed the panel in the late 1990s, at least two abandoned compositions surfaced beneath the guitarist: a seated woman with her head bowed and arm outstretched, and beneath her a young mother with a child and animals. The image of poverty was itself painted on the evidence of it.

## The World Behind It

Around 1900 Barcelona was Spain's most modern, restless city, a place of anarchist bombs, factory smoke, and a bohemian avant-garde that gathered in cafés like Els Quatre Gats, where the teenage Picasso first exhibited. But it was also a city of visible destitution, and the fashionable pessimism of the age — the melancholy of the fin de siècle, the vogue for Symbolist gloom — gave a young artist both a mood and a subject. Picasso's blue beggars belong to that tradition of the poeticized poor.

They also owe something to the raw economics of his life. He was crossing back and forth between Barcelona and Paris, unknown, unsold, and broke. The Blue Period pictures did not sell well at the time; collectors found them depressing. Only later would this unhappy, unprofitable phase come to look like the necessary apprenticeship of the century's most dominant artist — the years he learned to make emotion visible before he set about dismantling the whole idea of a picture.

## The Artist at This Moment

Picasso was twenty-two, and grief was still fresh. Casagemas, the friend with whom he had first come to Paris, had shot himself in a café in 1901 after a woman rejected him, and the loss haunted the younger man's imagination for years. Picasso had not been present at the death, but he took it into himself, painting Casagemas in his coffin and, more diffusely, pouring the darkness into an entire body of work.

He was also, simply, poor — and painting over old canvases because new ones cost money he did not have. The confidence that would soon make him the most disruptive figure in art was there in the drawing, but the swagger had not yet arrived. This is Picasso before Cubism, before fame, before the money: a serious, sorrowful young Spaniard turning his own cold rooms and dead friends into something that still stops people in the galleries of Chicago a century later.

## Interesting Facts

- Beneath the surface lie at least two abandoned paintings; a ghostly woman's face is faintly visible near the guitarist's neck to the naked eye, and X-rays in the late 1990s revealed the rest.
- Picasso painted over earlier works because, in his poverty, he could not afford new panels or canvas.
- The Blue Period was triggered in part by the 1901 suicide of his friend Carles Casagemas — "It was thinking of Casagemas that got me started painting in blue."
- The elongated, folded pose deliberately echoes the mannered saints of El Greco, the Spanish old master Picasso revered.
- The painting entered the Art Institute of Chicago in 1926 as part of the Helen Birch Bartlett Memorial Collection, where it has been a centerpiece ever since.
- The picture inspired Wallace Stevens's long 1937 poem "The Man with the Blue Guitar."
