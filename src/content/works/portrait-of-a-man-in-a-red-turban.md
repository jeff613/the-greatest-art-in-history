## The Story

A man of middle age turns his head and looks directly out of the darkness, his face creased, his eyes slightly bloodshot, an extravagant scarlet headdress folded into peaks above his brow. Nothing softens him. There is no landscape, no attribute, no flattery — just a weathered face rendered so exactly you feel you could name every pore. And across the top of the picture, painted in Greek-looking capitals as though carved into the wood, runs a phrase van Eyck used nowhere so boldly: *Als Ich Can* — "as I can," a pun on his own name and a painter's proud shrug, *as well as I am able, though not as well as I would wish.*

That motto, unusually large and prominent here, is the main reason many believe this is van Eyck looking at himself. So is the gaze. In an age when sitters were shown in three-quarter profile, gazing modestly aside, this man stares straight down the line of sight — the pose of someone studying his own reflection in a mirror, catching his own eye. A commissioned patron rarely met the viewer so directly; a painter at work on his own likeness could hardly avoid it.

Along the bottom of the original frame van Eyck added a second line in Latin, recording that he made the picture on 21 October 1433 — one of the exact, boastful dates he liked to stamp on his work. If the sitter really is the artist, this is one of the first independent self-portraits in Western painting, a man stepping out from behind his patrons to say, simply, *here I am, and this is what I can do.* We cannot prove it. But the confrontational stare and the double-edged motto have persuaded generations that the tired, unblinking man in the red turban is van Eyck himself, holding our gaze across six centuries.

## The World Behind It

The independent portrait — a picture of a person made for its own sake, not as part of an altarpiece or a donor's prayer — was still a young idea in the 1430s, and the Netherlandish painters were busy inventing it. Van Eyck led the way, turning out likenesses of merchants, clerics and courtiers with a forensic honesty that flattered no one and convinced everyone. His faces were not idealised types but specific, mortal individuals, wrinkles and stubble and all.

The "turban" itself belongs to that world of Flemish material display: it is really a *chaperon*, a hood of fine wool wound and tied into an elaborate shape, dyed the costly red that signalled means. Bruges, where van Eyck worked, was a city obsessed with cloth and colour, and even a plain bust portrait became a subtle inventory of status through the quality of the fabric on a sitter's head.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1433 van Eyck was in Bruges, secure at the summit of his career. He was the trusted painter and sometime envoy of Philip the Good, freshly celebrated for the Ghent Altarpiece completed the year before, and would sign the Arnolfini Portrait just months later. This was the tight, brilliant span in which he made the handful of works his fame now rests on.

If the man in the turban is van Eyck, the picture catches him in a mood of quiet self-assertion. He gives himself no props of wealth or rank — only the sober red hood and that motto, staking his claim not on birth but on skill. It is the self-image of a craftsman who knew exactly how good he was, and who had begun, almost alone among his contemporaries, to think that the maker of a picture was worth recording as carefully as the people who paid for one.

## Interesting Facts

- The Greek-lettered motto at the top, *Als Ich Can* ("as I can"), is a pun on the name "Eyck" and van Eyck's personal signature-phrase, here painted larger than on any other work.
- The sitter's rare, straight-out-at-you gaze — the look of a man watching himself in a mirror — is the strongest visual argument that this is a self-portrait.
- The original frame carries a painted inscription dating the work precisely to 21 October 1433, in van Eyck's habitual boastful manner.
- The "red turban" is not Eastern dress but a *chaperon*, a folded and knotted Flemish wool hood, tied up here into its dramatic peaked shape.
- If it is indeed van Eyck, it stands among the earliest independent self-portraits in Western art — the painter stepping out from behind his patrons.
- The likeness later served as the model for the presumed image of van Eyck himself, the face by which he is now remembered.
