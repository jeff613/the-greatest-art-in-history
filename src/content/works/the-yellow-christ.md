## The Story

In a chapel at Trémalo, on a wooded hill twenty minutes' walk above Pont-Aven, there hangs a wooden crucifix that nobody can date with confidence and nobody signed. The figure is crude, long-limbed, and painted a pale yellow that has gone chalky with age, the head tipped sideways in an expression somewhere between agony and sleep. Gauguin saw it in 1889 and lifted it wholesale.

He set it outdoors, in the middle of the Breton countryside in autumn, the fields behind it burnt orange and gold, a line of low hills and a village in the distance, one small figure climbing over a wall to get out of the picture. At the foot of the cross three women in Breton costume sit on the grass. They are not weeping. Their hands rest in their laps, their faces are calm and closed, and they look less like witnesses to an execution than like women waiting out a long afternoon. That is the whole disturbance of the painting: the crucifixion is not an event here, it is simply a permanent fixture of the landscape, as ordinary as the hedgerow, and the people beneath it have lived alongside it their entire lives.

The colour does the rest. Gauguin outlined every form in dark blue and filled the shapes with flat, unbroken hues, a manner critics named cloisonnism after the enamel technique where metal ribs hold each field of colour apart. There is no attempt at the flicker of real light. The Christ is yellow because the picture needs him yellow.

He painted it in the second of his Brittany summers, the one in which he was consolidating everything the *Vision after the Sermon* had opened up the year before. It mattered enough to him that he put it into a self-portrait: in a canvas now in the Musée d'Orsay he paints himself in front of it, the crucifixion reversed as if seen in a mirror, his own face directly beneath the hanging one. He was not a believer in any conventional sense. He was perfectly willing to identify with the man on the cross.

## The World Behind It

Rural Brittany kept a form of Catholicism that felt medieval to visitors from Paris — roadside calvaries at crossroads, processions, pardons, saints with local jurisdictions, and carved Christs in every chapel, worn and repainted by generations. To Gauguin this was evidence of a people who had not been sanded down by modern life, and he chased it the way he would later chase Polynesia.

France itself was going the other way. The Third Republic was in the middle of a hard campaign to prise the church out of public life, and 1889 was the year Paris raised the Eiffel Tower for the Universal Exposition — an iron monument to exactly the industrial future Gauguin had fled. He visited that exposition. He also arranged, with a group of friends, to hang their own paintings at a café on the grounds, unable to get into the official show.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was forty-one and the wreckage was piling up. The nine weeks in Arles had ended eight months earlier with van Gogh's razor; the two men were still writing to each other, carefully. His wife and five children were in Copenhagen and he was not going back. His money came in irregular scraps from a handful of loyal buyers.

Brittany was cheap and it worked on him. He had moved on from Pont-Aven, which he complained was overrun with painters, to the quieter coastal village of Le Pouldu, where he covered the walls and ceiling of a small inn with murals. Around him a group of younger artists had begun treating him as a master, which he did not discourage.

And he was already looking past all of it. Within two years he would sell what he could at auction, take the money, and sail for Tahiti.

## Interesting Facts

- The crucifix is a real object — an anonymous polychrome wood figure still hanging in the chapel of Trémalo near Pont-Aven, which visitors can go and see.
- Gauguin painted himself in front of this picture in *Self-Portrait with the Yellow Christ*, where the crucifixion appears reversed, as though reflected.
- The technique of flat colour locked inside dark outlines was named cloisonnism, after the enamelwork in which metal strips separate each field of colour.
- The three Breton women at the foot of the cross show no grief at all — a deliberate refusal of every convention of crucifixion painting.
- He made a companion picture the same year, *The Green Christ*, based on a stone calvary at Nizon.
- Gauguin was in Paris for the 1889 Universal Exposition and, shut out of the official art show, hung his work with friends in a café on the fairground instead.
