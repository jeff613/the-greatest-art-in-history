## The Story

One evening around 1892, walking a road above Kristiania with two friends, Edvard Munch stopped and felt the world come apart. He wrote it down: "I was walking along the road with two friends — the sun was setting — suddenly the sky turned blood red — I paused, feeling exhausted, and leaned on the fence — there was blood and tongues of fire above the blue-black fjord and the city — my friends walked on, and I stood there trembling with anxiety — and I sensed an infinite scream passing through nature." That last sentence is the whole picture. The gaunt figure on the bridge is not the source of the scream; it is clamping its hands over its ears to shut out a shriek that comes from everywhere at once, the sky, the water, the burning air.

Munch worked the image obsessively, making four versions between 1893 and 1910 in paint and pastel, plus a lithograph. Ours is the first painted one, from 1893, done in thin tempera and crayon on cheap cardboard — the surface a museum can never let out of its sight. The undulating sky bleeds down into a violent orange-red; the water and land twist into the same current, so that the lone figure seems to be dissolving into the landscape's panic. It is perhaps the most reproduced anxious face in history, a pictogram of dread that needs no explanation in any language.

For years people assumed a vandal had scrawled the tiny pencil line in the upper-left corner: "Kan kun være malet af en gal Mand!" — "Could only have been painted by a madman!" In 2021 the National Museum used infrared scanning to settle it. The handwriting is Munch's own. Stung by a critic who called him mentally ill, or perhaps agreeing, he had quietly labelled his own masterpiece the work of a lunatic.

## The World Behind It

Kristiania — today's Oslo — in the 1890s was a small, buttoned-up capital being pried open by a restless avant-garde. Munch belonged to its bohemian fringe, a set of writers and painters who scorned bourgeois propriety and treated modern anxiety as a fit subject for art. Their Europe was the fin de siècle, an age fascinated by nerves, hysteria, hypnotism, and the fragile machinery of the mind, and Symbolist artists across the continent were turning away from what the eye sees toward what the soul feels.

The Scream is the purest thing that impulse ever produced. Some scholars have even tied its lurid sky to a real event: the eruption of Krakatoa in 1883, whose ash tinted sunsets blood-red across the Northern Hemisphere for months. Whether or not Munch saw those skies, he painted a feeling the coming century would recognise instantly — the sense of a self standing exposed and alone under an indifferent, roaring universe.

## The Artist at This Moment

Munch was around thirty and freshly notorious. His father had died in 1889, deepening a lifelong preoccupation with mortality, and he had thrown himself into the Frieze of Life, the great cycle of pictures about love, dread, and death of which The Scream became the emblem. He was living between Kristiania, Paris, and Berlin, drinking hard and painting harder, chronically broke and prone to the attacks of anxiety he would later call the thing that "gnawed at my heart." He had recorded the blood-red-sky walk in his diary the year before, and now he distilled that raw note into an image.

In 1892 the Union of Berlin Artists had invited him to exhibit, then shut the show down after a week amid outrage — a humiliation he turned into a triumph, since the "Munch affair" made his name across Germany. He was a man who converted personal catastrophe into art at industrial scale, and The Scream sits at the exact centre of that project: the moment his private terror became a public symbol.

## Interesting Facts

- The figure is not screaming. It is covering its ears against a scream Munch felt "passing through nature" — the sound, not the source.
- A pencil inscription reading "Could only have been painted by a madman!" was confirmed in 2021 to be Munch's own handwriting, not a vandal's.
- There are four painted and pastel versions plus a lithograph; ours is the 1893 tempera-and-crayon original at the National Museum, Oslo.
- This 1893 version was stolen in 1994 — during the opening of the Lillehammer Winter Olympics — by thieves who left a note: "Thanks for the poor security." It was recovered undamaged that May.
- A different version, the 1910 tempera Scream, was taken in a separate armed robbery from the Munch Museum in 2004 (with Madonna) and recovered in 2006.
- The 1895 pastel version sold at auction in 2012 for nearly $120 million, then one of the highest prices ever paid for a work of art.
