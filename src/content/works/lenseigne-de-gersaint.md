## The Story

Watteau came back from London in the summer of 1720 with nothing to show for the trip but the knowledge that the best physician in England could not help him either. He moved in with Edme-François Gersaint, a young art dealer who kept a shop on the Pont Notre-Dame, one of the Paris bridges still built over with houses. And there, according to Gersaint himself, Watteau asked for work. He wanted to paint a sign for the shop, he said, to warm up his fingers. Gersaint tried to talk him out of it. Watteau did it anyway, in eight days, working only in the mornings because that was all the strength he had.

What he produced was a picture more than three metres wide of the shop it was meant to advertise, with the front wall removed. The paving of the street runs straight into the floorboards of the gallery, so that a customer standing outside would have found the painted room continuing the real one. Inside, the walls are packed to the ceiling with paintings, and the people are barely looking at any of them.

On the left, two men are lowering a portrait of Louis XIV into a packing crate. The king had been dead five years. A woman in shell-pink steps over the threshold on a young man's hand, her back to us, and does not glance at the crate. On the right an elderly couple stoop over a large oval canvas of nudes, one of them bent almost double to get close to it. At the counter beyond them a shop assistant holds something up for two more customers who are, plainly, more interested in each other. A dog lies on the paving in the corner. Everyone in the room is performing connoisseurship and conducting a flirtation at the same time, and Watteau lets you decide which is the cover story.

It hung outside the shop for about a fortnight and was then taken down and sold — the sign that was never really a sign. The collector Claude Glucq bought it, then Watteau's patron Jean de Jullienne. At some point before 1732 somebody cut it in two and squared off its arched top with new painted sections by an unknown hand; it has been in two pieces ever since, and you can see the join running down the middle of the doorway. Frederick the Great bought it in that state in 1748 and it has been in Berlin ever since. It has been compared to *Las Meninas*, and the comparison holds: both are pictures about the business of looking at pictures, painted by men who knew exactly what they were doing.

## The World Behind It

Paris in 1720 was in the middle of a financial catastrophe. The Scottish banker John Law had persuaded the Regency to back a scheme of paper money and colonial trading shares, and for two years the rue Quincampoix had been a scrum of speculators making fortunes on paper. That year the whole structure came apart. Shares collapsed, the paper turned worthless, and Law fled the country in December. The men in Gersaint's shop belong to precisely this world — new money, hungry for luxury, buying pictures as furniture and as proof.

The bridge itself is the point. The Pont Notre-Dame was a street of shops built over the Seine, the retail heart of the city, and Gersaint's boutique was in reality a cramped booth with a back room. Watteau made it a gallery the size of a ballroom. It was, one historian has noted, always more of an advertisement for the painter than for the dealer. The bridge's houses were pulled down in the 1780s and there is no trace of the shop left; the picture is the only record of it, and it is a lie.

## The Artist at This Moment

He had months to live. The tuberculosis that had dogged him since childhood was winning, and the London trip had been a last practical attempt to do something about it. He was thirty-six, unmarried, without a household of his own, staying with a dealer ten years his junior.

The eight mornings are the detail everyone remembers, and they are not bravado. He had spent his first Paris years in a piecework shop on this same bridge, copying Flemish pictures for a daily rate; painting fast was the trade he had actually been trained in. What is strange is what he chose to do with the speed. Instead of another park full of guitars, he painted the room he was standing in, the people who bought his work, and a dead king being boxed up and carried out. Soon after, he left Paris for Nogent-sur-Marne, where he died on 18 July 1721.

## Interesting Facts

- Gersaint's own account is the source for the eight days and the mornings: Watteau asked for the commission himself, saying he wanted to warm his fingers.
- The sign hung on the street for roughly a fortnight before it was taken down and sold to a collector. It never did the job it was painted for.
- The portrait going into the crate on the left is of Louis XIV, dead since 1715 — an old régime being packed away while the customers flirt.
- The painting was cut into two canvases and given a squared-off top by an unknown painter before 1732; the vertical join is still visible down the centre of the composition.
- Frederick the Great of Prussia bought it in 1748, already in two parts, from a Dutch dealer, and hung it at Charlottenburg, where it remains.
- The shop was in reality a small booth on a bridge lined with houses; Watteau enlarged it into a gallery. The bridge's buildings were demolished in the 1780s.
- Scholars still argue about the date: Gersaint's story places it after Watteau's return from London in 1720, while a 2007 study of the rebuilding of the shop front has proposed earlier that year.
