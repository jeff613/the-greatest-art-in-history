## The Story

Fifteen were still alive on the thirteenth day, when a passing brig found them by accident. At least a hundred and forty-six men and one woman had been put onto the raft.

The frigate Méduse ran onto a sandbank off the coast of what is now Mauritania on 2 July 1816, carrying a French expedition out to take possession of Senegal. Her captain, Hugues Duroy de Chaumareys, had scarcely been to sea in twenty years; he held the command because he was a returned royalist émigré and the restored Bourbon monarchy was handing posts to loyal men. There were not enough boats. So the crew lashed a raft together out of the ship's own timbers, crowded the soldiers and the lowest-ranking passengers onto it until the deck rode under water, and ran tow lines to the longboats. A few miles out, the lines were let go.

What happened over the next thirteen days was fighting in the dark, men going over the side, wine drunk to insensibility, and finally the eating of the dead. Two survivors — the ship's surgeon Henri Savigny and the engineer Alexandre Corréard — published the whole account in Paris in 1817, and it became the scandal of the Restoration.

Géricault, twenty-seven and rich enough to please himself, decided to paint it sixteen feet high and twenty-three feet across, with the bodies in the foreground life-size.

He worked like an investigator. He found Savigny and Corréard and questioned them, then enlisted a third survivor, the Méduse's carpenter Lavillette, to help build an accurate scale model of the raft. He took a studio across from the Beaujon hospital so that he could watch the dying, and carried severed arms and heads back with him to paint as they changed colour. In November 1818 he shaved his head — partly, it seems, so that he would be unfit to be seen in society — and stayed in for eight months.

One of the few people he let through the door was the twenty-year-old Eugène Delacroix, who lay down to model for the face-down corpse at the lower left. It "made so tremendous an impression on me," Delacroix wrote afterwards, "that when I came out of the studio I started running like a madman."

The Salon of 1819 hung the picture under the flat title Scène de Naufrage — Shipwreck Scene. Nobody was fooled. The jury gave it a gold medal; the state declined to buy it. Louis XVIII, touring the rooms, told the painter he had just made a shipwreck that was not one for him.

## The World Behind It

France in 1816 was two years into the return of the Bourbons, and the state was being restaffed. Men who had gone into exile during the Revolution came home expecting their old positions and got them; competence was a secondary consideration. Chaumareys was exactly such an appointment, and the Méduse went onto the sandbank because of it.

That is why the wreck could not be contained. It was not a natural disaster but a demonstration, in the plainest terms available, that the restored monarchy was handing men's lives to its friends. Savigny's report to the authorities found its way into the opposition press; he and Corréard then published everything themselves, and Corréard went on to run a Paris publishing house named after the wreck, a known gathering point for liberal agitation.

So by the time Géricault began painting, the raft was already a political object. To put it on a canvas the size of a coronation, with no hero and no rescue in sight, was to take a side, and the Salon audience read it exactly that way — some admiring the picture's nerve, others dismissing the whole thing as a heap of corpses.

## The Artist at This Moment

He had already failed in public once. In 1814 he had shown The Wounded Cuirassier, a beaten soldier walking his horse away from a battle, and the critics were merciless. He gave up painting for a while and enlisted. Then came two years in Italy, filling sketchbooks with Michelangelo and with the riderless horse races of the Roman carnival.

He had gone to Italy partly to escape a woman. Alexandrine-Modeste Caruel was the young wife of his mother's brother, and she was his lover. Distance did not settle it. In August 1818, while he was working out the composition of the raft, she gave birth to his son. The child was registered without his parents' names and sent to a wet-nurse in Normandy; neither of them raised him.

That is the man who shaved his head in November 1818 and locked himself in with the corpses: twenty-seven, humiliated once already, secretly a father, rich enough not to need a commission and stubborn enough not to want one. The Raft was painted for nobody.

## Interesting Facts

- Eugène Delacroix, then twenty and nobody in particular, posed for the face-down body at the lower left. He said the picture struck him so hard that he ran the whole way home.
- The Salon jury gave it a gold medal and no buyer appeared, so Géricault shipped it to London in 1820, where William Bullock exhibited it at the Egyptian Hall in Piccadilly and roughly forty thousand people paid to see it.
- Three real survivors of the raft sat for the painting: Savigny, Corréard, and the ship's carpenter Lavillette, who also helped build Géricault's scale model.
- The figure at the summit waving a scrap of cloth is identified as Jean Charles, a soldier who survived; Géricault used a Haitian model named Joseph, one of the best-known models in Paris, for at least two of the Black figures on the raft.
- Géricault painted with bitumen, which looks rich and velvety when fresh and then contracts and blackens. Large passages of the canvas are darker and flatter today than he made them, and the change cannot be reversed.
- The Louvre bought the picture from his heirs after he died in 1824, aged thirty-two. He never sold it in his lifetime.
