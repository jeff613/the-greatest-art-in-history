## The Story

A woman sits at a café table with a full glass of pale green absinthe before her, her shoulders slumped, her eyes empty, staring at nothing. Beside her a shabby man in a battered hat gazes off the other way, so that the two share a bench but not a word. Between them yawns a stretch of empty marble tabletop, and the whole composition slides off to the left, cropped like a snapshot, leaving the pair stranded in a wedge of cold morning light. It is one of the loneliest pictures in nineteenth-century art — a portrait of two people utterly alone together.

The scene looks like documentary truth, and that was the trouble. Degas set it in the Café de la Nouvelle-Athènes, the real Montmartre haunt where he and his friends actually gathered. But the two figures were not derelicts. They were friends of his, posing. The woman is the actress Ellen Andrée, a familiar model for the Impressionists; the man is the painter and etcher Marcellin Desboutin, a well-known Bohemian of the quarter. Neither was drunk, and by most accounts neither had any special taste for absinthe. They were performing a mood.

The painting first appeared, quietly, at an Impressionist exhibition in 1876 under the neutral title *Dans un café* ("In a Café"). Its real notoriety came seventeen years later, in 1893, when it was shown at the Grafton Gallery in London and rechristened *L'Absinthe*, tying it firmly to the era's terror of the "green fairy." Victorian critics recoiled. The conservative artist Walter Crane branded it "a study of human degradation, male and female," and the novelist George Moore, inventing a whole sordid life story for the woman, cried "what a slut!" — before later admitting the picture was simply a work of art with nothing to do with drink. Others, like the critic D. S. MacColl, defended it as inexhaustible. The uproar said far more about London's anxieties than about anything Degas had painted.

## The World Behind It

By the 1870s absinthe had become the emblematic drink of Paris, cheap, potent, and faintly sinister, blamed for madness, poverty, and moral collapse. The café was the stage on which modern city life played out — a place where strangers sat side by side in public solitude — and Degas, like his friend Manet, was drawn to it as a subject precisely because it was so new, so unheroic, so of-the-moment.

The London scandal of 1893 belonged to a different world. Late-Victorian Britain regarded French café society as a byword for decadence, and a large painting of a woman with absinthe at her elbow struck moralists as both foreign and dangerous. The furious debate in the press — was such a subject even fit to hang? — became a landmark clash between those who wanted art to preach and those who insisted it need only observe. The picture had done nothing but look honestly at two people; that, it turned out, was provocation enough.

## The Artist at This Moment

Degas painted this in the mid-1870s, in the thick of the years when his family's finances had collapsed and he was learning to live as a working artist rather than a gentleman of leisure. He was in his early forties, deep in the café-and-studio life of Montmartre, surrounded by the writers, painters, and models who peopled the Nouvelle-Athènes — the very circle from which he drew his two sitters.

This was the height of his engagement with the Impressionist group, whose exhibitions he helped mount even as he rejected their name and their creed. He had no patience for the idea of capturing a fleeting instant out of doors. Everything here — the daring off-center framing, the empty foreground, the exact slump of a tired body — was constructed with a draftsman's control back in the studio. The result only looks accidental. Degas spent his life making calculation pass for a glance caught by chance.

## Interesting Facts

- The two figures were friends of Degas posing as strangers: the actress Ellen Andrée and the artist Marcellin Desboutin, neither of them the absinthe-soaked wrecks they appear.
- The painting caused so little stir in 1876 that its real scandal waited until an 1893 London showing, where the new title *L'Absinthe* did much of the damage.
- The novelist George Moore imagined a sordid backstory for the woman and exclaimed "what a slut!" — then later recanted, calling the picture purely a work of art.
- The conservative artist Walter Crane dismissed it as "a study of human degradation, male and female."
- It was originally titled simply *Dans un café* ("In a Café") — the absinthe theme was played up only when it reached England.
- The setting, the Café de la Nouvelle-Athènes in Montmartre, was a genuine meeting place for Degas, Manet, and their circle.
