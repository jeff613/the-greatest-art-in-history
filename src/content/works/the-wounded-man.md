## The Story

A man lies propped against the trunk of a tree, eyes shut, head tipped back, a sword resting on the ground beside him and a dark red stain spreading over his heart. It looks like the aftermath of a duel — a young romantic hero mortally wounded, caught in the swoon between life and death. The man is Courbet himself, and for years the picture was admired simply as one of his most brooding, theatrical self-portraits, the artist casting himself as a doomed and beautiful casualty.

Then the X-rays told a different story. Beneath the surface lie two earlier versions of the same canvas, and in them there is no wound and no sword. Instead, Courbet's head rests not against a tree but on the shoulder of a woman, who sits beside him while he sleeps against her — a tender, intimate image of two lovers dozing together in the open air. The woman was almost certainly Virginie Binet, Courbet's companion through the 1840s and the mother of his son. He worked and reworked the painting across roughly a decade, and at some point, after the relationship fell apart and Virginie left him, he painted her out entirely. Where her body had been, he laid in the tree and the sword; over his own chest he added the bloody wound.

So the "wound" is not really from a duel at all. It is the visible scar of a vanished love — a private heartbreak reworked, quite literally, into a picture of a dying man. The transformation makes the canvas one of the most quietly confessional works Courbet ever made. He held onto it, reworking and keeping it by him rather than selling it, and it passed into a public collection only after his death. The lover was erased, but the ache she left was painted right onto his heart for everyone to see.

## The World Behind It

In the 1840s the young Courbet was still steeped in the Romanticism he would later claim to have buried. The wounded or dying hero — pale, solitary, beautiful in his suffering — was a stock Romantic figure, and in the painting's first guise Courbet was drawing on that whole sentimental tradition of lovers and languor. Only gradually did he strip the picture down to something starker and more personal.

The long reworking also reflected the instability of his early Paris years. He spent the 1840s scraping for recognition, submitting to the Salon and being rejected far more often than accepted, living the precarious life of an unestablished artist. A painting he kept in the studio could be revised again and again as his fortunes, his style, and his private life all shifted — which is exactly what happened here, the canvas quietly recording a decade of change beneath its calm surface.

## The Artist at This Moment

When he began the painting around 1844, Courbet was in his mid-twenties, freshly arrived in the capital and full of Romantic self-dramatization; casting himself as a wounded lover on a canvas was entirely in character for the young man who would soon declare himself the proudest artist in France. He was also, at that point, genuinely in love. Virginie Binet shared his life and bore his child, and the original tender image of the two of them asleep belonged to those hopeful years.

By the time he finished reworking it, around 1854, that world had collapsed. Virginie had left, taking their son, and Courbet — now notorious for the scandals of *A Burial at Ornans* and his combative Realism — was a very different man from the dreaming youth who first laid in the composition. Painting out the woman he had loved and replacing her with a sword and a wound was, in its way, the most honest thing in an oeuvre built on honesty. Behind the swaggering public rebel was someone quietly turning grief into art.

## Interesting Facts

- The picture is a self-portrait: the dying "wounded man" is Courbet himself.
- X-ray examination revealed two earlier versions hidden beneath the surface, in which Courbet's head rested on the shoulder of a woman rather than against a tree.
- The woman he painted out was almost certainly Virginie Binet, his companion in the 1840s and the mother of his son.
- What now reads as a duelist's wound was added only after the earlier, tender image of the couple was erased — the sword and bloodstain replaced the vanished lover.
- Courbet reworked the canvas across roughly a decade, from about 1844 to 1854, keeping it in his own possession.
- It entered a public collection only after the artist's death and now hangs in the Musée d'Orsay in Paris.
