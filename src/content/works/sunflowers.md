## The Story

A fistful of sunflowers crammed into a plain earthenware pot, some blazing at full bloom, others already going ragged and seedy — all of it painted in yellow against yellow against yellow, a single golden key struck again and again. On the vase, one word: "Vincent." It looks like the simplest picture in the world, and it is one of the most loved ever made.

Van Gogh painted it in Arles, in the south of France, in a burst of happiness in August 1888. He had rented a small house — the famous Yellow House — and convinced himself that it could become a "studio of the south," a brotherhood of painters working together in the sun. His hero Paul Gauguin had agreed to come, and Vincent, giddy with anticipation, set about decorating the guest bedroom for him. He decided sunflowers were the thing. Working "in the full heat of the sun," he wrote to his brother Theo, at a fever pitch, racing the flowers as they wilted, he turned out a whole series in about a week.

The sunflowers were a language. To Van Gogh yellow meant the sun, the south, warmth, friendship — gratitude made visible. He hung the best of the canvases in Gauguin's room as a welcome, a gift, and a quiet statement of loyalty to the man he wanted as his artistic leader. For a few weeks it worked. Gauguin arrived; the two painted side by side; Gauguin admired the sunflowers so much he later begged Vincent for one. Then it all curdled — the arguments, the breakdown, the razor, the severed ear that December. But the sunflowers were painted before the fall, in pure hope. They are what the dream looked like while Vincent still believed in it.

## The World Behind It

Arles in 1888 was a sun-scorched provincial town in Provence, and to Van Gogh, arriving from grey Paris, it was a revelation — the light, the color, the heat, a landscape that reminded him of the Japanese prints he adored. He came south deliberately, chasing intensity of color that the northern climate could never give him, and for one astonishing year he found it. He painted orchards, harvests, the night café, the starry Rhône, the postman and his family, at a pace few artists have ever matched.

Behind the productivity lay a fragile, lonely man betting everything on an idea. Van Gogh was supported entirely by Theo, his brother and an art dealer in Paris, who sent money and paint and belief. Vincent dreamed of paying that debt back by founding a community of artists in the south, with himself and Gauguin at its heart, sharing costs and canvases. The sunflowers were painted for that dream. Their optimism is real — and so, in retrospect, is their fragility, because the "studio of the south" would last only nine weeks before collapsing in the most notorious breakdown in art history.

## The Artist at This Moment

This was the happiest and most productive stretch of Van Gogh's short career. He was thirty-five, sober with purpose, living cheaply in his little yellow house, and painting as if his life depended on it — which, in a way, it did. The letters from this summer crackle with hope: plans for the studio, excitement about Gauguin, delight in the Provençal light. For once, Vincent seemed to be building something rather than losing it.

It could not hold. Van Gogh's mental health was already precarious, and the strain of Gauguin's arrival in October — two intense, difficult men crammed into a tiny house, arguing "electrically" about art — pushed him over the edge. On 23 December 1888 he cut off part of his own ear. Gauguin fled north and never returned. The dream of the southern studio died with that night, and Vincent, in and out of hospital, would voluntarily enter an asylum a few months later. The sunflowers, though, were finished in the good weeks before — which is why they glow with a confidence the rest of his story would never quite recover.

## Interesting Facts

- Van Gogh painted several versions of the sunflowers in Arles, planning to line Gauguin's bedroom with them; the National Gallery's yellow-background canvas is among the most famous.
- Gauguin later painted a portrait of Van Gogh at work titled "The Painter of Sunflowers" — and afterward asked Vincent to give him one of the canvases as a gift.
- Vincent signed the work simply "Vincent" on the vase, the same way he signed his letters; he felt his surname was mangled by the French and preferred his first name.
- One of the Arles sunflower paintings was destroyed in 1945, burned during an American air raid on the Japanese city of Ashiya in the last months of the Second World War.
- Another version stunned the art world in 1987 when it sold at auction for nearly £25 million, then a record price for any painting — for a picture by a man who sold almost nothing in his lifetime.
