## The Story

A green hill, a river, a bridge, and above them a city stacked up against a sky of black and blue-white cloud that looks about ten seconds from lightning. The grass is an unnatural, electric green, lit as if the storm has ripped a hole somewhere off the canvas. Nothing is happening. Nobody is in it. It is one of the most emotionally charged pictures in European art, and its subject is a town.

This is Toledo, where El Greco had lived for over twenty years, and it is not a record of Toledo. He moved the buildings. The Alcázar, the great royal fortress that in reality sits to the right of the cathedral, has been picked up and set on the other side, and other landmarks have been shifted, compressed, or left out to make the silhouette work. He was not painting the view from anywhere; he was painting the idea of the city, rearranged until it looked the way it felt.

Landscape as a subject in its own right barely existed in Spain in 1600. Painting was for saints, kings, and stories; a picture with no figures and no narrative had almost no place to hang and no obvious buyer. El Greco made only two surviving landscapes in a career of some forty years, and this is one. Nobody knows for certain who it was for.

What makes it uncanny is how thoroughly it skips the next three centuries. There is nothing between this and the storm skies of Romanticism two hundred years later, and its charge of pure feeling projected onto weather and geography reads as almost modern. When Europe rediscovered El Greco around 1900, this was one of the pictures that made painters sit up. It reached the Metropolitan Museum in New York in 1929.

## The World Behind It

Toledo had been the capital of Castile, the meeting point of Christian, Jewish, and Muslim Spain, and the seat of the country's most powerful archbishop. Then in 1561 Philip II moved the court permanently to Madrid, and the city began a long slide. Its silk industry decayed, its population fell, its great houses emptied.

What remained was the church, and an intense, inward, mystical Catholicism. This was the Spain of Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross, of visions and dark nights of the soul, and of a Counter-Reformation that had made spiritual intensity a public duty. El Greco's black sky over a half-emptied imperial city is not a bad picture of that world's mood.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was around sixty, established, prosperous enough, and completely out of step with fashion — which by now he seems to have decided was fine. The court had rejected him twenty years earlier. Toledo's churches and clergy kept him working.

His pictures had been getting stranger for years: figures longer, space less stable, colour further from anything you could see out of a window. He was running a busy workshop with his son Jorge Manuel, and simultaneously suing patrons over payment with a persistence that suggests he thought his own work was being systematically undervalued. He was probably right.

He had about fourteen years to live, and the strangest paintings — the *Laocoön*, the visionary late canvases — were still ahead of him.

## Interesting Facts

- El Greco moved the buildings: the Alcázar fortress appears on the wrong side of the cathedral, and other landmarks are shifted or omitted.
- It is one of only two surviving landscapes by El Greco, from a career of roughly forty years.
- There are no people in it at all — almost unheard of in Spanish painting around 1600.
- Nobody knows who commissioned it, or whether anyone did.
- The green of the hillside is not a colour that appears in the actual landscape around Toledo, which is dry and pale for much of the year.
- The painting entered the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York in 1929, at the height of El Greco's twentieth-century rediscovery.
