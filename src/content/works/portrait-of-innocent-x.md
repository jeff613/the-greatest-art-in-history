## The Story

A shrewd old man in a blaze of red glares out of the canvas, and there is nothing gentle about him. Giovanni Battista Pamphilj — Pope Innocent X — was in his mid-seventies when Velázquez painted him in Rome in 1650, and the painter gave him no flattery whatsoever: the flushed, suspicious face, the tight mouth, the eyes that seem to have just caught you in a lie. He grips a paper, sits upright in his gilded chair, and radiates a wary, worldly intelligence. It is one of the most alarmingly alive portraits ever painted, an entire ruthless personality pinned to the canvas.

The most famous story about it may not be true, and it is too good to leave out. When the pope saw the finished picture, tradition holds, he exclaimed *"Troppo vero!"* — "Too true!" Whether or not those exact words were ever spoken, they capture the shock of the thing: a portrait so honest it made its powerful sitter flinch. Innocent was pleased enough to reward the painter with a gold medal and chain, and the Pamphilj family kept the picture; it still hangs in their Roman palace, the Galleria Doria Pamphilj, to this day.

Velázquez had come to Rome not chiefly to paint but to buy art for the Spanish king. To prove his hand to the skeptical Roman art world, he first painted his own assistant, Juan de Pareja — a portrait so admired it reportedly earned him the papal commission. The result set a standard that haunted painters for centuries. Three hundred years later the Anglo-Irish painter Francis Bacon became obsessed with it, producing a long series of "screaming popes" in which Velázquez's serene, terrifying pontiff dissolves into a howling figure trapped in a cage of paint. Bacon, oddly, refused to go and see the original even when he was in Rome, preferring to work from reproductions — as if the real thing were too much to face.

## The World Behind It

In 1650 Rome was staging a Holy Year, a great jubilee that drew pilgrims from across Catholic Europe, and the papacy was still one of the central powers of the continent. Innocent X sat at the head of it — a canny, faction-ridden court where his formidable sister-in-law Olimpia Maidalchini wielded enormous influence, and where art and architecture were tools of prestige. It was the Rome of Bernini, all marble and spectacle, and a foreign painter who could conquer it carried that triumph home.

Velázquez arrived as the envoy of Spain, Catholicism's mightiest crown, shopping for statues and Old Master paintings to fill Philip IV's palaces. Painting the pope himself was the ultimate coup: proof that the Spanish king's own artist could stand among the greatest in the world's art capital. The portrait's unsparing realism drew on a long Roman and Venetian tradition of penetrating likeness, but pushed it further than almost anyone had dared with a sitter of such rank.

## The Artist at This Moment

This was the high point of Velázquez's second Italian journey, made when he was around fifty and at the full stretch of his powers. Sent by Philip IV in 1649 to acquire art for the crown, he lingered in Rome far longer than the king wished, painting, buying, and — some whispered — enjoying his freedom from the suffocating Madrid court, where he also fathered a child during his stay.

Painting the pope placed him, for a moment, at the very center of European art. He was no longer just the king of Spain's portraitist but a master measured against Titian and Raphael on their own ground. When he returned to Madrid he would take up burdensome court offices and paint less and less; the Innocent X stands as the last great flourish before that long, distinguished slowing-down. It remains, by common consent, one of the finest portraits ever made.

## Interesting Facts

- The pope is traditionally said to have called the portrait *"Troppo vero"* — "too true" — though scholars doubt the tale; either way he rewarded Velázquez richly and kept the picture.
- To announce himself in Rome, Velázquez first painted his enslaved assistant Juan de Pareja; the portrait so impressed the city that it helped win him the papal sitting. (That portrait now hangs in the Metropolitan Museum.)
- Francis Bacon based dozens of paintings on it from 1949 onward, warping the calm pontiff into a screaming figure — yet he deliberately avoided seeing the original in the flesh.
- The painting has never left the Pamphilj family; it still hangs in the Galleria Doria Pamphilj in Rome, near a marble bust of the same pope by Bernini.
- Velázquez granted Juan de Pareja his freedom in Rome in 1650; Pareja went on to become a painter in his own right.
