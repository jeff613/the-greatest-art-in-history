## The Story

On the top of the Butte Montmartre, beside one of the last working windmills in Paris, stood an open-air dance garden called the Moulin de la Galette, named for the little flat cakes its owners sold. On Sunday afternoons the working people of the neighborhood — laundresses, shop girls, clerks, art students — crowded in to dance, drink, and flirt under the trees and the strings of gas globes. Renoir, who was living nearby and scraping by, decided to paint the whole swirling scene, and he set out to do it as an Impressionist should: outdoors, in front of the thing itself.

That was easier said than done. The canvas was enormous, more than five feet wide, and the story handed down by Renoir's friend the writer Georges Rivière is that a little group of them carried it up the hill to the garden and back again over the course of months, painting between rain showers and setting up among the tables. To fill his crowd, Renoir simply used the people around him. The two dancers at the left are the model Margot — Marguerite Legrand — spinning a shy Cuban painter, Pedro Vidal de Solares y Cardenas, through a polka; at the table in the foreground sit Rivière and the painters Franc-Lamy and Norbert Goeneutte, with young women of the quarter leaning in to talk.

What Renoir caught is not a posed picture but a feeling: sunlight falling in coins and blotches through the leaves, faces half-lit and half-shadowed, movement everywhere. When he showed it at the third Impressionist exhibition in 1877, hostile critics sneered that the flickering patches of light on the dancers' clothes looked like grease stains or mildew. Today it hangs in the Musée d'Orsay as one of the best-loved pictures of the whole movement — a poor young man's snapshot of an ordinary happy afternoon, made eternal.

## The World Behind It

In 1876 Montmartre was not yet the tourist legend it would become; it was a scruffy, half-rural hill on the edge of Paris, cheap enough for hungry artists, dotted with vegetable plots and a few surviving windmills. Rents were low, wine was cheaper still, and the neighborhood drew a bohemian crowd of painters, writers, and models. The Moulin de la Galette was the heart of its weekend life, a *guinguette* where the classes mixed and a girl could dance all afternoon for a few coins.

This was the leisure of the young Third Republic. Paris was rebuilding its confidence after the disasters of war and the Commune of 1871, and Renoir belonged to a generation of painters determined to record modern city pleasure — not saints and emperors, but the way real Parisians actually spent a Sunday. The Moulin de la Galette was exactly that subject: the good life, lived out of doors.

## The Artist at This Moment

Renoir was thirty-five and still poor, sharing a studio on the rue Cortot with a garden that opened toward the dance hall. He had thrown in his lot with the Impressionists at their first show two years earlier and was gambling everything on the new painting, even as pictures went unsold and money stayed tight. Friends like Rivière believed in him fiercely and often modeled for nothing, and the Moulin de la Galette was his most ambitious statement yet — a huge, complex, sunlit crowd scene attempted by a man who could barely afford the paint. It was a bold wager on the idea that ordinary weekend pleasure, caught outdoors in flickering light, was worthy of the grandest treatment.

He would make at least two versions of the composition. The large one went eventually to the French state; a smaller variant slipped into private hands and, a century later, would generate one of the great sagas of the art market.

## Interesting Facts

- The dance garden took its name from the *galette*, a small flat cake the proprietors sold; it sat beside one of Montmartre's last surviving windmills.
- By tradition Renoir and his friends hauled the big canvas up the hill and back for months, working on it partly on the spot between rain showers.
- Nearly every figure is a friend or neighbor — the model Margot dancing with a Cuban painter, the writer Georges Rivière seated at the foreground table.
- Critics at the 1877 Impressionist exhibition mocked the dappled sunlight, complaining the light on the clothes looked like grease spots.
- A smaller second version sold at Sotheby's in 1990 for $78.1 million to the Japanese businessman Ryoei Saito — then among the highest prices ever paid for a painting.
- Saito caused an international outcry by musing that he might have this Renoir and a Van Gogh cremated with him; he later called it a joke, and after his finances collapsed the picture was quietly sold on.
