## The Story

A row of Breton women in stiff white bonnets stand with their eyes shut and their hands folded. They have just come out of Mass, where the priest preached on Jacob wrestling the angel through the night at the ford of the Jabbok. And in front of them, in the upper right of the canvas, the fight is actually happening — a small figure in blue locked against a figure with orange wings — on a ground of flat, roaring vermilion.

Gauguin painted it in Pont-Aven in the summer of 1888. What made it startling was not the subject but the decision underneath it: he had stopped painting what the eye reports. The ground is red because the vision is red, not because any field in Brittany is. A tree branch cuts diagonally across the whole picture and a cow wanders through the top left at the wrong scale entirely, devices lifted from the Japanese prints then circulating in Paris. The women's headdresses are drawn as bold white shapes with dark outlines, closer to stained glass or cloisonné enamel than to anything in the Impressionist repertoire he had grown up in. He and the younger painter Émile Bernard were working side by side that summer, pushing each other toward this flattened, outlined manner; both later claimed to have got there first, and the argument helped end the friendship.

Gauguin thought he had made something religious, and tried to give it away. He offered the canvas to the parish church at Nizon, a short walk from Pont-Aven, imagining it hung above the altar among the local congregation it depicted. The curé looked at the red field and the wrestling angel and declined. By one account he tried a second church and was refused there too. The painting he could not donate is now among the most important pictures of the nineteenth century, and one of the treasures of Scotland's national collection.

## The World Behind It

Brittany in the 1880s was the closest thing to a foreign country that a broke Parisian painter could reach by train. It had its own language, its own saints and pardons, women in regional costume, and a reputation among artists for being centuries behind the rest of France — which was precisely the appeal. Pont-Aven had a cheap inn, a landlady willing to extend credit to painters, and by that summer a whole colony of them.

Gauguin came looking for what he called something primitive and got a version of it: a devout rural Catholicism where a sermon could plausibly produce a vision. The irony he did not much dwell on is that Brittany was already a tourist destination, its costumes half-performed for visitors, its authenticity partly a product being sold to men like him. It was the first of his paradises, and he would spend the rest of his life looking for the next one further away.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was forty, three years past walking out on his family, and living on almost nothing. The stockbroker's income was long gone; Martinique the previous year had given him dysentery and a handful of canvases. He was borrowing against future work and eating on credit at Marie-Jeanne Gloanec's inn.

But this was the summer he stopped being a talented follower of Pissarro and became Gauguin. He wrote to friends with the confidence of a man who knows he has crossed a line, telling Vincent van Gogh's brother Theo about the picture and describing the women and the sheer unnatural red without apology.

Two months after finishing it he took the train south to Arles to live with Vincent van Gogh in the Yellow House. That experiment lasted nine weeks and ended two days before Christmas with a razor, a severed ear, and Gauguin on the first train back to Paris.

## Interesting Facts

- The red ground has no source in nature — Gauguin painted the women's vision rather than the field they are standing in, one of the first steps toward abstraction in European art.
- He offered the picture free to the village church at Nizon; the priest turned it down, reportedly appalled by it.
- The cow strolling through the upper left is wildly out of scale, borrowed from the compositional logic of Japanese woodblock prints.
- Gauguin and Émile Bernard both later claimed credit for inventing the flat, outlined style the picture launched; the dispute soured their friendship permanently.
- The canvas is signed and dated in the lower left with the year Gauguin considered his real beginning as an artist.
- Nine weeks after this painting, Gauguin was in Arles with van Gogh — the visit that ended with van Gogh cutting off his own ear.
