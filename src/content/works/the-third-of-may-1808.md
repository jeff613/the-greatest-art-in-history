## The Story

On 2 May 1808 the people of Madrid rose against the French army that had occupied their city, attacking Napoleon's troops in the streets with knives and fists. The reprisal came the next day. Through the night and morning of 3 May, French firing squads rounded up hundreds of Spaniards — many of them ordinary citizens, some entirely innocent — and shot them in batches on the hills outside the city. It was mass execution as policy, cold and efficient.

Goya painted the scene six years later, in 1814, once the French had finally been driven out and the Spanish crown restored. He petitioned the provisional government for the chance to commemorate, in his words, "the most notable and heroic actions of our glorious insurrection against the tyrant of Europe," and produced a pair of huge canvases — one of the uprising, and this, of the punishment.

What he made broke every rule of how battle had been painted. There is no hero, no general on a horse, no glory. The killing is done by a faceless rank of soldiers, bent like a single machine over their rifles, their expressions hidden. The victims are anonymous laborers, and Goya throws all his light onto one of them: a man in a brilliant white shirt, kneeling in a spreading pool of blood, arms flung wide. Look closely and his right hand carries a dark mark, an echo of the wound in Christ's palm — this nobody is the painting's martyr and its sun. Around him others cover their eyes, clench their fists, or already lie dead in the dirt; behind, a shadowy town sleeps under a black sky.

The picture was radical and, for decades, largely unseen. It seems to have hung out of the way and drawn little notice through much of the nineteenth century. But its charge never faded. Manet borrowed its firing squad for his Execution of Maximilian; Picasso reworked it in Massacre in Korea. Goya had invented the modern image of atrocity: war not as triumph, but as murder.

## The World Behind It

Spain in these years was a country coming apart. Napoleon had lured the Spanish royal family into abdication and installed his own brother, Joseph Bonaparte, on the throne, expecting an easy conquest of a supposedly backward nation. Instead he got the Peninsular War — six years of ferocious guerrilla resistance, siege, famine, and reprisal that bled the French empire and gave the very word "guerrilla," little war, to the world.

Goya lived through all of it inside occupied Madrid, a court painter watching his city starve and burn. He served whoever ruled — he painted for the French-backed regime and then for the restored Spanish king — a survivor's flexibility that later left his own loyalties under suspicion. But privately, in etchings no patron ever commissioned, he was recording the war's true face: torture, rape, mutilation, and heaps of the dead. The Third of May is that private horror made monumental and public, the deaf old painter's verdict on what nations do to one another.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1814 Goya was sixty-eight and had been completely deaf for more than twenty years. He was still First Court Painter in title, but the man who had once designed sunlit tapestries of picnics was long gone. Two decades of silence, illness, and now war had turned him into something darker and more solitary — an artist increasingly working for himself.

He had already poured the war into The Disasters of War, prints of such unflinching brutality that they could not be published in his lifetime. The commission for the Second and Third of May gave him, at last, a vast public surface for the same fury. Whether he personally witnessed the executions on the hill of Príncipe Pío is unknown and much debated; what is certain is that he understood the event in his bones. Within five years he would abandon public commissions almost entirely, retreat to a house on the edge of Madrid, and begin painting nightmares directly onto its walls.

## Interesting Facts

- The Third of May has a companion, The Second of May 1808, showing the street uprising the day before — the crime and the punishment, painted as a pair.
- Goya threw all the light onto the central victim's white shirt, and marked his outflung right hand with a dark spot echoing the stigmata of Christ, turning an anonymous laborer into a martyr.
- The executioners are shown only from behind — a faceless, mechanical rank, denied any individual humanity.
- Both paintings were damaged when they were evacuated from Madrid during the Spanish Civil War in the 1930s; the Third of May still bears losses near the lower left.
- Édouard Manet and Pablo Picasso both built famous paintings directly on Goya's composition, making it the ancestor of modern protest art.
