## The Story

On 13 July 1793 a young woman from Normandy named Charlotte Corday talked her way into the Paris home of Jean-Paul Marat, one of the most feared voices of the Revolution, and drove a kitchen knife into his chest as he sat in his bath. Marat suffered from a savage skin disease — probably contracted years earlier while hiding in the sewers of Paris — that forced him to spend hours soaking in medicated water, a board laid across the tub so he could keep writing. Corday, a supporter of the moderate Girondins, held him responsible for the massacres tearing the country apart. She had gained entry with a note promising to name traitors, and she went calmly to the guillotine four days later.

Jacques-Louis David got the commission almost at once. He was not only the Revolution's leading painter but Marat's friend and fellow deputy in the National Convention — and, by his own account, he had visited Marat in that very bathroom the day before, finding him at his board, writing. The Convention wanted a martyr, and David gave them one. He worked for months and delivered a picture of shocking economy: the dead man slumped like a fallen Christ, the wound beneath his collarbone, the knife on the floor, the sheet stained with blood.

Everything anecdotal is stripped away. There is no assassin, no struggle, no crowd — only Marat, the letter from Corday still in his hand, a wooden packing crate serving as his desk, and a great empty darkness above him where a religious painter would have put heaven. On the crate David painted a simple dedication: "À MARAT, DAVID." He was not signing a report of a crime. He was consecrating a saint.

The painting was unveiled to the Convention that autumn and hung in its hall as revolutionary scripture. But the cult of Marat curdled fast. Within two years Robespierre had fallen, the Terror was over, and the picture became an embarrassment; David quietly took it back. It vanished for decades, hidden by his family and pupils, too dangerous to show. Only in the nineteenth century did it resurface, and it finally came to rest in Brussels — the city where its disgraced, exiled maker died — where it hangs today as one of the most powerful political images ever painted.

## The World Behind It

In the summer of 1793 the French Revolution was at its most extreme and most terrified. The king had been guillotined in January, France was at war with half of Europe, and in Paris the radical Jacobins were tightening their grip in what would become the Terror. Marat, with his inflammatory newspaper, had been one of the loudest voices demanding blood to save the Republic, which made him a hero to the Paris streets and a monster to his enemies.

His murder handed the Jacobins a gift: a martyr. The Revolution had torn down the saints and kings of the old order and needed sacred images of its own, and David was its stage-manager, designing festivals, funerals, and emblems for the reborn nation. The Death of Marat was propaganda in the most literal sense — a painting meant to move a grieving, frightened public and bind them to the cause. It borrowed the visual language of Christian art, the pietà and the entombment, and poured it into a secular vessel. That is exactly why it outlived the politics that produced it: strip away the name, and what remains is a timeless image of a human being killed for what he believed.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1793 David was at the dizzying summit of his power. Elected to the National Convention, he had voted with the majority to send Louis XVI to the guillotine, aligning himself completely with Robespierre and the radical faction. He ran the artistic life of the Republic almost single-handedly, and for a time sat on the feared Committee of General Security, signing off on arrests — the painter as functionary of the Terror.

Marat was his friend, and the commission was personal as well as political. David threw himself into it as an act of devotion. But he was standing on a trapdoor. When Robespierre fell in July 1794 and the Terror collapsed, David's turn came: he was denounced, arrested, and imprisoned, and only narrowly escaped the fate he had helped mete out to others. The Death of Marat, painted at the height of his zeal, would soon become a relic he had to hide. It marks the exact moment before his fall — the artist as true believer, before he learned to survive by trimming his sails to whoever held power next.

## Interesting Facts

- Marat worked and died in his bathtub because of a disfiguring skin disease he may have caught while hiding in the Paris sewers during earlier years on the run.
- David claimed he had visited Marat the day before the murder and found him exactly as he painted him — at a board laid across the tub, still writing.
- The note in Marat's hand is not a real quotation from Charlotte Corday but words David invented, casting her as a cold deceiver who abused Marat's kindness.
- David deliberately echoed Christian art — the drooping arm recalls depictions of the dead Christ — to turn a political killing into a secular martyrdom.
- After Robespierre's fall the painting became so politically toxic that it was hidden away for decades and largely forgotten until the mid-1800s.
- The original hangs in Brussels; a later version in the Louvre replaces David's dedication with the line "Unable to corrupt me, they assassinated me."
