## The Story

At first glance it is a tender Nativity: the Virgin kneels in adoration over the newborn Christ, Joseph slumps nearby, the ox and ass look on, and shepherds and wise men approach a thatched stable. But look up, and the picture cracks open into something far stranger. A ring of twelve angels wheels through a golden opening in the heavens, dancing hand in hand with olive branches and crowns; a line of Greek runs across the very top; and at the bottom small angels embrace men while horned devils flee into cracks in the earth. This is no ordinary Christmas scene. It is a vision of the end of the world.

The Greek inscription is the key, and it is extraordinary. In it the painter names himself and dates the work to the end of 1500, "in the troubles of Italy," and declares that he has painted the scene according to the Book of Revelation — during the loosing of the devil "for three and a half years," after which the devil will be chained and cast down "as in this picture." Botticelli, in other words, believed he might be living in the last days, and he painted the Nativity as a prophecy of Christ's imminent return and the defeat of Satan.

That apocalyptic dread had a name in Florence: Savonarola. The fiery friar who had ruled the city and preached that its sins would bring down God's wrath had been burned as a heretic in 1498, but his warnings lingered, and Botticelli, by many accounts, was among those they marked for life. The "troubles of Italy" were real enough — foreign invasions, the fall of the Medici, plague and upheaval — and the ageing painter answered them not with the pagan grace of his youth but with this urgent, hopeful, frightening image of a world about to be made new.

It is also, uniquely, a signed picture. Of all his surviving works, this is the only one Botticelli signed and dated — as if, at the close of the year 1500, he wanted to put his name to a personal act of faith.

## The World Behind It

Florence in 1500 was a shaken city. The golden age of Lorenzo de' Medici had ended with his death in 1492; two years later the Medici were expelled and the French armies of Charles VIII swept through Italy, beginning decades of war on the peninsula. Into that vacuum had stepped Girolamo Savonarola, the Dominican friar whose apocalyptic sermons and "bonfires of the vanities" briefly turned Florence into a puritan republic before he was excommunicated, tortured, and burned in the public square in 1498.

Many Florentines carried his prophecies with them long after his death, half-expecting the judgement he had foretold. Botticelli's brother Simone was a devoted follower of the friar, and the painter himself seems to have absorbed that grave, penitential mood. The Mystical Nativity belongs to a Europe half-convinced it might be living at the hinge of history — the year 1500 itself, a great jubilee year in Rome, was heavy with millennial expectation. The painting is Botticelli's contribution to that fevered hope: not a record of the past but a prayer about what he feared, and hoped, was coming.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1500 Botticelli was in his mid-fifties, and the world that had made him had vanished. The Medici who had commissioned his dancing Venuses were gone; the philosophers of their circle were scattered or dead; and fashion in Florence had turned toward the young Leonardo and Michelangelo. The painter of the Birth of Venus was sliding out of favour, and his art had changed with his fortunes, growing harder, more anxious, and more intensely devout.

The Mystical Nativity is the fullest surviving statement of that late Botticelli. Gone is the cool, weightless paganism; in its place is raw religious feeling, expressed through deliberately old-fashioned devices — figures sized by their importance, a golden heaven, hand-lettered scrolls — that hark back to an older, more pious kind of painting. It is the work of a man who had lived through his city's collapse and staked everything on faith. He signed it, dated it, and let it speak; within a decade he was dead and largely forgotten, not to be rediscovered for centuries.

## Interesting Facts

- It is the only surviving painting Botticelli signed and dated — with a Greek inscription across the top naming himself and the year 1500.
- That inscription describes the picture as a vision from the Book of Revelation, painted "in the troubles of Italy" during the loosing of the devil, whom Christ will soon chain and cast down.
- The scene fuses a traditional Nativity below with an apocalyptic heaven above, where twelve angels dance in a ring and, at the bottom, angels embrace men while devils flee into the ground.
- It reflects the lingering influence of the friar Savonarola, burned in Florence in 1498, whose apocalyptic preaching had gripped the city — and whose followers included Botticelli's own brother.
- Unusually for a major Florentine work, it is painted on canvas rather than on a wooden panel.
- Forgotten for centuries, it was bought by the National Gallery in London in 1878 and helped drive the modern revival of Botticelli's reputation.
