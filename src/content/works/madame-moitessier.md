## The Story

Ingres did not want to paint her. He was in his sixties, he considered portraiture a lesser art than history painting, and he had spent his career resenting the private commissions that paid for the grand mythological pictures nobody bought. When the banker Sigisbert Moitessier approached him in 1844 about a portrait of his wife, Ingres said no.

Then he met her. Marie-Clotilde-Inès de Foucauld was twenty-three, dark, imposing, and — by every account of the encounter — so striking that the old man reversed himself on the spot and took the job. It became one of the longest and most tormented commissions of his life.

Twelve years. He started with the sitter's small daughter Catherine in the composition, found the child impossible to work with, and painted her out around 1847. Work stopped altogether in 1849 when his wife Madeleine died and Ingres, wrecked, could not paint at all. When he came back to it, Madame Moitessier reportedly nudged him about the unfinished picture in terms affectionate enough — *la belle et bonne* — that he responded by producing an entirely different portrait, standing, in black, and delivering that one in 1851 while the original seated version continued to defeat him. He finally finished the seated one in 1856, twelve years after saying yes.

The standing portrait is the one here. She stands in a room of crimson damask in a black gown weighted with lace, her arms bare, a fan folded in one hand, gold and jewels at her wrists and throat — and a face that gives away absolutely nothing. Ingres has painted her as a monument. There is no warmth being offered to the viewer and no anecdote in sight; the picture's argument is that this woman is a fact you have to deal with. The flowers in her hair are the only things in it that are not perfectly still.

Both portraits now hang in national collections on opposite sides of the Atlantic — the standing one in Washington, the seated one in London.

## The World Behind It

This is the France of the Second Empire in the making — Louis-Napoleon's coup came in December 1851 — and of a banking class with more money than the old aristocracy and a keen sense of what it was owed. The Moitessiers were exactly that: new commercial wealth buying the most prestigious brush in France.

Ingres was by now an institution, loaded with honours, running the official taste of the country against Delacroix and the Romantics. A portrait by him was a form of certification. What his sitters got in exchange was a picture in which their clothes, jewels, and furniture are rendered with hallucinatory precision and their inner life is politely declined — which was arguably what they wanted.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was seventy-one when he delivered this canvas, and the previous two years had been the worst of his life. Madeleine Chapelle — the wife he had married in 1813 without ever having met her, after a courtship by correspondence — died in 1849 after thirty-six years. He stopped working entirely.

The recovery, when it came, was abrupt. In 1852, the year after finishing this portrait, he married Delphine Ramel, a relative of a close friend; he was seventy-one and she was forty-three, and he described the effect on himself as rejuvenating. He had another fifteen years and some of his strangest work still in front of him.

He also, characteristically, never stopped resenting portraiture even while producing the greatest portraits of the century in it.

## Interesting Facts

- Ingres turned the commission down and only accepted after meeting the sitter.
- It took twelve years from commission to the finished seated portrait — and this standing version was painted in the middle of it, essentially as an apology for the delay.
- The sitter's young daughter was originally in the composition; Ingres found her impossible to paint and removed her.
- Work halted completely in 1849 when Ingres's wife died and he was unable to paint.
- There are two Madame Moitessiers: this standing one of 1851 in Washington, and the seated one of 1856 in the National Gallery, London.
- Ingres regarded portrait painting as beneath him for his entire career, and made much of his living from it.
