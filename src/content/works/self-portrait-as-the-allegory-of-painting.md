## The Story

She is leaning into it. The right arm is thrown up and forward with the brush at the top of its reach, the left hand rests on a stone slab holding the palette and a fistful of brushes, and her whole body is pitched off the vertical, twisting away from us towards a canvas we cannot see. Her hair has come loose. Her sleeve is shoved back. She is not posing at all — she is caught in the middle of a stroke, and she is not going to stop to look at you.

The joke, if that is the word, is in the gold chain. In 1593 — the year Artemisia was born — an Italian scholar named Cesare Ripa published the *Iconologia*, a handbook telling artists exactly how to personify abstract nouns, and it became the standard reference of the century. Ripa prescribed Painting as a beautiful woman with dark hair, dishevelled and twisted into knots, with arched brows that show imagination, a gold chain at her throat from which hangs a mask, and — crucially — her mouth bound with a cloth, because painting is an art that cannot speak.

Artemisia gives herself the hair, the arched brow, the chain, the mask. She leaves off the gag.

And she does the thing no man could. Painting was female, as all the personified arts and virtues were female; a male artist could paint an allegory of Painting, or he could paint a self-portrait, but he could never be both at once. As the historian Mary Garrard put it, that particular fusion was structurally available only to a woman, and no woman before Artemisia was in a position to take it.

She made it in London. Her father Orazio had been court painter to Charles I since 1626, and in 1638 Artemisia — by then one of the best-known painters in Europe — came to join him, probably at the king's invitation. Orazio died the following February. This canvas went into Charles's collection, and it is the only firmly attributed work of hers to survive from the London years.

Then the king lost his head. In 1649 Parliament put the royal collection up for sale to pay the dead king's creditors, and in October 1651 the picture went out of the palace to a lawyer named John Jackson. After the Restoration a Colonel William Hawley was commissioned to track down what had been scattered, and in 1660 it came back. It has been in the Royal Collection ever since.

## The World Behind It

Charles I had not called a Parliament in nine years and was spending money he did not have on the finest art collection in Europe — the Gonzaga pictures bought wholesale out of Mantua, Van Dyck installed as court painter, agents scouring Italy. Bringing Orazio Gentileschi from Paris in 1626 and then his celebrated daughter in 1638 was part of the same project: a Stuart court that could hold its own against Madrid and Rome.

The bill was already coming due. The Scots had risen against the king's prayer book in 1637; the Bishops' Wars followed; by 1642 England was at war with itself, and Artemisia had left. In January 1649 Charles was beheaded on a scaffold outside his own Banqueting House, walking out to it through a hall roofed by a ceiling he had commissioned from Rubens. Within months Parliament had voted to sell the pictures he spent his reign collecting, and the greatest art collection in Europe was broken up into lots and carried out of the palaces, this canvas among them.

## The Artist at This Moment

She was forty-five and, by any measure that mattered in the seventeenth century, she had won. Since 1630 she had run her own workshop in Naples with assistants under her, taking altarpieces and cathedral commissions and turning out pictures for collectors from Sicily to Spain. Her name travelled; the invitations came to her.

The private ledger was thinner. Her husband had been gone for fifteen years. Four of her five children had died young. In London she buried her father, the man who had taught her, sold her, prosecuted her case and set the terms of most of her life.

She went back to Naples and worked another fifteen years there. In 1649, arguing a price with a Sicilian collector called Don Antonio Ruffo, she wrote him a line that has outlasted most of the paintings: you will not lose with me, and you will find the spirit of Caesar in the soul of a woman.

## Interesting Facts

- Cesare Ripa's handbook required the figure of Painting to be gagged with a cloth, on the grounds that painting is mute. Artemisia kept every other attribute and dropped that one.
- It is the only work firmly attributed to her that survives from her years in England.
- Charles I's collection was broken up and sold after his execution; this picture left the royal palaces in October 1651 and was recovered for Charles II after 1660.
- Since 2015 it has hung in the Cumberland Art Gallery at Hampton Court Palace.
- Michael Levey proposed in the twentieth century that the allegorical figure is Artemisia herself; some historians still question whether the face matches her other likenesses.
- Her father Orazio died in London in February 1639, months after she arrived, and was buried in the Queen's Chapel at Somerset House.
- She is thought to have worked with him on the ceiling canvases for the Queen's House at Greenwich, his last great commission.
