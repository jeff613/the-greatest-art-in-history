## The Story

This is Vermeer's largest, most ambitious, and most personal painting — the one he seems to have made not to sell but to declare what he believed his art could be. A heavy tapestry curtain is drawn back, as if we have been let in to spy on a scene, and inside a sunlit studio a painter sits at his easel with his back to us, brush poised, beginning to work. His model stands by the window in blue, crowned with laurel, holding a trumpet and a heavy book, her eyes lowered.

She is not just a girl in costume. She is Clio, the muse of History, dressed with the exact attributes prescribed in a famous handbook of symbols of the age: the laurel of honor and glory, the trumpet of fame, the book of the historical record. What the painter is shown starting to paint, on his fresh canvas, is her laurel wreath — so the picture depicts the art of painting reaching for lasting fame and the recording of great deeds. Behind the figures hangs a large map of the old Netherlands, the seventeen provinces as they were before war split them into the Protestant north and the Spanish-held south, its creased surface glowing in the light. The whole room is an allegory: painting itself, seated in a Dutch studio, in service to history and glory.

The painter at the easel is turned away, his face hidden, dressed in an elegant slashed black doublet and red stockings — and he is almost certainly Vermeer himself, showing us his own back as he works. It is the closest thing we have to a self-portrait by an artist who left no confirmed image of his face. Vermeer lavished everything he had on the picture: the shimmering chandelier, the folds of the tapestry, the pattern of light and shadow on the map, the tiny bright touches everywhere. It is a summation, a manifesto in paint about the dignity and permanence of his craft.

## The World Behind It

Vermeer painted this in the 1660s, in a Dutch Republic that had won its long war of independence from Spain and was enjoying a golden age of trade, science, and art. Maps like the one on the studio wall were objects of pride and beauty, hung in prosperous homes as emblems of a seafaring nation that measured and mastered the world. The map here, showing the whole of the Netherlands still undivided, carries a note of nostalgia and politics — a memory of a unity the war had broken.

The elaborate symbolism drew on a widely used dictionary of allegory that told artists exactly how to personify abstract ideas like History or Fame. Educated viewers could read such pictures the way we read a coat of arms. But this was also a supremely commercial art world, where painters lived or died by sales, which makes Vermeer's refusal to part with this masterpiece all the more telling. He kept it in his own home for the rest of his life. It was the picture that stated his highest ambitions, and he would not let it go, even as his finances slid toward disaster.

## The Artist at This Moment

Vermeer was in his mid-thirties, a respected figure within Delft — twice elected a head of the painters' guild — but almost unknown beyond it, working with painful slowness and supporting a large family on shaky ground. The Art of Painting represents the peak of his powers and his self-image: this is how he wanted painting, and himself, to be understood, as a noble calling worthy of the muse of history.

He held onto it fiercely. When he died in 1675, deep in debt after the French invasion wrecked the Dutch economy and his own livelihood, his widow Catharina fought to keep this one canvas out of the hands of creditors, transferring it to her mother in a desperate attempt to save it from being sold off with the rest. It was in vain, and the picture eventually left the family. Its later journey is extraordinary: it passed through aristocratic collections and, in 1940, was bought by Adolf Hitler for his planned museum in Linz. Recovered after the Second World War, it now hangs in Vienna — the grandest surviving statement by a painter who died poor and forgotten, insisting to the end on the greatness of what he did.

## Interesting Facts

- The model is dressed as Clio, muse of History, with the laurel wreath, trumpet, and book prescribed by a well-known handbook of symbols — making the whole picture an allegory of painting itself.
- The painter seen from behind is thought to be Vermeer himself; it is the nearest thing to a self-portrait by an artist who left no confirmed likeness of his face.
- The large wall map shows the Netherlands still undivided into its northern and southern halves — a politically charged image of a unity the war of independence had ended.
- Vermeer never sold it. He kept it in his home all his life, and after his death his widow tried to shield it from creditors by transferring it to her mother.
- Adolf Hitler purchased the painting in 1940 for his intended Führermuseum in Linz; it was recovered by the Allies after the war.
- It is one of Vermeer's largest works and is regarded as his most complex and self-conscious statement about the value and permanence of his art.
