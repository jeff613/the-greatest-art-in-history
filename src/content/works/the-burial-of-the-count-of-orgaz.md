## The Story

The story the painting tells is two and a half centuries old at the time of painting. In 1323 Don Gonzalo Ruiz de Toledo, lord of the town of Orgaz, a man remembered for his generosity to the church, was carried to his grave in the parish of Santo Tomé — and according to the legend that grew up around him, Saint Stephen and Saint Augustine appeared in person, in gold and jewelled vestments, and lowered his body into the tomb with their own hands.

El Greco was given the commission in the spring of 1586 by Andrés Núñez, the parish priest, as the last stage of doing up the count's burial chapel, and he painted it directly onto the wall it still hangs on. He divided the picture in half. Below is the funeral: the dead knight in polished black armour that catches all the light in the room, the two saints bending to take his weight, and behind them a long rank of Toledo gentlemen in black with white ruffs — real, identifiable, contemporary faces, the men who ran the city, come to a burial that happened before any of them were born. Above, the sky splits open into a heaven of swirling cloud and elongated figures, Christ at the apex, the Virgin and John the Baptist interceding, and an angel carrying the count's soul upward through a narrow gap like a baby being delivered.

It is a picture that puts a miracle in a room full of people you could have met on the street. El Greco painted his own eight-year-old son, Jorge Manuel, as the boy at the lower left who points at the scene and looks straight out at us, and signed the picture on the handkerchief in the boy's pocket, dating it 1578 — the year the child was born. Saint Augustine wears the face of the archbishop of Toledo. A man above a raised hand at the back is generally taken to be El Greco himself.

Then came the part that was entirely in character. The contract said the price would be set by expert appraisal. The appraisers valued it high; the parish disagreed; the argument went on, and El Greco eventually took the lower figure of 13,200 reales. He would spend much of the rest of his life in court over what his work was worth.

## The World Behind It

Toledo in the 1580s was a city with a magnificent past and a shrinking future. It had been the capital of Castile, and it was still the seat of Spain's primate archbishop and stuffed with churches, convents, and clerical money — but Philip II had moved the court to Madrid in 1561, and the trade was going with it.

This was also Counter-Reformation Spain at full pressure. The Council of Trent had ruled on what religious art was for: it was to teach, to move, and to leave no doubt about doctrine, and it explicitly defended the veneration of saints and the value of good works that Protestants had attacked. A painting in which two saints physically descend to honour a man for his charity is, among other things, a piece of argument. The rows of Toledo notables lined up as witnesses make it a local one.

## The Artist at This Moment

El Greco was forty-five and had settled for Toledo. The hoped-for court career had died with Philip II's dislike of the *Martyrdom of St Maurice* a few years earlier; there would be no Escorial, no royal appointment.

What he had instead was a city that liked him. He was working for its churches and its intellectuals, renting grand rooms, and had built a manner nobody could imitate. This commission was the one that fixed his reputation locally and, four hundred years later, is the reason people go to Santo Tomé at all.

He was also living with Jerónima de las Cuevas, whom he never married, and raising the boy he painted into the foreground and used as his signature.

## Interesting Facts

- The painting still hangs in the church it was made for, on the wall El Greco painted it for, in Toledo.
- The boy at the lower left is the artist's son, Jorge Manuel; El Greco signed the work on the handkerchief in the boy's pocket and dated it 1578, the year the child was born.
- Saint Augustine is given the features of Gaspar de Quiroga y Vela, the archbishop of Toledo.
- The row of mourners is a group portrait of real Toledo notables attending a funeral that took place in 1323, more than 250 years earlier.
- The angel carries the count's soul up through a narrow opening in the clouds, a passage often described as a birth.
- El Greco disputed the fee, the matter went to appraisal, and he settled for the lower valuation of 13,200 reales.
