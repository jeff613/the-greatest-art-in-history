## The Story

Two stacks of wheat sit in a field outside Giverny, one large and one small, throwing long violet shadows across stubble that glows pink and gold in the low light of late summer. Behind them a line of trees, a few village roofs, and a band of hills catch the last warmth of the day. It looks like the simplest subject imaginable — farmers' grain, drying in a field anyone could walk past — and that was exactly the point. Monet wanted to prove that the humblest thing on earth, painted at the right moment, could hold as much beauty and drama as any cathedral or coronation.

These were not haystacks but stacks of harvested wheat, built to shed rain and left standing in the fields near his home until they could be threshed. Monet had noticed how completely they changed with the hour and the season — blazing at sunset, cold and blue in frost, softened in mist — and he set out to capture not the stacks themselves but the light on them, across a whole cycle of times and weathers. He worked outdoors with several canvases going at once, moving from one to the next as the sun dropped and the color shifted, sometimes managing only a few minutes on each before the effect vanished.

The result was the first true series of his career: some twenty-five canvases of the same two stacks. In May 1891 he hung fifteen of them together at Durand-Ruel's gallery in Paris, and the show was a sensation. Nearly all sold within days, several to buyers who competed for them, at prices that finally lifted Monet clear of decades of money worry. Critics grasped that something new had happened: the subject barely mattered any more, and the painting had become almost purely about perception — color, light, the passing instant. It was the breakthrough that turned the mocked radical of 1874 into the most sought-after painter in France.

## The World Behind It

The stacks stood in the fields around Giverny, the Norman village where Monet had rented, then bought, a farmhouse in the 1880s. This was still deeply rural France, where grain was cut by hand and stacked to dry much as it had been for centuries — but Monet was painting it at the very moment that world was beginning to vanish, as railways, machines, and the pull of the cities transformed the countryside. There is a quiet elegy in these glowing ricks of wheat.

The series' reach turned out to be astonishing. In 1896 a thirty-year-old Russian named Wassily Kandinsky saw one of Monet's stacks at an exhibition in Moscow and was thunderstruck. He could not, at first, even tell what it showed — "that it was a haystack the catalogue informed me," he wrote later; "I could not recognize it." The picture had gripped him without his being able to name its subject, and it planted a revolutionary thought: that color and form might carry all the power of a painting on their own, with no recognizable object at all. Kandinsky went on to become a pioneer of pure abstraction, and he traced the first spark back to a Monet stack of wheat he could not read.

## The Artist at This Moment

Monet painted the stacks in 1890 and 1891, at the hinge of his fortunes. He was fifty, at last able to buy the Giverny house he had only been renting, and just emerging from the poorest, most grief-stricken stretch of his life — the years around the death of his first wife, Camille, and of relentless debt. The stacks in the fields were, quite literally, the view from his own doorstep.

They also marked a change in how he worked. Earlier he had painted a scene once and moved on; now he grasped that a single motif, returned to again and again under changing light, could become something greater than any one picture. The stacks were his first deliberate series, and their triumphant sale gave him both the money and the confidence to keep going. Straight afterward came the poplars along the Epte, then the Rouen cathedrals, then the long decades at his lily pond. Everything the late Monet became — the serial obsessive, the painter of light rather than things — began in these humble mounds of drying wheat in a field near his house.

## Interesting Facts

- Despite the common English title "Haystacks," these are stacks of harvested wheat, built up in the fields to protect the grain until it could be threshed.
- Monet painted around twenty-five versions of the same two stacks, working several canvases at once and switching between them as the light changed.
- Fifteen were shown together at Durand-Ruel in Paris in May 1891; the exhibition was a runaway success, and most sold almost immediately at record prices for the artist.
- Seeing one of the stacks in Moscow in 1896, the young Wassily Kandinsky could not tell what it depicted — an experience he credited with setting him on the road to abstract art.
- The stacks were the first true series of Monet's career, the model for the poplars, the Rouen cathedrals, and ultimately the water lilies.
- The Art Institute of Chicago holds the largest group of the wheatstack paintings of any museum in the world.
