## The Story

Out of the gloom rears the silhouette of an enormous he-goat — the Devil, in the form witches were said to worship. Around him crowds a semicircle of women, hunched, toothless, and gaping, their faces caught between ecstasy and terror as they attend their master's sermon. On the far right, apart from the coven, a single younger woman sits stiffly on a chair, hands tucked into a muff, as if she has been brought here against her will. The whole scene stretches out in a long, dark frieze, more shadow than paint.

Like Saturn Devouring His Son, this Witches' Sabbath was never a commission or a picture for a gallery. Goya brushed it straight onto the plaster wall of his house, the Quinta del Sordo, sometime around 1819 to 1823, as one of the fourteen Black Paintings that turned his home into a chamber of visions. It hung, wall-sized, in a room where the old deaf painter lived out his days. He gave it no title and left no word about what it meant.

The subject was not new to him. Twenty years earlier Goya had painted witches and devil-goats as biting satire — mockery of the superstition, ignorance, and Inquisition-fed hysteria he despised in Spain. But the wall painting has none of that earlier wit. It is heavier, darker, closer to genuine dread, as if the joke had stopped being funny. When the murals were later cut from the walls and glued onto canvas, this one suffered: a strip along the right side was lost in the transfer, trimming away figures and part of the scene Goya originally painted. What remains is a congregation of the damned, listening to something we will never hear.

## The World Behind It

Goya's Spain was still a place where the Inquisition operated and where belief in witchcraft, curses, and the Devil ran deep in the countryside. As a man of the Enlightenment, Goya loathed this world of fear and priestly control; witches had long been his shorthand for the credulity and cruelty he saw strangling his nation. Under the restored king Ferdinand VII, who brought the Inquisition back to life after 1814, such superstition was not a relic but state-backed power.

Yet by the time he painted the walls of the Quinta del Sordo, Goya seems to have moved past mockery into something bleaker. Surrounded by repression, illness, and silence, he filled his home with images of humanity at its most benighted — herded, howling, gathered around false gods. The Witches' Sabbath belongs to that private reckoning. Whether he meant it as a swipe at the Church, a portrait of mass hysteria, or simply a vision that would not leave him, he never said. He painted it, lived beside it, and kept his counsel.

## The Artist at This Moment

In these years Goya was old, deaf, and increasingly withdrawn from the court and the world. He had survived war and grave illness and had chosen a kind of internal exile in his house on the Manzanares river before the literal exile in France that would end his life. The Black Paintings are the work of that retreat: fast, dark, and unsparing, made with no eye on sale or reputation.

Painting on his own walls freed him completely. There was no patron to please, no Salon to pass, no need to be legible. Goya could let the figures loom out of the murk exactly as grim as he wished. The result is a body of work that looks less like early-nineteenth-century painting than like something a full century ahead of its time. He never spoke of these images publicly, and when he left for Bordeaux he simply left them on the walls, for whoever came next to puzzle over.

## Interesting Facts

- The huge black goat at the left is the Devil himself, presiding over the witches' gathering — the "Great He-Goat" of the traditional title.
- It is one of the fourteen Black Paintings Goya painted directly onto the interior walls of his home; he never named or exhibited any of them.
- When the mural was transferred to canvas in the 1870s, a strip was cut from the right-hand side, removing figures from Goya's original composition.
- Goya had painted a smaller, more satirical Witches' Sabbath decades earlier for the Duchess of Osuna — the wall version is far darker in mood.
- The lone young woman seated apart on the right has puzzled viewers for generations; Goya offered no explanation for her presence.
