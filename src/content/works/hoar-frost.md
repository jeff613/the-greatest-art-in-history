## The Story

The old road out of Pontoise toward Ennery ran between ploughed fields, and one morning in the winter of 1873 Pissarro set up where he could look along it. What he painted was not the road. It was the ground: a slope of furrows turned over by a plough and then frozen solid, the ridges dusted white, the troughs holding cold shadow, the whole field running away in stripes of ochre and violet and dirty green. A man crosses it on a diagonal with a bundle of kindling on his shoulder. Behind him there is a haystack, three bare trees, a smear of village roofs and a sky that has not yet decided whether the sun is coming out.

The strangest thing in it is the shadows. Long blue-grey bars fall across the field from the left, thrown by a row of trees that Pissarro left outside the frame entirely. Nothing in the picture explains them. They simply lie there, striping the frost, and a viewer trained on the polished landscapes of the Salon had no idea what to do with them.

In April 1874 the canvas went up — one of five he sent — in the photographer Nadar's vacated studio on the boulevard des Capucines, in the show that gave Impressionism its name. Louis Leroy of *Le Charivari* toured the rooms in the character of a bewildered academic painter and stopped dead in front of this one.

"By Michalon! What on earth is that?"

"You see... a hoarfrost on deeply ploughed furrows."

"Those furrows? That frost? But they are palette scrapings placed uniformly on a dirty canvas. It has neither head nor tail, top nor bottom, front nor back."

"Perhaps... but the impression is there."

It was meant to finish the painting off. Instead it became the most quoted sentence ever written about it. Leroy was not wrong about what he saw, only about what it was worth: there is no anecdote here, no ruined abbey, no pretty peasant girl, nothing arranged for the eye. There is a cold field, a working man taking a short cut across it, and light doing something specific on a specific morning. The canvas now hangs in the Musée d'Orsay.

## The World Behind It

Pontoise sat thirty kilometres northwest of Paris on the river Oise, and it was not the countryside of picnics. It was market-garden country — cabbage plots, orchards, a distillery, a gasworks, chalk quarries — worked hard by people who lived off it. Pissarro painted that version of rural France for a decade, at a moment when the Paris art world wanted its landscapes either heroic or charming.

France in 1873 was two years out of catastrophe: defeat by Prussia, the siege of Paris, the Commune and the slaughter that ended it, and a five-billion-franc indemnity the country was paying off at humiliating speed. The Salon, restored along with everything else, remained the only door to a career, and its juries kept shutting it. So in December 1873 a group of painters, Pissarro among the organisers, set up a joint-stock company to hold their own exhibition and split the costs. It opened the following April, drew more mockery than buyers, and by the end of the year the company was wound up in debt.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was forty-three, and he had recently lost almost everything he had ever made. Fleeing the Prussian advance in 1870, he had left roughly 1,500 canvases at his rented house in Louveciennes; the soldiers billeted there used them as duckboards and aprons, and about forty survived. He came back from London in 1871 with a new wife — he and Julie Vellay had finally married in Croydon, after a decade together and years of his family's disapproval — and started again from almost nothing at Pontoise. He was the oldest of the group by some years, the one the younger men brought their canvases to, and the one with the least money and the largest household.

Then, in the spring of 1874, his eldest daughter Jeanne, whom the family called Minette, died at Pontoise of a respiratory illness a week short of her ninth birthday. He had drawn and painted her through the last stages of it. She died on 6 April. The first Impressionist exhibition opened nine days later, with his five canvases on the wall.

## Interesting Facts

- The critic Louis Leroy called it "palette scrapings placed uniformly on a dirty canvas" — in the same 1874 review that coined the word Impressionism.
- The long shadows crossing the field are cast by a row of trees Pissarro deliberately kept outside the picture.
- Its full French title names the exact spot: *Gelée blanche, ancienne route d'Ennery, Pontoise* — the old Ennery road.
- Pissarro's daughter Minette died nine days before the exhibition that showed this painting opened; he exhibited anyway.
- The critic Théodore Duret is said to have written to him around this time urging him to stay with rural subjects, telling him he had neither Monet's fanciful eye nor Sisley's decorative sense but something they lacked — a profound feeling for nature.
- He was the only artist to exhibit in all eight Impressionist exhibitions, from this first one in 1874 to the last in 1886.
