## The Story

On the balcony of a riverside restaurant, the remains of a long lunch sit on the table — bottles, glasses, grapes, crumpled napkins — and a dozen young people linger in the warm light, talking, flirting, and in no hurry to leave. This is the Maison Fournaise at Chatou, a boating spot on the Seine just outside Paris, and the people are not anonymous types but Renoir's own friends, gathered into what became his largest and most ambitious painting of modern happiness.

Nearly everyone can be named. In the lower left, the young woman lifting a small affenpinscher dog to her face is Aline Charigot, a seamstress from Renoir's neighborhood — and the woman he would marry nine years later; he reportedly painted her back into the scene after falling out with an earlier model. Slouched at the lower right, straddling a chair in a singlet and straw boater, is the painter and Renoir's great patron Gustave Caillebotte, himself a keen oarsman. Leaning on the railing at the left is Alphonse Fournaise, the restaurant owner's son, with his sister Alphonsine smiling nearby. The actress Ellen Andrée drinks at the table; the top-hatted man deep in the background is the wealthy connoisseur Charles Ephrussi.

Renoir laid all this festivity down over the winter and spring of 1880–81, wrestling a huge, complicated group of figures into a single sunlit composition. It is a summing-up of everything Impressionism loved — leisure, friendship, dappled light, the good life along the river — and it was also a kind of farewell. Soon afterward Renoir grew restless with the loose Impressionist manner, traveled to Italy to study the old masters, and began tightening his drawing. He never again painted a scene quite this large, this crowded, or this joyful.

## The World Behind It

By 1880 the railway had turned the Seine villages west of Paris — Chatou, Bougival, Argenteuil — into a weekend playground. Clerks and shopgirls, artists and their models poured out of the city on Sundays to row, swim, eat, and dance along the water; the sport of *canotage*, or recreational boating, gave the era one of its defining pleasures. The Maison Fournaise rented out skiffs downstairs and served lunch on its balcony, and it drew exactly the mixed, cheerful, slightly raffish crowd Renoir loved to paint.

This riverside leisure was the very subject the Impressionists had claimed for themselves: not history or myth, but ordinary modern people enjoying an ordinary sunny day. It was also a world in gentle flux, where a shopgirl, a painter, a restaurateur's daughter, and a wealthy connoisseur could share the same balcony and the same bottle of wine. Renoir's lunch party is its warmest monument — a whole social world caught in one long, unhurried afternoon.

## The Artist at This Moment

Renoir was around forty, finally beginning to earn a living after years of poverty, and standing at a turning point. He had spent the 1870s as a committed Impressionist, but he was starting to feel he had reached the end of that road — that he had, as he later put it, "wrung Impressionism dry" and no longer knew how to paint or draw. The Boating Party was his grand last word in the sunlit style before he went to Italy in 1881, fell under the spell of Raphael and the frescoes of Pompeii, and reworked his whole approach.

It was also, quietly, a personal picture: the woman with the dog would become his wife and the mother of his three sons. The painting passed through the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel and, in 1923, was bought by the American collector Duncan Phillips, who had chased it for years; it remains the centerpiece of The Phillips Collection in Washington.

## Interesting Facts

- The setting is the real Maison Fournaise restaurant at Chatou on the Seine, which rented boats below and served lunch on the balcony seen here.
- The young woman playing with the little dog is Aline Charigot, whom Renoir married in 1890; he is said to have painted her in after quarreling with an earlier sitter.
- The man lounging backwards on a chair at the lower right is the painter and patron Gustave Caillebotte, a devoted rower.
- The cast also includes the actress Ellen Andrée, the restaurant owner's children Alphonse and Alphonsine Fournaise, and the collector Charles Ephrussi in a top hat.
- Duncan Phillips bought the picture in 1923 for a reported $125,000 after pursuing it for years; it is the treasure of his Washington museum.
- The painting marks the edge of a turning point — soon after finishing it, Renoir left Impressionism behind to study the old masters in Italy.
