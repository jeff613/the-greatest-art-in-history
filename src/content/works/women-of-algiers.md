## The Story

Three women sit in the cool half-light of a tiled interior, among silk, cushions, and the soft gleam of a water-pipe. One leans on her elbow, drowsy and inward; two others sit close, half-murmuring; a fourth figure, a Black servant, turns to leave, drawing back a curtain and glancing over her shoulder. The air is heavy, the light dim and golden, the mood one of enclosed, sensual stillness. Delacroix offers a glimpse into a world Europe had mostly only imagined — the harem, the sealed women's quarters of a North African household.

What set the picture apart from the flood of European fantasies about the "Orient" was that Delacroix seems actually to have been inside such a room. In 1832 he travelled to North Africa as part of a French diplomatic mission to the Sultan of Morocco, and on the journey home he stopped for a few days at Algiers, recently seized by France. There, by his own later account, a local man — variously remembered as a harbour official or a former privateer — let the visiting painter into his home and allowed him to see and sketch the women of his household. Delacroix filled notebooks with rapid watercolour studies of their dress, their poses, and the coloured light of the rooms.

The details of that visit are uncertain and much retold, and how much he really saw is impossible to know. But the encounter haunted him. Two years later, back in Paris, he worked the sketches up into this large canvas and showed it at the Salon of 1834. Instead of the usual titillating fantasy he painted something quieter and stranger: real-seeming women who do not perform for the viewer, absorbed in their own closed world, rendered in a shimmer of reds, golds, and greens that would influence painters for a century. Cézanne, Renoir, and Matisse all revered it; Picasso, obsessed, would eventually paint fifteen variations on it.

## The World Behind It

France had invaded Algiers in 1830, beginning a long and brutal colonisation of Algeria, and Delacroix arrived in the immediate wake of the conquest. His trip was a diplomatic one — the count Charles de Mornay was sent to smooth relations with the Sultan of Morocco, and Delacroix went along as an artist-observer — but it dropped him into a North Africa being pulled into the French empire. For a European painter the region was doubly charged: politically, as new colonial territory, and imaginatively, as the "Orient" of European daydreams, a supposed realm of luxury, indolence, and forbidden women.

That fantasy, later named Orientalism, sold enormously well in Paris, and Delacroix's picture fed it. Yet it also complicated it. Because he had glimpsed a real interior, his women feel observed rather than invented, and the painting sits uneasily between colonial fantasy and something closer to reportage. It remains one of the most beautiful and most debated images the encounter between France and its new colony produced.

## The Artist at This Moment

Delacroix was thirty-six in 1834 and already the acknowledged leader of French Romantic painting, famous — and to conservatives notorious — for the violent colour and passion of The Massacre at Chios, The Death of Sardanapalus, and Liberty Leading the People. The North African journey of 1832 had been the revelation of his life. The light, the colour, and the dignity of the people he saw there struck him as a living antiquity, and he came home with a store of subjects and a warmer, brighter palette that would feed his art for the next thirty years.

Women of Algiers was the first major painting to come out of that trip, and it marks a subtle shift in him — away from the roaring drama of the barricades and toward a more contemplative, sensuous kind of picture. He kept the Moroccan notebooks like treasure and returned to their scenes again and again. Late in life he painted a second, darker version of the same subject, now in Montpellier, as though he could never quite leave that dim, glowing room behind.

## Interesting Facts

- Delacroix seems to have gained rare entry to a real harem during a brief 1832 stop in French-occupied Algiers, on his way home from a diplomatic mission to Morocco — though the details of the visit are uncertain and much embroidered in the retelling.
- He worked the painting up two years later from watercolour sketches and notes made on the spot, and exhibited it at the Paris Salon of 1834.
- The fourth figure, a Black servant shown from behind drawing back a curtain, leads the eye out of the enclosed women's world.
- Unlike most European "Orientalist" fantasies, the picture shows women absorbed in their own lives rather than posed for a viewer, which is part of why later painters admired it so much.
- Pablo Picasso was so obsessed with the work that in 1954–55 he painted a series of fifteen variations on it; one sold in 2015 for about $179 million, then a record for any painting at auction.
- Delacroix painted a second, more shadowy version of the scene around 1847–49, now in the Musée Fabre in Montpellier.
