## The Story

There is no story in it, and that is the point. No saint, no allegory, no patron's coat of arms — just a hare, crouched and alert, ears up, painted against a bare sheet of paper with nothing around it at all. And yet it is one of the most famous images in Western art, because Dürer looked at this ordinary animal with an attention no one had ever brought to a rabbit before. Every strand of fur is described: the darker guard hairs, the softer down beneath, the way the coat lies flat on the back and bristles at the haunch, all built up in thousands of tiny brushstrokes of watercolour and opaque bodycolour, some of them apparently drawn with a brush of a single hair.

The living spark is in the eye. Look closely and you can see, reflected in the dark bulge of it, the cross-barred frame of a window — the studio window, casting its light across the animal and, with it, the whole implied space of a room the picture never shows. It is the same trick Dürer painted into his own eyes in the 1500 self-portrait: a reflection that turns a rendered surface into something that seems to see. Whether he worked from a live hare held or tamed in the workshop, or from a dead specimen he then reanimated from memory and earlier sketches, scholars still argue. The creature's calm, upright poise suggests careful staging; the flawless individual hairs suggest long, patient study of something that would not have sat still.

What is not in doubt is how seriously Dürer took it. In the lower corner he placed his famous monogram — the "D" nested inside the "A" — and the date, 1502, large and deliberate. Artists did not sign study sheets that way. By claiming it so openly, Dürer declared that a watercolour of a single animal, made from nothing but observation, was a finished work of art in its own right, as worthy of his signature as any altarpiece. It was a radically modern idea about what a picture could be about: simply the truth of how something looks.

## The World Behind It

The Young Hare belongs to a small group of nature studies Dürer made around the turn of the sixteenth century — a stag beetle, a bird's wing, and above all the Great Piece of Turf of 1503, a portrait of a clump of weeds rendered with the same devout exactness. They grew out of a distinctly northern habit of mind. Flemish and German artists had long filled the margins of religious pictures with meticulously observed plants, insects, and animals, treating close attention to the created world as a form of piety: to look hard at a blade of grass was to honour the God who made it.

Dürer pushed that impulse to its logical end and let the creature stand alone, with no holy subject to justify it. This was also the age of the first great printed natural histories and cabinets of curiosities, when Europeans were beginning to catalogue the world with fresh, systematic curiosity. His hare sits at the hinge between medieval devotion and the coming scientific gaze — an object of wonder studied for its own sake.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1502 Dürer was about thirty-one, established in Nuremberg as the most celebrated artist in Germany and running a busy workshop that turned out prints, portraits, and altarpieces. His Apocalypse woodcuts had made him rich and internationally known, and he had already begun the theoretical studies of proportion and geometry that would occupy him for life. He was, in other words, at the height of his powers and free to make a picture simply because it interested him.

That year also brought grief: his father, the goldsmith Albrecht the Elder, died in 1502, and Dürer wrote a moving account of the deathbed in his family chronicle. It is tempting, though unprovable, to feel the tenderness of that year in the delicacy of the hare. What is certain is that the watercolour reflects a mind that could turn from the thunder of the Apocalypse to the quiet miracle of an animal's fur, and find both worth the whole of his skill.

## Interesting Facts

- Reflected in the hare's dark eye is the cross-barred frame of a studio window — the same lifelike trick Dürer painted into his own eyes in the 1500 self-portrait.
- Though called the Young Hare, the animal's features are those of a fully mature specimen; the German title simply means "field hare."
- Dürer signed it with his monogram and dated it 1502 in large, deliberate letters, marking it as a finished, independent work rather than a mere preparatory study.
- Some of the finest fur is thought to have been laid down with a brush of only a single hair.
- The picture was copied and forged constantly; at least a dozen sixteenth-century variants are known, and the painter Hans Hoffmann imitated it so skilfully that some of his versions passed as genuine Dürers into the twentieth century.
- It is so fragile that the Albertina keeps it in storage and only rarely puts it on public display.
