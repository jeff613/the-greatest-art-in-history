## The Story

It is one of the most violent pictures in Western art. Muscular soldiers wrench babies from screaming mothers and dash them to the ground; a woman claws at a killer's face, another shields a child with her own body, and the pavement is strewn with tiny broken figures. The subject is the biblical episode in which King Herod, hearing that a future king has been born in Bethlehem, orders every infant boy in the town put to death. Rubens turns it into a churning knot of muscle, terror, and grief, painted with the raw energy of a young man newly returned from Italy and determined to outdo everything he had seen there.

For most of its life, though, the painting was a nobody. It passed into the great Liechtenstein collection in Vienna around 1700 and hung there for two centuries under the name of Jan van den Hoecke, a minor artist who had worked in Rubens's studio. Catalogued as the work of an assistant, it was quietly admired and largely ignored — a big, dark, anonymous canvas of the school of Rubens.

The reckoning came in 2001, when the picture was consigned to Sotheby's in London. The auction house's Old Master expert George Gordon studied it and felt the hand was too commanding for any assistant. Comparing it to Rubens's undoubted early masterpiece Samson and Delilah, painted at the very same moment, he became convinced it was the master himself. When the reattributed painting came up for sale on 10 July 2002, it detonated. Bidding blew past every estimate, and it sold for £49.5 million — a record for any Old Master painting — to the Canadian press baron Kenneth Thomson, Lord Thomson of Fleet. A canvas that had languished for two hundred years as a workshop copy was suddenly one of the most valuable paintings ever sold.

## The World Behind It

Rubens painted the Massacre around 1611, in the first flush of his return to Antwerp after eight years in Italy. There he had drunk deep of Michelangelo's straining nudes, of ancient sculpture, and of the shocking realism of Caravaggio, and this picture is where he poured it all out. The heaped, twisting bodies and the raking light show a painter racing to prove that the north could produce drama as fierce as anything in Rome.

Antwerp was then the great Catholic city of the Spanish Netherlands, and its churches and collectors hungered for exactly this kind of grand, emotionally overwhelming art. Subjects of martyrdom and slaughter were popular in an age still scarred by decades of religious war; a scene of innocent children butchered by a tyrant's soldiers carried a charge that contemporary viewers, who had lived through real massacres, understood in their bones.

## The Artist at This Moment

Rubens was around thirty-four, freshly established as the leading painter of Antwerp and building the workshop that would soon make him famous across Europe. He had just married Isabella Brant, been named court painter to the Spanish archdukes, and begun the flood of major commissions — including the Descent from the Cross — that announced his arrival. The Massacre belongs to this explosive early moment, when he was still proving what he could do.

The painting's later fate is a lesson in how easily even a great work can vanish into anonymity. Rubens ran a huge studio, and countless pictures poured out of it with varying degrees of his own involvement, so attributions blurred and slipped over the centuries. That an autograph masterpiece by the most famous painter of his age could hang for two hundred years under an assistant's name says as much about the fog of art history as it does about the picture — which needed only a fresh pair of expert eyes to blaze back into view.

## Interesting Facts

- For roughly two centuries the painting hung in the Liechtenstein collection under the name of Jan van den Hoecke, a minor former assistant of Rubens.
- Sotheby's expert George Gordon reattributed it to Rubens by comparing it with Samson and Delilah, painted at the same early moment in the artist's career.
- At its 2002 auction it sold for £49.5 million to Kenneth Thomson, Lord Thomson of Fleet — a record price for an Old Master painting.
- Thomson lent the picture to the National Gallery in London before donating it in 2008 to the Art Gallery of Ontario in Toronto, where it now hangs.
- Rubens painted it soon after eight years in Italy, and its straining, muscular bodies show how deeply he had absorbed Michelangelo and Caravaggio.
- He painted a second, later version of the same subject decades afterward; this early one is considered the more ferocious of the two.
