## The Story

She is leaning in. Elbow braced on the balustrade, a folded fan in her lap, both hands holding the glasses hard against her eyes — and her gaze is level, aimed straight across the auditorium, not down at the stage. Whatever she is looking at is another box, and whoever is in it does not know.

Behind her the theatre curves away in tiers of gold and shadow, and across the gap, in a box at the upper left, a man in black has leaned out over his own rail with his glasses up and aimed squarely at her. He is not watching the play either. The picture is a chain of looking with three links: the woman looks, the man looks at the woman, and you look at both. Cassatt built the whole thing so that you arrive last and find yourself doing exactly what he is doing.

That the woman is unaware makes her no less formidable. She is not arranged for the viewer. She is not glancing coyly over a shoulder; she is working, scanning, taking the house apart. Every other painter of the loge — Renoir most famously, and Degas — had put a woman in the box to be admired. Cassatt gave her the instrument and let her use it.

She is dressed in black, buttoned to the throat, a dark bonnet on her head and one pearl at her ear. That is the tell for the time of day: this is an afternoon performance, most likely at the Comédie-Française, where a respectable woman went in street clothes rather than the bare-shouldered evening dress of the night crowd. The sitter is generally identified as Cassatt's sister Lydia, who had come from Pennsylvania to live with her in Paris the year before and who sat for painting after painting.

The theatre box mattered to Cassatt for a reason that had nothing to do with plush and gaslight. She could not go to the cafés where the Impressionists argued; that was not a place a woman of her class could be seen. The loge was one of the very few public stages in Paris where a woman could sit in full view, in daylight, and look at whatever she liked. She painted it as what it was: a hunting blind.

The canvas was among the first of her paintings ever shown in America, exhibited in Boston in 1878. It stayed with her family until the 1890s and entered the Museum of Fine Arts in 1910.

## The World Behind It

Paris in the 1870s had been rebuilt as a machine for looking at people. Haussmann's boulevards, the department stores, the café terraces and the new opera house all did the same work — they put strangers in front of each other and made the city into an audience. The Impressionists painted almost nothing else.

But the rules of that looking were not the same for everyone. A man could go where he pleased and stare where he liked. A middle-class woman had a short list of places she could be, and the theatre was the best of them: an evening or an afternoon in which her whole social world was arranged in lit boxes, visible, gradeable, and available to be examined through a small brass instrument that made staring polite. Marriages were made across that gap. So were reputations.

The loge itself was a subject the Impressionists worked over repeatedly in these years — Renoir had exhibited his own at the first group show in 1874, Degas kept returning to the orchestra and the boxes above it. What none of them had done was hand the glasses to the woman.

## The Artist at This Moment

1878 was Cassatt's first full year as an Impressionist by choice rather than by rejection. Degas had invited her the previous autumn, after the Salon threw out both her submissions, and she had said yes gratefully. The group's next exhibition had been planned for 1878 and was pushed to 1879 because of the world's fair, which gave her a long, useful year to prepare.

She was thirty-four and no longer a studio painter. She had begun carrying a sketchbook into the theatre and out into the streets. Her parents and Lydia had arrived from America and taken a fifth-floor apartment with her on the Avenue Trudaine, which gave her a household, a model, and an audience. Degas' studio was a five-minute walk away and he had the habit of dropping in with advice. She was learning fast and she was, for the first time, painting exactly what she wanted to paint.

## Interesting Facts

- The woman's black daytime dress dates the scene to an afternoon performance rather than an evening one — most likely at the Comédie-Française.
- The sitter is generally identified as Cassatt's sister Lydia, her most frequent model until Lydia's death in 1882.
- The man in the distant box has his own glasses trained on her, making the viewer the third and last link in a chain of staring.
- Cassatt could not join her fellow Impressionists in the cafés where they argued — it was not a place a woman of her class could be seen — so she painted the theatre, one of the few public rooms open to her.
- It was one of the earliest Cassatt paintings shown in the United States, exhibited in Boston the year it was made.
- The picture is often reproduced under the alternative title *At the Opera*.
