## The Story

In the last days of May 1889, Vincent van Gogh stood at the barred window of his bedroom in the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole and watched the sky before dawn. "This morning I saw the countryside from my window a long time before sunrise," he wrote to his brother Theo, "with nothing but the morning star, which looked very big." That star was Venus, and out of that pre-dawn vigil came the most famous night sky ever painted.

He did not paint it at the window. Patients were not allowed paint in their bedrooms, so The Starry Night was made by day, in a ground-floor studio, from studies and memory — which is exactly why it looks the way it does. Freed from the scene in front of him, Vincent let the sky move. Eleven stars roll and boil, the moon burns orange, and between them a great wave of light curls across the canvas like a current made visible. Below, he painted a village that does not exist: the Alpilles hills are Provence, but the slender church spire is pure Holland, smuggled in from the country of his childhood. A cypress — the tree of southern graveyards, which he said astonished him because no one had yet painted it as he saw it — surges up the left edge like a green flame connecting earth to sky.

Then he mentioned it to Theo almost in passing, as "a new study of a starry sky," rolled it up, and shipped it to Paris with a batch of other canvases. Nobody involved treated it as a masterpiece — least of all Vincent. That November he wrote to the painter Émile Bernard that in reaching for stars too big he had let himself go astray, and called the picture a setback. He never saw it hung, never saw it praised. He was dead fourteen months after painting it, and The Starry Night passed, unsold and almost unnoticed, into the hands of the family of the brother who had believed in him.

## The World Behind It

While Vincent watched Venus rise over Provence, Paris was throwing the party of the century. The Exposition Universelle opened in May 1889, and the Eiffel Tower — brand new, electric-lit, the tallest structure on earth — announced the triumph of the modern age. The contrast could not have been crueler: the era's most original painter spent that summer behind the walls of a half-empty former monastery, one of perhaps forty patients in a provincial asylum where treatment amounted to two baths a week and quiet.

Madness in 1889 was a life sentence of shame. Families hid their afflicted; the Arles neighbors who petitioned to have Vincent confined called him "the redheaded madman." Yet Saint-Paul was, by the standards of the day, humane — its director gave Vincent a second cell to use as a studio and, when he was well, freedom to paint in the walled wheat field and the olive groves beyond.

Meanwhile the avant-garde he had left behind in Paris was splintering. Seurat was building pictures from scientific dots; Gauguin and Bernard preached painting from imagination rather than nature. Vincent followed the argument by letter and distrusted it — he needed the real thing in front of him. The Starry Night is his one great concession to the other side: a picture painted mostly from the inside. It troubled him that he loved making it.

## The Artist at This Moment

Vincent had been in the asylum three weeks. Five months earlier, on 23 December 1888, his dream of an artists' colony in Arles had collapsed in a single night: the quarrels with Gauguin, the razor, the severed ear delivered to a girl at the brothel, the police finding him unconscious in a blood-soaked bed. Hospitalizations followed, then a petition from thirty of his neighbors demanding he be locked up. In May 1889 he did it himself, signing into Saint-Paul-de-Mausole voluntarily. He was thirty-six.

What is astonishing about the asylum year is not that he kept painting but the pace of it — around 150 canvases in fifty-two weeks, painted in the intervals between attacks that left him incoherent for weeks at a time; during one, he swallowed his own paints. Work was the only medicine that held. In the first burst, June 1889, came the irises, the wheat field behind the walls, the cypresses, and this sky.

He painted The Starry Night sane, lucid, and homesick — a Dutchman's church spire under a Provençal sky is not a hallucination, it is a longing. Weeks later a violent attack silenced him until autumn. He had fourteen months to live, and he spent nearly every good hour of them painting.

## Interesting Facts

- Vincent thought the painting was a failure. That November he wrote to Émile Bernard that by doing "stars too big" he had let himself be led astray into abstraction — a "setback" he'd had enough of.
- The "morning star" was real. Astronomers have calculated that Venus blazed unusually bright over Provence at dawn in late May and June 1889, exactly where and when Vincent said he saw it.
- The village is invented. It is not visible from his east-facing window, and the church's slender spire belongs to the Netherlands, not Provence — he transplanted it from home, more than 1,000 kilometers away.
- Unsalable in his lifetime, it entered the Museum of Modern Art in 1941 through the Lillie P. Bliss Bequest — MoMA traded away two Cézannes and a Toulouse-Lautrec to get it. It has been the museum's crown jewel ever since.
- In 2024, physicists writing in Physics of Fluids argued that the sky's swirls follow Kolmogorov's law of turbulence — a physical theory not formulated until half a century after Van Gogh's death.
- Don McLean's 1971 ballad "Vincent (Starry, Starry Night)," written after reading a Van Gogh biography, carried the painting to a global audience and reached No. 1 in the UK charts.
