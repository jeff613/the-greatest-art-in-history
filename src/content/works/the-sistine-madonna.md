## The Story

Raphael painted the Madonna countless times, but this one steps toward you. Green curtains have just been drawn back, and the Virgin walks forward out of a haze that resolves, when you look closely, into the faces of countless unborn souls. She carries the Christ child not with the gentle poise of Raphael's earlier Madonnas but with a grave, forward momentum, as if she is coming down to meet the viewer. On her right kneels Saint Sixtus, an old pope in gold, gesturing out of the frame; on her left kneels Saint Barbara, eyes lowered. The whole painting is built as a vision descending through parted curtains toward the world below.

And then, along the very bottom edge, lean two small winged boys — chin on hand, elbows on the ledge, gazing up with the frank boredom of children stuck at the end of a long ceremony. They are the most famous accident in the history of taste. Cut loose from the solemn scene above them, reproduced on stamps, postcards, wrapping paper, socks, and greeting cards beyond counting, these two putti have become one of the most recognizable images in Western art — often floating free with no idea that they once anchored a sacred altarpiece.

The picture was commissioned around 1512, most likely by Pope Julius II, for the high altar of the church of San Sisto in the northern Italian town of Piacenza — which is why it is "Sistine," after Saint Sixtus, the church's patron and a patron saint of Julius's family. Painted on canvas rather than Raphael's usual wooden panel, it may have been conceived to be carried, and it hung quietly in Piacenza for more than two centuries. It was only later, and far to the north, that it became one of the most worshipped paintings in Europe.

## The World Behind It

That fame was made in Germany. In 1754 Augustus III, Elector of Saxony and King of Poland, bought the altarpiece from the monks of San Sisto for an enormous sum and carried it off to Dresden. There it detonated. German writers and philosophers made pilgrimages to stand before it; it was called divine, wept over, argued about, treated as proof of what human art could reach. The painting became a fixture of the German Romantic imagination and, later, a favorite of Russian visitors too — Dostoevsky kept a reproduction of it above his desk.

Its modern history is nearly as dramatic as its devotion. Hidden away during the Second World War, the *Sistine Madonna* was seized by the Soviet Red Army in 1945 and taken to Moscow, where it hung in secret for a decade. In 1955, after Stalin's death, the Soviet Union returned it to Dresden along with other rescued masterpieces, and it went back on the wall of the Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister, where it still draws crowds who often come, above all, to see two bored little angels.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1512 Raphael was at the summit of his Roman career, the favorite of the papal court and drowning in commissions. He was frescoing the Vatican apartments, designing tapestries, painting portraits of the powerful, and running a workshop that could barely keep pace with demand. The intimate, sweet-faced Madonnas of his Florentine youth were behind him; this was the work of a mature master at the center of Christendom's art, able to make a towering altarpiece feel as immediate as an apparition.

He had less than a decade to live. Within a few years he would be named architect of St. Peter's and set to work on the Transfiguration, the painting he would leave unfinished when he died suddenly in 1520 at thirty-seven. The *Sistine Madonna* belongs to his great Roman prime, when everything he touched turned to grace and the demands on him were nearly limitless — a serene, monumental vision produced by a man working at a pace that may well have helped burn out his short life.

## Interesting Facts

- The two cherubs at the bottom were long thought to be an afterthought; today they are among the most reproduced figures in all of art, printed on everything from stamps to gift wrap.
- The painting is named for Saint Sixtus (San Sisto), the church in Piacenza it was made for, not for the Sistine Chapel.
- Raphael painted it on canvas rather than his customary wooden panel, unusual for him and possibly meant to make the large work easier to move.
- In 1754 Augustus III of Saxony bought it for Dresden at a price so high it remained a record for a painting for decades.
- The Red Army carried it to Moscow after World War II; the Soviet Union returned it to Dresden in 1955.
- Dostoevsky revered the picture and kept a reproduction of it on the wall of his study.
