## The Story

Open the great hinged panels in St Bavo's Cathedral and the Ghent Altarpiece unfolds into a shining vision: God enthroned in crimson, Adam and Eve life-sized and naked, choirs of angels, and at the centre a white lamb standing on an altar, blood streaming from its breast into a golden chalice while pilgrims stream in from every corner of the earth to adore it. Finished in 1432, it is a two-ton wall of oil paint, twelve interior panels blazing with a colour and detail Europe had never seen. An inscription on the frame credits two hands: the painter Hubert van Eyck, "than whom none was greater," began it, and his brother Jan, "second in art," completed the "weighty task." For centuries scholars doubted Hubert even existed; only recent study confirmed the older brother's part.

But the altarpiece is famous for a second reason entirely. It is, by most counts, the most stolen work of art in history — the target of some thirteen crimes across six centuries. Calvinist rioters nearly burned it; Napoleon's troops carted panels off to the Louvre; a later canon sold the wings, which ended up in Berlin. In 1934 thieves struck again, prising the lower-left panel, the Just Judges, out of its frame in the night. They cut it in two, returned half — a grisaille of John the Baptist — to a train-station cloakroom as proof, and demanded a ransom for the rest. A dying suspect gasped that he alone knew where it lay. The Righteous Judges have never been found, and the panel visible today is a 1940s copy.

The Nazis wanted it most of all. Hitler regarded the altarpiece as a German trophy owed by the Treaty of Versailles and had it seized and hidden deep in the Altaussee salt mine in Austria, among thousands of looted masterpieces. There the retreating SS rigged the mine with explosives. It was the Monuments Men who reached it in 1945 and hauled the van Eycks back into daylight.

## The World Behind It

The altarpiece was not a royal commission but the gift of a rich Ghent couple. Joos Vijd, a wealthy patrician and city official, and his wife Elisabeth Borluut paid for it to adorn their private chapel in the church that is now St Bavo's Cathedral — securing prayers for their souls and their names in the frame for eternity. In the Flanders of the 1430s, that was how the merchant class bought a foothold in heaven: not with land or titles but with art of overwhelming ambition.

Ghent itself was a proud, turbulent cloth town, one of the largest cities north of the Alps and forever bristling against its Burgundian overlords. The wealth that paid for van Eyck's pigments — the imported blues, the gold — came from wool and weaving. That a single couple could fund so vast and dazzling an object says everything about how much money now pooled in the hands of Flemish burghers.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1432 Jan van Eyck was newly settled in Bruges and freshly in the service of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, who valued him as both painter and confidential envoy. The altarpiece marks his emergence as the supreme artist of the North. Yet the project was not his alone. The frame names his brother Hubert as the one who began it, and Hubert seems to have died in 1426, leaving Jan to bring the enormous work to completion over the following years.

It was, in effect, an inheritance and a proving ground. Whatever Hubert had started, it was Jan who finished the panels that made the altarpiece immortal — the astonishingly real nudes of Adam and Eve, the jewelled robes, the deep landscape teeming with tiny figures. He signed off the whole with a dated verse in 1432, planting his name, as he always did, at the foot of his greatest achievement.

## Interesting Facts

- It is widely called the most stolen artwork in history, targeted in roughly thirteen separate crimes over nearly six hundred years.
- Napoleon's forces took panels to the Louvre; after Waterloo they came home, only for other wings to be sold off and land in a Berlin museum for decades.
- In 1934 thieves cut away the Just Judges panel; it was never recovered, and the version on view today is a copy painted in the 1940s.
- Hitler coveted the altarpiece as a German trophy and hid it in the Altaussee salt mine, where the SS wired the chambers with explosives before the Monuments Men rescued it in 1945.
- A frame inscription credits Jan's older brother Hubert with beginning the work — a figure so shadowy that historians once doubted he was real, until modern research confirmed his hand.
- An eight-year, $2.4-million restoration completed in 2019 stripped away later overpaint to reveal the central Lamb's startlingly human, forward-staring face — the way the van Eycks first painted it.
