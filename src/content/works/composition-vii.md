## The Story

*Composition VII* is the wildest painting Kandinsky ever made and, by his own reckoning, the most important. Across nearly ten feet of canvas color storms in every direction — reds bleeding into blues, black lines whipping like broken cables, forms surging and colliding around a dark, swirling center. There is no ground and no sky, no up or down, nothing to hold on to. It looks like chaos. It is anything but.

Buried inside the tumult are subjects Kandinsky had painted for years, now dissolved almost past recognition: the Deluge, the Last Judgment, the raising of the dead, the garden of paradise beyond the storm. He wanted a viewer to *feel* the end of the world and the salvation on its far side, not to read them like a picture-book. The apocalypse here is meant to hit you as pure sensation before your eye can name a single thing in it — the same jolt he had felt years earlier before a Monet haystack he could not identify.

What astonishes is how it was made. Kandinsky prepared the picture like a general planning a campaign: more than thirty preparatory works over the course of 1913 — pencil sketches, ink drawings, watercolors, and full oil studies — each testing where a surge or a knot of color should fall. Then, having planned it exhaustively, he painted the enormous final canvas in a furious burst of roughly four days, from 25 to 28 November 1913, in his Munich studio. His companion, the painter Gabriele Münter, photographed the canvas as it grew, and her images show the composition erupting outward from that central vortex, the whole crowded field filling in stage by stage. The frenzy on the surface was the fruit of relentless discipline underneath.

The painting stayed with him through the collapse that followed. When war broke out in 1914 and Kandinsky, a Russian national, was forced to leave Germany, the great canvas eventually made its way to Moscow. Today it hangs in the Tretyakov Gallery, the acknowledged summit of his pre-war art and, for many, the single most important abstract painting made before 1914.

## The World Behind It

Kandinsky painted *Composition VII* in the last golden months before the catastrophe of the twentieth century. Europe in 1913 was prosperous, restless, and shadowed by a widespread sense that some vast reckoning was coming — a mood that ran through the art, music, and mystical writing of the age. Kandinsky, steeped in Theosophy and in dreams of a coming spiritual epoch, felt it keenly, and his flood-and-resurrection imagery belongs to that apocalyptic climate.

He was working at the heart of Munich's avant-garde, then one of the liveliest art cities in Europe. Two years earlier he and Franz Marc had founded Der Blaue Reiter and published its almanac, gathering painters who believed color and form carried spiritual values that could stand against the materialism of the age. Kandinsky had set out that faith in *Concerning the Spiritual in Art*. Within a year of finishing this canvas, the world he made it in would be gone: the war would scatter the Blue Rider circle, kill Marc at Verdun, and send Kandinsky back to Russia.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1913 Kandinsky was forty-seven and at the height of his powers, a Russian émigré who had remade himself in Bavaria. He lived and worked with Gabriele Münter, dividing his time between Munich and the couple's house in the Alpine village of Murnau, and he had become as much a theorist as a painter — the author of a book that argued painting could be a spiritual force on the level of music.

He could not have known how brief this summit would be. The intense, productive Munich years were about to end abruptly. Within months the war would break the partnership with Münter, uproot him from Germany overnight, and close the most fertile chapter of his life. *Composition VII* stands at the very peak of that vanished world, painted by a man at the top of his form, in full command of everything he had spent seventeen years learning, just before it was all swept away.

## Interesting Facts

- Kandinsky made more than thirty preparatory studies — drawings, watercolors, and oil sketches — before touching the final canvas.
- Despite that vast preparation, he painted the finished picture in a burst of roughly four days, 25–28 November 1913.
- His companion Gabriele Münter photographed the canvas at successive stages, leaving a rare record of an abstract masterpiece taking shape.
- At about two by three meters, it is the largest and most complex of his pre-war paintings.
- Hidden within the abstraction are apocalyptic themes he had painted for years — the Deluge, the Last Judgment, and the Resurrection.
- The painting now hangs in the Tretyakov Gallery in Moscow, a short journey from the city where Kandinsky was born.
