## The Story

It is a bright ordinary afternoon in a country house. The piano is open, there is a cloth on the table, the light comes off the scrubbed floorboards. And a man has just come through the door in a long shabby coat, thin, bearded, hollow at the eyes, and stopped, because he does not yet know what he is walking into either.

The room is caught mid-realisation, and Repin has given every figure a different rate of understanding. The grey-haired woman in black is already out of her chair and moving toward him, and she is painted from behind — we get the back of her head and one hand on the chair rail, and no face at all, which is the most brutal decision in the picture, because it makes us do the work. The younger woman at the piano has turned on her stool and gone rigid. The schoolboy at the table has got it and is lighting up with it. The little girl beside him has not, and is frowning at a strange man in her house. In the doorway a maid holds the handle and watches with open suspicion. Nobody in the painting is sure enough to speak.

He is a political exile, back from Siberia. The pictures on the wall say so before he does: the poets Shevchenko and Nekrasov, a photograph of Alexander II on his deathbed, and a print of Christ on Golgotha — a household that had made martyrdom its furniture. But Repin refuses to explain the return. No amnesty of the period covered men like him; there is no note in the picture, no official paper, no reason. He is simply there, and the not-knowing is the whole event.

Repin could not leave the man's face alone. He showed the picture at the Peredvizhniki exhibition of February 1884 and then went back into it in 1885, in 1887, and again in 1888, repainting that head at least three times — changing the tilt, the eyes, the degree of shame in it — hunting for the expression of a man who has been away for years and has no idea whether he is still wanted. Pavel Tretyakov, who bought it in 1885, had hesitated over exactly that face, feeling it did not sit right with the rest of the family. What Repin finally settled on is not triumph and not collapse. It is a question.

## The World Behind It

In the 1870s thousands of young educated Russians went "to the people" — out of the lecture halls and into the villages to educate, agitate and live among the peasants they had theorised about. The peasants mostly reported them to the police. The government answered with mass arrests and show trials, and out of the wreckage came the terrorists of the People's Will, who spent years trying to kill Alexander II and finally managed it with a bomb in March 1881.

The standard punishment for the rest was exile: administrative banishment to Siberia or the far north, often without trial, for terms measured in years. Families were left in exactly the state Repin paints — intact, respectable, going on with the piano lessons, and missing a person. His *narodniki* cycle followed those people through the whole arc, from the arrest to the refusal of confession before execution. This is the only one of them in which somebody comes home.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was forty when the picture first hung and forty-four when he stopped touching it, working at the top of his form and on several fronts at once — the murdered tsarevich, the Cossacks in their eleventh year, this. The Wanderers' exhibitions were now the most important shows in Russia and he was their biggest name.

His household sat for it: his wife Vera at the piano, his daughter Vera at the table. And through the four years in which he kept scraping the returning man's face off the canvas and painting it again, his own marriage was ending. He and Vera separated in 1887, between the third repainting and the fourth. He also gave the exile something of Vsevolod Garshin, the writer whose face he thought was marked for death and who had already served him as the dying tsarevich; Garshin killed himself in 1888, the year Repin finally put the brush down.

## Interesting Facts

- The Russian title *Ne zhdali* means, roughly, "they weren't waiting for him" — nobody in the room knew he was coming.
- Repin never lets us see the face of the woman rising to meet him. She is painted entirely from behind, and her back does the work of a scream.
- The pictures on the walls identify the family's politics: portraits of Taras Shevchenko and Nikolai Nekrasov, a photograph of the assassinated Alexander II on his deathbed, and a print of Christ on Golgotha.
- Repin repainted the returning exile's head at least three times over four years, in 1885, 1887 and 1888, long after the picture had been exhibited and sold.
- An earlier, much smaller version painted on wood in 1883 shows a young woman student coming home instead, portfolio in hand; Repin went back and reworked her too, fifteen years later.
- Pavel Tretyakov bought it in February 1885 for seven thousand roubles, having hesitated because he found the returning man's expression at odds with the rest of the room.
