## The Story

In the winter of 1897 Gauguin was living in a hut in Tahiti with nothing left. He was in debt. He had eczema, conjunctivitis, and the syphilis that would eventually kill him. A letter had reached him from Copenhagen months earlier telling him that Aline, his only daughter and the child he had loved best, had died of pneumonia at nineteen. He wrote to his friend Daniel de Monfreid in December that he intended to die, and that before he did he wanted to paint one great canvas.

He worked on a piece of coarse sackcloth nearly four metres wide, feverishly, by his own account through a single month. What he produced is not a scene so much as a procession — twelve figures and a scatter of animals laid across a shadowed blue-green world, with a strange pale idol raising its arms in the middle distance. In the corner he lettered the three questions that give the picture its name, and he insisted the thing be read backwards, right to left: an infant asleep on the ground, then a young man reaching up to pick fruit at the centre, then, at the far left, an old woman crouched with her head in her hands, waiting. Birth, the brief business of living, and death. Gauguin said the old woman had accepted her fate. He also said the picture asked its questions and answered none of them.

Then, the work finished, he walked into the hills and swallowed arsenic. The dose was too large; he vomited it up and survived, which he seems to have regarded as a further humiliation. He lived another five years.

He rolled the canvas up and shipped it to Paris, where the dealer Ambroise Vollard hung it in November 1898. The reception was mixed — admiring, baffled, not the vindication he wanted. It sold three years later to a collector in Bordeaux for 2,500 francs, drifted through Paris and Norway and New York, and finally reached the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston in 1936, thirty-three years after the painter's death.

## The World Behind It

The Tahiti Gauguin had sailed to in 1891 was not the one he had advertised to himself. It was a French colony with a governor, a customs house, and missionary schools, and its people had been reduced by imported disease from perhaps forty thousand at first European contact to a fraction of that. The old religion he wanted to paint had been largely dismantled a generation before he arrived; much of what he put on canvas he assembled from a scholarly book, secondhand accounts, and his own invention.

He knew this and painted the imagined version anyway, partly out of longing and partly because it was what Europe would buy. Meanwhile the actual colony ground him down. He fought the administration, wrote furious articles, and by 1897 was ill enough to be in and out of the hospital in Papeete, unable to afford to stay. The great canvas was made by a man losing an argument with the real world.

## The Artist at This Moment

Gauguin was forty-nine and had spent a decade proving that leaving had not worked. The Paris career had collapsed, the family in Copenhagen had effectively written him off, the first Tahitian voyage had ended in a failed Paris exhibition, and the second had left him poorer than the first.

Aline's death broke something. She was the child he had written to, kept a notebook for, imagined a future with; he learned of it by letter, months late, thousands of miles away, from a wife he had abandoned. He never saw the grave. The old woman at the left edge of the painting, hunched and resigned, is as close as he came to painting his own state.

He would leave Tahiti for the more remote Marquesas in 1901, still fighting the authorities, and die there in 1903 with a prison sentence hanging over him. This canvas is the summary he made when he thought he had days left.

## Interesting Facts

- Gauguin insisted the painting be read from right to left, the reverse of European habit — infancy at one end, an old woman awaiting death at the other.
- He painted it on rough sackcloth because it was what he could afford; the weave shows through the paint.
- Immediately after finishing it he attempted suicide with arsenic, took too much, vomited it back up, and survived.
- The three questions lettered in the top corner are the title — one of the few paintings whose name the artist physically wrote onto the canvas.
- At nearly 3.75 metres wide it is by far his largest work, and he told Monfreid he thought it better than anything he had done or ever would do.
- It changed hands through Bordeaux, Paris, Norway, and New York before Boston bought it in 1936; Gauguin had been dead for a third of a century.
