## The Story

There was one view Cézanne never tired of. Mont Sainte-Victoire, the long limestone ridge that rises east of Aix-en-Provence, appears in some thirty of his paintings and many more drawings and watercolours — he returned to it, obsessively, for the last two decades of his life, the way a writer returns to a single, inexhaustible sentence. This version, from around 1887, is among the most beautiful and the most fully resolved: the mountain floating pale and blue on the horizon, the wide valley of the river Arc spread beneath it, and in the foreground a great pine whose sweeping branches arc across the sky to echo the very slopes of the peak behind.

That pine is doing quiet, deliberate work. Its curving limbs tie the near and the far together, pulling the distant mountain forward and knitting the whole scene into a single, breathing structure — a favourite device of Cézanne's, who wanted a landscape to feel constructed and permanent, not merely glimpsed. He painted the view from the Bellevue estate belonging to his sister and brother-in-law, on a rise that gave him the sweep of cultivated valley leading the eye toward the mountain.

Look closely at that valley and you can find the thin horizontal line of a railway viaduct and the roofs of scattered farms — the modern world quietly threading its way into an ancient, sun-baked landscape. Cézanne did not dramatise it; he simply absorbed it into the timeless order of the scene, as if the works of men and the bones of the earth belonged to the same slow eternity.

The picture carries another sign of his rare contentment: it is one of the very few canvases he signed. He later gave it to Joachim Gasquet, a young Provençal poet whose sincere admiration moved the ageing, mistrustful painter — a small, generous gesture from a man who parted with his work reluctantly.

## The World Behind It

Mont Sainte-Victoire was more than scenery to Cézanne; it was the emblem of home. It had loomed over his boyhood, over the river where he and his schoolfriends had swum, over the whole sun-struck world of Provence he had returned to after Paris wore him down. To paint it again and again was to root himself in the one place that felt permanent.

Provence in the 1880s was changing. Railways now stitched the region to Marseille and beyond, tourists were beginning to discover the southern light, and the old agricultural rhythms were meeting the machinery of a modern republic — the viaduct in the valley is a small monument to that shift. Yet the mountain endured above it all, indifferent and eternal, which is surely part of why it gripped Cézanne so. In an age obsessed with speed and novelty, he chose to paint the thing that does not change.

## The Artist at This Moment

The year 1887 fell just after the most upheaval-filled stretch of Cézanne's life. In 1886 his father had died, leaving him wealthy and finally free; that same year he had at last married Hortense Fiquet, his companion of many years. And in 1886 his oldest, dearest friend, the novelist Émile Zola, had published *L'Œuvre*, the story of a brilliant painter who fails and destroys himself.

Tradition holds that Cézanne, recognising a cruel portrait of himself, broke with Zola forever — though recent scholarship has softened that tale, pointing to warm letters that passed between the two men afterward, and suggesting the friendship cooled slowly rather than shattering at a blow. Either way, these were years when Cézanne withdrew ever deeper into Provence and his work. Turning from the wounds of friendship and family to the steadfast mountain of his childhood, he found in Mont Sainte-Victoire a subject that would never disappoint him, and would occupy him to the end of his life.

## Interesting Facts

- Cézanne painted Mont Sainte-Victoire around thirty times in oil, plus many watercolours and drawings, over the last twenty-odd years of his life.
- This is the most monumental and highly finished of three canvases he painted of this particular view, taken from the Bellevue estate of his brother-in-law.
- The great pine in the foreground is a framing device: its branches deliberately trace the contours of the mountain behind.
- Hidden in the valley are a railway viaduct and farmhouses — signs of the modern world entering an ancient landscape.
- It is one of the rare works Cézanne signed, and he gave it as a gift to the young poet Joachim Gasquet.
- Samuel Courtauld, who assembled Britain's greatest group of Cézannes, spoke of feeling "the magic" the first time he saw the artist's work.
