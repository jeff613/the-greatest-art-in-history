## The Story

Around 1503 a Florentine silk merchant named Francesco del Giocondo commissioned a portrait of his young wife, Lisa Gherardini, probably to mark the birth of a son or the purchase of a new house. It was an ordinary job for an extraordinary painter — and Leonardo never handed it over. He carried the small poplar panel with him for the rest of his life, from Florence to Milan to Rome and at last across the Alps to France, returning to it again and again over sixteen years, thinning the oil into veils of shadow until the face seemed to breathe. The patron who paid for it appears never to have received it.

What Leonardo kept was not really a portrait of anyone anymore. He had dissolved the hard outlines that every other painter used, letting the corners of the mouth and eyes melt into soft, smoky ambiguity — the technique the Italians called *sfumato*, "gone to smoke." The result is a woman who seems to be turning toward you, on the edge of a smile that changes each time you try to fix it. Behind her stretches an unearthly landscape of winding rivers and blue crags that belongs to no real place, painted as if seen from the height of a soaring bird.

When Leonardo died at Amboise in 1519, the panel stayed in France. King Francis I acquired it, and for centuries it hung among the royal pictures, admired by connoisseurs but no more famous than a dozen other masterworks. It took a crime to make her immortal. On an August morning in 1911 the painting simply vanished off the wall of the Louvre, and the two-year manhunt that followed — with its false leads, its celebrity suspects, and its astonishing recovery — turned a Renaissance portrait into the most recognized image in the history of the world. The empty space where she had hung drew bigger crowds than the painting ever had.

## The World Behind It

Leonardo began the picture in a Florence that had just thrown off tyranny and Savonarola alike and was calling itself a republic again. Portraiture was booming: the city's merchant families, newly rich and freshly self-important, wanted their faces preserved with the dignity once reserved for saints and princes. Most such portraits were stiff profiles, the sitter turned aside like a head on a coin.

Leonardo broke every rule. He turned Lisa to face the viewer at a gentle three-quarter angle, gave her a living hand resting over hand, and refused to weigh her down with jewels or heraldry that would pin her to a family and a rank. The scholar Agostino Vespucci, scribbling a note in the margin of a book in October 1503, recorded that Leonardo was then at work on the head of "Lisa del Giocondo" — the single strongest thread tying this most famous of faces to a real Florentine woman, though the painter kept her so long that she became something more universal than a wife.

## The Artist at This Moment

Leonardo was past fifty when he started the portrait, and the world had begun to see him less as a painter than as a marvel of a man — engineer, anatomist, deviser of war machines and flying contraptions, chronically unable to finish what he began. In these same Florentine years he took a public commission for a vast battle mural, *The Battle of Anghiari*, and abandoned it half-done when his experimental paints ran down the wall. Just across the city, a furious young Michelangelo was assigned the opposite wall, and the two geniuses circled each other with open contempt.

Against that backdrop of grand failures and rivalries, the little panel of Lisa was Leonardo's secret companion. He never treated it as a job to be completed and delivered; he treated it as a problem he could not stop refining. It followed him into old age and exile, and it was still in his rooms, unfinished in his own mind, when he died.

## Interesting Facts

- On 21 August 1911 an Italian handyman named Vincenzo Peruggia, who had once worked at the Louvre, walked out of the museum with the painting hidden under his smock. It was missing for over two years; he was caught in 1913 only when he tried to sell it to a Florence art dealer, claiming he wanted to return it to Italy.
- The theft made the picture world-famous. During the investigation the poet Guillaume Apollinaire was arrested on suspicion, and he in turn pointed police toward his friend Pablo Picasso, who was questioned and released.
- The identification of the sitter as Lisa del Giocondo rests largely on Giorgio Vasari's biography and a 1503 margin note discovered in a Heidelberg library in 2005 — strong evidence, but the painting's decades-long evolution means scholars still argue over how literal a likeness it really is.
- It has been attacked more than once: in 1956 a vandal threw acid at the lower half and another hurled a rock at it. Today it sits behind bulletproof glass in a climate-controlled case.
- Leonardo never delivered the portrait to the man who commissioned it. It passed instead to King Francis I of France, which is why an Italian merchant's wife has hung in Paris for five centuries.
