## The Story

A pale young woman stares out past us, unsmiling, her face luminous against a dark thicket of spiky juniper. Her skin has the cool, waxen glow of porcelain; her mouth is set; her eyes seem fixed on some private thought. Behind her, past the juniper, a soft Tuscan landscape of water and hazy trees dissolves into the distance. This is Ginevra de' Benci, daughter of a rich Florentine banker, painted by Leonardo da Vinci around 1474 to 1478 when he was barely more than twenty — and it is the only painting by Leonardo anywhere in the Americas.

The picture we see is a fragment of a larger one. At some point, probably because of damage, the panel was cut down along the bottom, removing Ginevra's arms and hands; a surviving Leonardo drawing of delicately crossed hands is thought to record what was lost. Even trimmed, it is a startling debut. Leonardo has given a teenage girl the grave, self-contained presence of someone with a real inner life, and set her against nature with a subtlety no Florentine portraitist had managed before.

The back of the panel is painted too, with an emblem: a sprig of juniper encircled by a wreath of laurel and palm, wound about with a scroll that reads, in Latin, *VIRTVTEM FORMA DECORAT* — "beauty adorns virtue." The juniper is a visual pun, for the Italian *ginepro* echoes Ginevra's name, while the laurel and palm were the personal devices of Bernardo Bembo, a Venetian ambassador who admired her; infrared study has found his own motto hidden beneath. The portrait seems to have been caught up in one of the courtly, chaste devotions the age adored.

For centuries the panel belonged to the princes of Liechtenstein. In 1967 the National Gallery of Art in Washington bought it for around five million dollars — the highest price ever paid for a painting at the time — and a conservator carried it across the Atlantic packed in a specially fitted suitcase. It has not left Washington since.

## The World Behind It

Ginevra de' Benci lived in the same brilliant, ruthless Florence that produced Botticelli and the Medici. She was born into the wealthy Benci family — bankers close to the Medici — and was known in her own day not only for her beauty but for her intelligence and her poetry, of which a single wistful line survives. Around 1474 she married a cloth merchant, Luigi Niccolini, and the portrait may have been made to mark the wedding.

But its emblems point elsewhere, to Bernardo Bembo, the Venetian envoy to Florence, who conducted a very public, probably platonic devotion to Ginevra in the fashionable manner of the day — the sort of idealised courtly love, celebrated in poems by mutual friends, that flattered a married woman without scandal. Renaissance Florence delighted in such refined, coded relationships, and in portraits loaded with private symbolism. Leonardo's panel, with its punning juniper and its hidden motto, is a perfect specimen of that culture: a likeness that is also a compliment, a riddle, and a token in a sophisticated social game.

## The Artist at This Moment

When he painted Ginevra, Leonardo was a young man still in the shadow of his teacher. He had trained in the busy Florentine workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio and was only beginning to work on his own; this is among the earliest paintings we can confidently give to him, made before he left for Milan and before any of the works that would make him famous. Already, though, the qualities that would define him are visible — the melting transitions of light and shade, the fascination with landscape and water, the sense of a mind at work behind a face.

He was in his early to mid-twenties, ambitious, curious, and easily distracted, the illegitimate son of a notary making his way in a city crowded with talent. Portraits were a reliable source of income, but Leonardo treated even this commission as an experiment, turning the sitter to face us and dissolving the edges of her form in a way that looks forward, across thirty years, to the Mona Lisa. Ginevra is where that lifelong project quietly begins.

## Interesting Facts

- It is the only painting by Leonardo da Vinci on public view anywhere in the Americas.
- The National Gallery of Art bought it in 1967 from the Prince of Liechtenstein for about five million dollars — the highest price paid for any painting up to that time.
- The panel was cut down at the bottom, cutting away Ginevra's hands and arms; a Leonardo drawing of crossed hands is thought to preserve their lost pose.
- The reverse is painted with a wreath of laurel and palm around a juniper sprig and the motto "beauty adorns virtue" — the juniper (*ginepro*) a pun on her name.
- The laurel and palm belonged to the Venetian ambassador Bernardo Bembo, who cultivated a courtly devotion to Ginevra; his personal motto was found hidden beneath the visible emblem.
- To bring it to Washington, a gallery conservator carried the priceless panel across the Atlantic in a custom-padded suitcase.
