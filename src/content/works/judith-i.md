## The Story

A woman gazes out from a field of beaten gold, head tilted back, lips parted, eyelids heavy — the very picture of erotic rapture. Only when your eye travels to the bottom right corner do you notice what her hand is holding: the severed head of a man. This is Judith, the heroine of the Old Testament book that bears her name, who saved her besieged city by charming her way into the tent of the enemy general Holofernes, getting him drunk, and cutting off his head. Klimt painted her in 1901, sheathed in a gold choker and a transparent gown, more triumphant lover than pious widow.

That was exactly the problem. Vienna could not reconcile this brazen, orgasmic femme fatale with the virtuous biblical heroine of tradition, and so for decades critics and the public simply insisted the painting must be Salome — the other, wickeder beheader of scripture, made fashionable by Oscar Wilde's scandalous play. The misnaming stuck so stubbornly that it still turns up on gallery labels and prints today.

Klimt anticipated the confusion and tried to shut it down. He had his brother Georg, a skilled metalworker, make the picture's frame, and across the top he had the title hammered into the metal: JUDITH UND HOLOFERNES. It made no difference. Vienna wanted a Salome — a wanton temptress with no moral cover — and refused the harder, stranger truth that Klimt had painted a national heroine in the grip of sexual ecstasy. The head at her fingertips is almost an afterthought, cropped and pushed to the margin; the real subject is her face, and her pleasure. It remains one of the most unsettling images of female power ever painted, precisely because it fuses violence, sanctity, and desire into a single unbothered golden smile.

## The World Behind It

The femme fatale haunted Vienna at the turn of the century. In an era when respectable women were tightly policed and female sexuality was a source of public anxiety, artists and writers grew obsessed with the dangerous seductress who destroys men — Salome, Judith, the sphinx, the vamp. Klimt's Judith arrived in 1901, the same year his Faculty Paintings were igniting national outrage, and it fed directly into that fascination and that fear. Freud was then writing about the tangle of sex and aggression a few streets away; Klimt painted it in gold.

The gold itself was a provocation. Its beaten-gold ground borrows the visual language of sacred art — the halos and glimmering fields of Byzantine icons — and lends it to a woman flushed with murderous desire. To wrap a killing in the surfaces of holiness was pointed, even blasphemous. Small wonder a proper Viennese audience preferred to call her Salome and look away from what Klimt was really saying about their heroines, their women, and themselves.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1901 Klimt was thirty-nine and in the thick of the fight of his life. A founder of the Secession and the most talked-about painter in Austria, he was also the target of a ferocious backlash: his Faculty Painting Medicine was shown that same year to cries of pornography, and the affair was headed for parliament. Judith I comes from this combative moment, when Klimt was turning decisively away from official approval and toward the private, ornamental, unapologetically sexual style that would define the rest of his career.

It also marks the true start of his Golden Phase, the manner that would peak a few years later in Adele Bloch-Bauer I and The Kiss. Judith is among his first experiments with gold leaf, the sacred glitter he would deepen two years later after seeing the Byzantine mosaics of Ravenna. Some have speculated that the model's face belongs to Adele Bloch-Bauer herself, which would make Vienna's most notorious femme fatale the same woman the state would later try to rename — but Klimt, characteristically, never confirmed a thing.

## Interesting Facts

- The severed head at the lower right is Holofernes, the Assyrian general Judith beheaded to save her city — yet it is cropped and marginal, while Klimt makes her ecstatic face the true subject.
- Vienna insisted for decades on calling the painting "Salome," unable to accept a pious biblical heroine shown in open sexual rapture.
- To fight the misnaming, Klimt had his brother Georg forge the metal frame with "JUDITH UND HOLOFERNES" hammered into it — and the mislabeling persisted anyway.
- Judith I (1901) is one of the earliest works of Klimt's Golden Phase, which would culminate in The Kiss.
- The painting hangs in the Belvedere in Vienna; a very different later version, Judith II (1909), lives in Venice.
- The jeweled gold choker at Judith's throat became a Klimt signature, reappearing at the throat of Adele Bloch-Bauer.
