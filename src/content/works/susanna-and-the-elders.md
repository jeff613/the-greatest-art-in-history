## The Story

Two men have come over the top of a stone parapet. Their heads are almost touching. One of them presses a finger to his lips. Below them a girl is sitting naked on the ledge with nothing but a scrap of cloth across her thighs, and she has twisted her whole body away from them — one hand up in front of her face, the other pushing at the air, her head turned so far that her neck strains. There is no garden here. No fountain, no fruit trees, no bower. There is a bare wall, a hard grey step, and nowhere at all to go.

The story is an appendix to the Book of Daniel. Susanna, the wife of a rich man in Babylon, is bathing in her own garden when two elders of the community — respected men, judges — corner her and make their offer: sleep with us, or we will swear on oath that we found you here with a young lover. Adultery carried the death penalty. She refuses, they testify, and she is condemned on the word of two men of standing, until a boy named Daniel questions the elders separately and catches them naming different trees.

By 1610 European painters had been working this subject for a century, and they had turned it into a licence. Susanna was a way to sell a nude. She is usually surprised mid-bath in a lush garden, lit softly, sometimes half-amused, while the old men peer round a hedge like figures in a comedy. Nobody had painted the thing from inside her body.

Artemisia signed this one on the stone step at the lower left — ARTEMISIA GENTILESCHI, and the year. She was seventeen. It is the earliest surviving painting she signed and dated, and for a long time it was doubted: scholars read the last digits as 1616 or 1619, on the reasonable-sounding grounds that a girl that age could not have made it. An X-ray in 1970 settled the date at 1610.

The temptation is to read it as her own story, and it has been read that way — the art historian Gianni Papi has argued that the two elders stand for her father and the man who assaulted her. But the dates rule out the simplest version of that: Agostino Tassi raped her in May 1611, and this canvas was finished the year before. Whatever she understood about men closing in on a woman with no exit, she understood it first.

## The World Behind It

Rome in 1610 was a building site and an art market. Paul V was pouring money into the city, Caravaggio had fled it four years earlier under a death sentence and would die that July on a Tuscan beach, and every ambitious painter in Italy was working out what to do with the darkness he had left behind. Orazio Gentileschi, Artemisia's father, had been close enough to Caravaggio to be dragged into a libel suit with him in 1603, where he testified that he had lent the man a Capuchin habit and a pair of stage wings to paint from.

His daughter was learning inside that workshop because it was the only place she could learn. Women were barred from the academies, barred from drawing the nude model, barred from taking commissions in their own name or holding a contract. What a painter's daughter got instead was pigment to grind, panels to prepare, and whatever she could pick up standing behind her father. Three of Orazio's children were sons. None of them took to it.

## The Artist at This Moment

She was seventeen and had been running her father's household since she was twelve, when her mother Prudenzia died. She had no schooling at all — she could not yet read or write, and would not learn until Florence, several years and one trial away. Orazio wrote later to the Grand Duchess of Tuscany that in three years of study his daughter had become so accomplished that she had no equal, and it reads less like a father's boast than a sales pitch, because that is what it was.

Around this time he made an arrangement that would define the rest of her life. Perspective was the one thing his workshop could not teach her, so he hired a colleague to come to the house and give her private lessons in it. The man was Agostino Tassi, a specialist in architectural illusion whom Orazio was working alongside on a Roman commission. He was in his thirties. She was seventeen.

## Interesting Facts

- Artemisia signed only about nineteen paintings in her whole career, and this is the earliest of them — signed and dated at seventeen.
- Scholars spent decades insisting the date read 1616 or 1619 because the picture seemed too accomplished for a teenager; an X-ray in 1970 confirmed 1610.
- She returned to Susanna again and again over forty years — versions attributed to her survive in England, Italy and the Czech Republic, the last of them painted in her sixties.
- X-rays show the composition was reworked underneath, which some historians read as her father's hand in the arrangement of the figures rather than in the painting of them.
- The picture spent the eighteenth century in Germany without her name on it; the painter Benedetto Luti owned it by 1714, it entered the Schönborn collection by 1719, and it was not published as an Artemisia until an 1845 guidebook.
- It still hangs in that same private family collection at Schloss Weißenstein, in a village in Bavaria, rather than in any museum.
