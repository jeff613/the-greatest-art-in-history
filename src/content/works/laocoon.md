## The Story

Laocoön was the priest who told the Trojans not to take the horse. For that — depending on which version you read, and for other offences besides — the gods sent serpents out of the sea to kill him and his two sons in front of the whole city. It is one of the oldest images of divine unfairness in Western storytelling, and it had a famous form: the marble group dug up in Rome in 1506, all knotted muscle and heroic agony, which Michelangelo studied and which set the standard for how bodies in torment were supposed to look.

El Greco, in his seventies, painted the story as something else entirely. His Laocoön lies flat on his back on bare rock, a gaunt old man with a serpent at his face, more exhausted than heroic. One son is already down. The other twists at the left, thin as a candle flame, still fighting. The bodies are pale, boneless, stretched — nothing like marble, nothing like Michelangelo.

And behind them is not Troy. It is Toledo, plainly recognisable, with its walls and gate, painted from the city where El Greco had lived for more than thirty years. Down the hill, small and unhurried, the wooden horse is trotting toward the gate. Why the substitution has never been settled; one suggestion is a local tradition that the people of Toledo were descended from Trojans, though that is a hypothesis rather than anything El Greco wrote down.

Then there are the figures at the right, standing and watching, naked and calm. Nobody is certain who they are — Apollo and Artemis has been the usual guess. One of them is unfinished, a head and a leg and nothing else, and the group as a whole seems to have been altered. The picture was still in the studio when he died.

This is the only mythological painting by El Greco that survives. It is also one of the last things he made, and it does not look like the work of a man who was mellowing.

## The World Behind It

By 1610 Spain was visibly past its peak. The silver from the Americas had not saved royal finances, the country had defaulted on its debts more than once, and Toledo in particular had been in decline since the court left for Madrid half a century before.

Classical mythology, meanwhile, was not natural territory for a painter in Counter-Reformation Spain, where the market was overwhelmingly religious and the Inquisition took an interest in what got painted. A large canvas of nude pagan figures in agony was an unusual thing to make, and there is no record of a patron for it. It looks like a picture made because the painter wanted to make it.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was around seventy, and had been in Toledo since 1577. The court had never wanted him. He ran a workshop with his son Jorge Manuel, took on architecture and sculpture as well as painting, and sued patrons over payment with a doggedness that ran through his whole career — a long fight with a hospital at Illescas had dragged through 1607 and 1608.

His late style had gone as far from Venice as it was possible to go. Figures had stopped obeying anatomy, space had stopped being measurable, and the light came from nowhere identifiable. Whether contemporaries thought this was genius or eccentricity is not entirely clear; within a generation of his death, Spanish critics had settled on ridiculous.

He died in April 1614 with this canvas apparently still unfinished. Three centuries later, painters looking for a way out of realism found him waiting.

## Interesting Facts

- It is the only surviving mythological painting by El Greco, made at the very end of a career spent almost entirely on religious commissions.
- The city in the background is Toledo, not Troy — walls, gate and all — with the wooden horse trotting toward it.
- The picture is unfinished: one figure on the right is just a head and a leg.
- The identity of the standing figures at the right has never been settled; Apollo and Artemis is the common guess.
- El Greco's version deliberately ignores the famous antique *Laocoön* marble unearthed in Rome in 1506, which had defined how the subject was painted for a century.
- The canvas was still in his possession when he died in 1614, and no commission for it has ever been found.
