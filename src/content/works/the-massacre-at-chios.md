## The Story

Across the foreground of an enormous canvas, a group of Greek captives sits and sprawls in exhausted despair. They are wounded, half-naked, and doomed — survivors of a massacre, waiting to be killed or dragged into slavery. A dying woman slumps against a man who stares blankly into space; an old woman gazes up in numb grief; at the lower right, a naked young woman is bound by the wrist to the saddle of a mounted Ottoman soldier who wheels his horse to carry her off, while a live infant clutches at the breast of its dead mother beside her. Behind them, the island burns and distant fighting smokes on the horizon.

Delacroix based the picture on a real and recent horror. In 1822, during the Greek War of Independence, Ottoman forces descended on the island of Chios and slaughtered or enslaved much of its Greek population — tens of thousands killed, tens of thousands more sold into bondage. The atrocity outraged liberal Europe, where sympathy for the Greek cause, "philhellenism," ran high; the poet Byron would die the same year lending his fame to the Greek fight. Delacroix, twenty-six, chose this contemporary catastrophe rather than a safe scene from myth or scripture — a bold, almost journalistic decision.

He exhibited it at the Salon of 1824, where it caused a storm. Admirers saw a thrilling new kind of history painting, alive with color and suffering; hostile critics were repelled by its bleakness and its loose, un-classical handling. The veteran painter Antoine-Jean Gros, half in horror, is said to have called it "the massacre of painting." Whatever they thought, no one could ignore it. The French state bought the picture, and it helped install Delacroix, still young, as the leader of the Romantic movement.

There is a famous twist in its making. While the painting hung, nearly finished, at the Salon, Delacroix saw landscapes by the English painter John Constable, whose fresh, vivid greens and luminous skies stunned him. Electrified, he is said to have reworked the background of his own canvas in a matter of days, brightening and enlivening it under the influence of what he had just seen — a rare, documented glimpse of one master's work transforming another's in real time.

## The World Behind It

The 1820s were the decade of the Greek struggle for independence from the Ottoman Empire, and it gripped the imagination of educated Europe like few causes before it. To Romantics, the Greeks were the heirs of Homer and Pericles, fighting to throw off a foreign yoke; their suffering became a rallying point for poets, artists, and volunteers across the continent. Byron sailed to join them and died at Missolonghi; newspapers carried lurid accounts of Ottoman reprisals. The massacre on Chios was among the most notorious of these episodes.

By painting it, Delacroix planted himself in the middle of a live political passion. This was not distant history but current events, the kind of subject academic painting usually avoided. That made the work feel urgent and modern — and, to conservative eyes, dangerously raw. It also placed French Romantic painting on the side of liberty and against tyranny, a stance that would culminate a few years later in his Liberty Leading the People.

## The Artist at This Moment

Delacroix was in his mid-twenties and still building his reputation. He had made a striking debut two years earlier with The Barque of Dante, and The Massacre at Chios was his make-or-break follow-up — a huge, ambitious bid for Salon glory. He poured himself into it, studying costumes and figures, wrestling with the vast composition, and, as his letters and journal show, agonizing over whether it was any good.

The last-minute transformation of the background after seeing Constable reveals a young artist still hungry to learn, confident enough to tear up his own work when he saw something better. The gamble paid off. However divided the critics, the painting announced a major new force in French art and confirmed the direction of his whole career: toward color, feeling, and the depiction of real human suffering on the grandest scale. From here, the road led on through Sardanapalus to the barricades of 1830 and Liberty Leading the People.

## Interesting Facts

- The painting depicts the 1822 massacre on the Greek island of Chios, in which Ottoman forces killed or enslaved much of the population during the Greek War of Independence.
- After seeing John Constable's luminous landscapes at the 1824 Salon, Delacroix is said to have hastily repainted the background of his nearly finished canvas.
- The elder painter Antoine-Jean Gros reportedly recoiled from the work and called it "the massacre of painting."
- Delacroix chose a recent, real-world atrocity rather than a mythological or biblical subject — an unusually modern and political choice for a grand Salon picture.
- The French government bought the painting, and it helped establish Delacroix, still in his twenties, as the leader of the Romantic school.
