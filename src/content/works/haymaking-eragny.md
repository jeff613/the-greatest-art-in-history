## The Story

A woman stands in the front of the field with a rake, her back to a heap of cut grass, her blue skirt and dark red bodice the only strong colours for fifty yards. Behind her the meadow runs flat to a line of low hills, dotted with haycocks and with figures so small they read as marks: a man pitching hay onto a stack at the left, a girl perched on top of it, a row of workers strung out along the far edge like stitching. Nothing is happening. The hay is being got in, as it is every summer, and the light is doing what light does in the north of France in July.

What is extraordinary is how it is made. Every square inch of this canvas is built from separate touches of unmixed colour laid side by side — green against pink against pale violet — so that the field seems to vibrate rather than sit still. Pissarro was fifty-seven when he painted it, and he had adopted this laborious method only two years earlier, after meeting Paul Signac and, through him, Georges Seurat. It cost him almost everything. Canvases that had taken days now took months. Durand-Ruel, the dealer who had kept him alive for years, had little enthusiasm for the new work. Collectors who wanted Pissarros wanted the old kind.

He pressed on anyway, and in 1886 he pushed Seurat and Signac into the eighth Impressionist exhibition over furious objections — Monet, Renoir, Sisley and Caillebotte all stayed away from that show. The organisers put the newcomers in a separate room, and Pissarro hung his own dotted canvases with them, at fifty-five the oldest man in the room and the newest convert.

Then, about three years later, he abandoned it. "It was impossible to be true to my sensations and consequently to render life and movement," he explained afterwards, "impossible to be faithful to the effects, so random and so admirable, of nature, impossible to give an individual character to my drawing — I had to give up." He went back to a looser touch and never apologised for either decision. The four or five years of dots stand in the middle of his career like a room he walked through on purpose.

## The World Behind It

Éragny-sur-Epte, where the Pissarros had moved in 1884, was a village of a few hundred people in the Vexin, and the hay harvest was the year's real event. French agriculture was then in a long depression — cheap American grain, phylloxera in the vineyards, young people leaving for the cities — and the labourers in Pissarro's fields were the losing side of that story.

He knew it. He was an anarchist, a reader of Kropotkin who sent money to Jean Grave's papers, and in 1889 he would draw twenty-eight pen sketches called *Turpitudes sociales* about the misery of the poor and the indifference of the rich. But he never painted a protest. He painted the same thing his politics valued: people doing necessary work in the open air, given the same seriousness that other painters reserved for gods and generals. The rake in the woman's hands is drawn as carefully as anything in the picture.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was in the worst financial stretch of a life that had never been comfortable. The Éragny house was rented — he could not afford to buy it until 1892, and then only with fifteen thousand francs lent by Monet — and Julie, running a household of children on almost nothing, told him so regularly. His eldest son Lucien was in London, and the letters between them from these years are among the frankest documents any painter has left about doubt.

He was also, at fifty-seven, doing the thing that comes hardest to a successful artist: taking instruction from a man half his age. He called himself a "scientific Impressionist" for a while and defended Seurat in print and in company. Then his own eyes and hands told him it would not work, and he changed course again. Within two years an eye infection would end his outdoor painting altogether and send him to the windows of Paris.

## Interesting Facts

- Pissarro's pointillist period lasted only about four years, from 1885 to around 1890 — he called the results too artificial and gave it up.
- He insisted that Seurat and Signac be admitted to the eighth and final Impressionist exhibition in 1886; Monet, Renoir, Sisley and Caillebotte all refused to take part.
- He was the oldest painter in the Neo-Impressionist circle by a wide margin — Seurat was twenty-nine years his junior.
- Six of his children became painters, including Lucien, who designed the cover for his father's anarchist album *Turpitudes sociales*.
- Cézanne, who had painted beside him at Pontoise years earlier, later called him "humble and colossal."
- The canvas now belongs to the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam — Pissarro had known Vincent's brother Theo, who sold his work in Paris.
