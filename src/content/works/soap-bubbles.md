## The Story

A young man in a dusty pink coat, split open at the shoulder and frayed at the cuff, has draped himself over a stone window ledge, holding a straw in both hands, cheeks working, entirely lost in the thing he is making. The bubble hangs at the end of the straw, enormous, wobbling, catching a faint sheen of light on its skin. Off to one side a small child in a soft dark cap has climbed up on something to get his eyes over the stone and watch. A glass of soapy water stands within reach. A tangle of leaves spills over the top of the window frame.

Nothing happens. That is the whole picture. Chardin has stopped it at the instant before the obvious — the bubble is going to burst, the boy is going to laugh, the afternoon is going to end — and refused to paint any of it.

This is, by most accounts, among the very first pictures in which he painted a person. He had spent five years as an officially certified painter of animals and fruit, and according to a note published by the collector Pierre-Jean Mariette in 1749, he was stung into changing by a friend's joke. Chardin had urged the portraitist Joseph Aved to take four hundred livres for a commission; Aved answered that he would, if a portrait were as easy to paint as a sausage — a dig at the still life Chardin happened to be painting for a firescreen at the time. Whether or not the remark landed quite that way, around 1732 Chardin turned to figures, and never stopped.

He kept the subject and painted it more than once; versions survive in Washington, New York and Los Angeles, and scholars still argue over which came first. One of them hung at the Salon of 1739, and prints after the composition carried it into houses that would never see a Salon.

The subject was not new and Chardin's audience knew it. Northern painters had used a child with a bubble for over a century as a memento mori — *homo bulla*, man is a bubble, here and then not. Chardin takes the whole moralising apparatus and quietly drops it. What is left is a boy, a straw, and a fragile sphere holding its shape for a second longer than seems possible.

## The World Behind It

The fashionable French painting of the 1730s was going in the opposite direction at speed. Boucher was making his name with mythologies, nymphs, and pink flesh; the walls of Paris townhouses wanted charm, movement, and no weight at all. Against that, a picture of an unnamed adolescent blowing bubbles at a window was almost a provocation by understatement.

What made it possible was the print trade. Chardin was slow — he produced only a handful of pictures a year — but engravers reproduced his compositions and sold them cheaply, often with a few lines of verse underneath drawing out a moral the painting itself declines to state. Those prints, more than the paintings, built his reputation across France and abroad, and they are why his kitchen maids and card-playing boys became familiar images to people who owned no art at all.

There was also a Dutch and Flemish taste running underneath fashionable Paris: collectors bought Teniers and Rembrandt, and a French painter working in that register had a market waiting, even if the Académie's official hierarchy had no respect for it.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was in his early thirties and had just, finally, got a household. After an eight-year engagement he had married Marguerite Saintard in 1731; a son, Pierre-Jean, was baptised that November, and a daughter, Marguerite-Agnès, followed in 1733. It was the shortest happy stretch of his life. His wife was already ill, and she would be dead by 1735; the daughter died two years after that.

Professionally he was stuck. The Académie had him filed under animals and fruit and there was no procedure for promotion. Turning to figures was not an act of ambition so much as of stubbornness — a demonstration, made without any announcement, that the man who could paint a copper pot could paint a face too.

He painted these figures the way he painted objects: slowly, from memory as much as from a model, building surfaces until they seemed to have been breathed onto the canvas rather than brushed. Contemporaries who watched him work said they could not see how it was done.

## Interesting Facts

- The subject descends from a Dutch and Flemish emblem, *homo bulla* — "man is a bubble" — a warning about how briefly life holds its shape.
- Mariette's note of 1749 records the taunt that supposedly pushed Chardin into figure painting: the portraitist Joseph Aved said he would work cheaply if a portrait were as easy to paint as a sausage.
- Chardin painted at least three versions of this composition; the ones in Washington, New York and Los Angeles differ in size and detail, and there is no settled agreement on their order.
- One version was exhibited at the Paris Salon of 1739, and engravings after it circulated widely.
- The Washington canvas is the largest of the surviving versions, nearly a metre high, and came to the National Gallery of Art as a gift in 1942.
- Chardin worked so slowly that a few finished pictures a year was normal for him — one reason his output is small and the prints after it were so important.
