## The Story

He is looking straight out at you, and he has arranged the picture so that you cannot get away from it. The head is turned hard over the shoulder, the mouth set, one eyebrow raised. The top of the skull and the bottom of the torso are both sliced off by the edges of the panel, so the face is shoved forward like something held up against a window. Beside him hang the drooping stems of a physalis — the winter cherry, the Chinese lantern plant — its papery orange husks the only warm thing in a picture otherwise built from bone white, black, and a bruised green.

The panel is small, roughly thirty-two by forty centimetres, oil and gouache on a board you could carry under one arm. Schiele was twenty-two when he made it in 1912, and he did not make it alone. It is one half of a pair. The other half is *Portrait of Wally Neuzil* — the same size, the same pale ground, the same cropping — and the two were painted to hang facing one another, so that her eyes turn toward him and his turn back toward her. They have hung together in Vienna ever since.

Walburga Neuzil was seventeen when she met Schiele in 1911. She had modelled for Gustav Klimt before she came to him, and by the time these panels were painted she had been living with him for a year, sitting for him, keeping house, and following him out of two towns that would not tolerate the pair of them. Nineteen twelve was the year that went badly wrong: in April the police came to their rented house at Neulengbach, took Schiele away, and carried off more than a hundred drawings as evidence. Wally waited it out, wrote to him, and brought him what he needed.

The physalis is a bright, hollow thing — a paper lantern grown around a single berry — and it is easy to read the fruit as a comment on the man beside it. Schiele left no explanation. What he did leave is the arrangement: two small panels made to look at each other for good. Three years later he married someone else, and Wally never saw him again.

## The World Behind It

Vienna in 1912 was the capital of an empire that had six years left to live, a city where Freud was writing about the unconscious a tram ride from the courtroom that would jail a painter for a wall of drawings. It ran on that contradiction. The Secession had already dragged Austrian art into the twentieth century, and Klimt's gilded women hung in the best houses in town; the same society expected its young men to keep their appetites decently framed.

Schiele's generation refused the frame. He had walked out of the Academy of Fine Arts in 1909 with a handful of other students and founded the Neukunstgruppe, the New Art Group, on the argument that art schools taught obedience. What he wanted from a picture was the body without the flattery — nerves, joints, blotched skin, the gaze that will not soften.

The provinces liked this even less than Vienna did. Schiele and Wally had already been driven out of Krumau in Bohemia, his mother's birthplace, for living together unmarried and using local girls as models. They moved to Neulengbach, twenty miles west of the capital, and the same story caught up with them within the year.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was twenty-two and had just been in prison. In April 1912 he was arrested and charged over a runaway teenage girl who had turned up at the house; the abduction and seduction charges collapsed once the circumstances were examined, and what remained was a conviction for keeping erotic drawings where children could see them. The sentence was twenty-four days, twenty-one of which he had already served waiting for trial. A judge burned one of the confiscated drawings in the courtroom.

He came out in May, angry and famous in the worst way, and went back to Vienna. He was also, by then, unmistakably good. Klimt had been buying his drawings and steering collectors toward him since 1907, and the pictures of this year — the tight, cropped panels, the deliberate awkwardness — are the work of a man who has stopped trying to be liked. He and Wally would stay together three more years.

## Interesting Facts

- The plant is *Physalis alkekengi*, the Chinese lantern or winter cherry: each orange husk is a papery case grown around a single berry.
- The panel's twin, *Portrait of Wally Neuzil*, is almost exactly the same size and was painted as its counterpart; both are in the Leopold Museum in Vienna.
- That twin became one of the most litigated paintings of the twentieth century. Taken from the Viennese Jewish dealer Lea Bondi Jaray in 1939, it was seized by New York prosecutors in 1998 after a loan to the Museum of Modern Art, and the case ran for more than twelve years.
- It ended on 20 July 2010, when the Leopold Museum paid Bondi's heirs nineteen million dollars. Under the settlement the picture must always be shown with a label explaining how it was taken.
- Rudolf Leopold, the collector who built the museum around his Schieles and fought the case to the end, died on 29 June 2010 — three weeks before the settlement was announced.
- Schiele signed the panel in block capitals at the lower right, EGON SCHIELE 1912, the stamp-like monogram he used through these years.
