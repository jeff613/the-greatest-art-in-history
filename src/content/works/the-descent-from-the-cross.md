## The Story

A pale, broken body slides down from the cross into a tangle of reaching hands, wrapped in a long white sheet that one of the mourners grips in his teeth to keep from slipping. This is the moment after death, before the tomb, and Rubens makes it almost unbearably physical: the dead weight of Christ, the strain in every arm, the way the whole composition seems to pour diagonally down toward the ground. At the center, a young man in a brilliant red cloak — the apostle John — braces the body against his shoulder, while the Virgin reaches up in blue and Mary Magdalene, golden-haired, cradles Christ's bloodied feet.

The painting is the central panel of a great triptych, and it came from an unlikely patron: the Confraternity of the Arquebusiers, Antwerp's militia guild of musketeers. Their dean was Nicolaas Rockox, a burgomaster of the city and one of Rubens's closest friends, and it was almost certainly his hand that steered the commission to the painter freshly home from Italy. The contract was signed in 1611, and Rubens delivered a work that announced a new kind of religious art for the Catholic revival — muscular, emotional, and staged like theatre.

There was a private joke buried in the subject. The guild's patron saint was Christopher, whose name means "Christ-bearer," the giant who carried the infant Jesus across a river. Rubens took that idea of bearing Christ and ran it through the entire triptych: on the outer shutters, Saint Christopher wades across with the child on his shoulder; inside, the pregnant Mary carries Christ to Elizabeth on one wing and presents him at the temple on the other; and in the center, grieving hands bear his dead body down from the cross. Every panel is someone carrying Christ. Four centuries later the altarpiece still hangs where it was made, in the cathedral it was painted for.

## The World Behind It

Antwerp in 1611 was a wounded city trying to heal. Decades of religious war had split the Low Countries in two: the Protestant north broke away to become the Dutch Republic, while the south, including Antwerp, stayed Catholic and under Spanish rule. In 1566 and after, Calvinist mobs had smashed the images out of the churches, and the great cathedral had stood stripped and bare. A fragile truce signed in 1609 finally brought peace, and the Catholic Church launched a campaign to fill its emptied walls with new, emotionally gripping art that could win hearts back to the faith.

Rubens was the perfect instrument for that campaign. He had just returned from Italy steeped in the drama of the Counter-Reformation, and Antwerp's guilds and clergy competed to hire him. The Arquebusiers were not churchmen but a civic militia, yet like every guild they maintained an altar and wanted it to outshine their neighbors'. Commissioning the city's most talked-about painter for their chapel in the cathedral was a statement of wealth, piety, and pride all at once.

## The Artist at This Moment

Rubens was in his mid-thirties and riding a wave that would not break for the rest of his life. Only three years back from Italy, newly appointed court painter to the Spanish archdukes who governed Antwerp, and recently married to Isabella Brant, he was assembling the workshop that would make him rich. Commissions arrived faster than any single man could paint them, and he was already building the celebrated house and studio that survives today as the Rubenshuis.

The Descent, painted alongside its companion the Raising of the Cross, was the pair of works that sealed his reputation at home. They proved he could take everything he had learned from Italian art and make it his own — grander, warmer, more human. He was no longer a promising talent returned from abroad; he was Antwerp's master, the painter every patron in northern Europe now wanted, and the diplomat and courtier he would become was already visible in the ease with which he moved among burgomasters and princes.

## Interesting Facts

- The commission came from the Arquebusiers, Antwerp's civic militia of musketeers, whose dean Nicolaas Rockox was a close friend of the painter — his portrait appears on one of the triptych's wings.
- The guild's patron was Saint Christopher, the "Christ-bearer," so Rubens made every panel a scene of someone physically carrying Christ.
- The white shroud is the compositional key: one mourner clenches it in his teeth to keep Christ's body from slipping, a startlingly real touch.
- The altarpiece still hangs in Antwerp Cathedral, its original home, more than four hundred years after it was installed.
- Napoleon's troops looted the panels and carried them to Paris in 1794; they were returned to Antwerp after his fall.
- Rubens painted it in his early thirties, freshly home from eight years in Italy, and it launched him as the dominant painter of northern Europe.
