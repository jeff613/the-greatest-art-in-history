## The Story

A quarter-century after finishing the ceiling, Michelangelo came back to the same chapel to paint its altar wall, and this time he painted the end of the world. More than three hundred figures churn across a stormy blue void: Christ rises at the center like a thunderbolt, arm raised, and the whole of humanity swirls around him — the saved hauled upward toward heaven, the damned dragged and clawing down into hell, where Charon ferries them across the Styx and demons wait. It is a vision of terror and judgment with almost no comfort in it, painted on the wall behind the altar where every pope would say Mass beneath it.

What scandalized Rome was the flesh. Michelangelo painted nearly everyone — saints, angels, sinners, the dead climbing out of their graves — stark naked, their bodies twisting in fear and hope. To him the nude was the noblest subject in art, the soul made visible in muscle and bone. To many churchmen it was obscene. The pope's own master of ceremonies, Biagio da Cesena, toured the unfinished fresco and sniffed that it belonged in a public bathhouse or a tavern, not a papal chapel.

Michelangelo took his revenge in paint. He worked Biagio's face into the very bottom corner as Minos, the donkey-eared judge of the underworld, his nakedness wrapped by a snake that bites down on his genitals. When Biagio stormed to Pope Paul III to demand the insult be removed, the pope is said to have laughed that his authority did not reach into hell — so there Biagio stays, damned for eternity by a wounded genius. The fresco was unveiled in 1541 on the eve of All Saints' Day, exactly twenty-nine years to the day after the ceiling above it.

## The World Behind It

The Last Judgment is a picture of a Church under siege. It was commissioned as the Protestant Reformation was tearing Europe apart and just a few years after 1527, when mutinous imperial troops had sacked Rome itself, raping and looting the holy city for months. The confident, expansive faith of the ceiling had curdled into something darker and more defensive, and Michelangelo's swirling, merciless judgment matched the mood of a Rome that felt God's wrath was near.

That anxiety soon turned on the painting. The Counter-Reformation Church, closing ranks at the Council of Trent, decided that sacred art must be decent above all, and the fresco's nudity became intolerable. Even before Michelangelo died, a campaign was underway to cover it. The painter Daniele da Volterra — ironically a devoted friend of Michelangelo's — was hired to paint draperies and loincloths over the offending parts, earning the mocking nickname *Il Braghettone*, "the breeches-maker." Later hands added still more. Restorers in the 1990s removed some of the coverings but left others, now historic, in place.

## The Artist at This Moment

Michelangelo was past sixty when he began the wall, no longer the combustible young man of the ceiling but an aging, devout, melancholy artist preoccupied with death and salvation. Pope Clement VII had ordered the fresco; Clement died, and his successor Paul III insisted the work go ahead. In these years Michelangelo was writing anguished religious poetry and had formed a deep spiritual friendship with the noblewoman and poet Vittoria Colonna, whose piety colored his thinking about sin, judgment, and grace.

His self-portrait is buried in the fresco, and it is a bleak one: he painted his own sagging face onto the empty, flayed skin that the martyr St. Bartholomew holds dangling in his hand — a hollow, discarded husk hanging over the abyss. It is one of the strangest signatures in art, a great man depicting himself not in glory but as a shed skin, unsure whether he will be saved. The reluctant painter of the ceiling had become an old master staring hard at his own mortality.

## Interesting Facts

- Biagio da Cesena, the papal official who called the fresco fit only for taverns, is painted into hell as Minos with donkey ears and a snake biting his groin. The pope reportedly refused to remove him, joking that hell was outside his jurisdiction.
- Michelangelo hid a grim self-portrait in the flayed skin held by St. Bartholomew — his own features drooping from an empty human hide.
- After his death, the nudes were partly painted over with drapery. The artist who did it, Daniele da Volterra, was nicknamed *Il Braghettone* — "the breeches-maker."
- Daniele da Volterra, who covered the nudes, also painted the portrait of Michelangelo now used on this very site — he was a loyal follower, not an enemy.
- The wall was unveiled on 31 October 1541, precisely twenty-nine years after the ceiling above it was revealed on 31 October 1512.
- Christ is shown beardless and youthful, more like a classical Apollo than the traditional Judeo-Christian figure — another choice that unsettled contemporary critics.
