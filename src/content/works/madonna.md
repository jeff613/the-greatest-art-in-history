## The Story

Munch called her a Madonna, but there is no child, no halo of gold, no holiness in the churchly sense. A bare-breasted woman leans back with her eyes closed and one arm behind her, caught in a swoon that is unmistakably sexual — and, Munch insisted, sacred at the same time. He first titled the picture *Loving Woman*, and he described the instant it shows in raw terms: the moment of conception, when "life reaches out its hand to death," when a new life is created and the woman is most beautiful and most doomed at once. Around her head glows not a golden ring but a blood-red band, an anti-halo that fuses ecstasy, procreation, and mortality into a single image.

Munch returned to the composition again and again, painting five versions between 1892 and 1895 and pulling prints from a lithographic stone. The most notorious is one of the prints. There he wrapped the woman in a painted border of wriggling spermatozoa, their tails trailing round three sides of the frame, and tucked a shrivelled, embryonic foetus — huge-eyed, cadaverous — into the lower-left corner. It made explicit what the paintings only implied: that the woman is not a lover but the entire terrifying cycle of sex, birth, and death, personified. Audiences of the 1890s were scandalised, which was rather the point.

The painting hanging in Oslo, our version, has its own dramatic history. On a Sunday morning in August 2004, masked, armed men walked into the Munch Museum, forced staff and visitors to the floor, cut this Madonna and a version of The Scream from the wall, and drove off in a black Audi. The two paintings vanished for two years before police recovered them in 2006. Madonna came back with a tear in the canvas — but restorable, and home.

## The World Behind It

Madonna was conceived in Berlin, where Munch spent the early 1890s among a circle of writers and free-thinkers who gathered at a wine bar they nicknamed the Black Piglet. The playwright August Strindberg drank there; so did the Polish writer Stanisław Przybyszewski, whose magnetic Norwegian wife, Dagny Juel, fascinated the whole group and is often linked to this image. It was a hothouse of talk about sex, the unconscious, Nietzsche, and the dangerous power of women.

Out of that world came the fin de siècle's favourite and most anxious figure: the *femme fatale*, the woman as both irresistible and destroying. Munch's Madonna is the Symbolist version of her, stripped of anecdote and raised to the level of myth. By borrowing the most sacred title in Christian art and giving it to a naked woman in the throes of desire, he deliberately collapsed the era's rigid line between the holy and the carnal — a provocation that felt, to many viewers, like blasphemy.

## The Artist at This Moment

In the mid-1890s Munch was assembling the Frieze of Life, his epic sequence on love and death, and Madonna was one of its central panels, meant to hang near The Scream and Vampire. He was in his early thirties, chronically short of money, ricocheting between Berlin, Paris, and Norway, and living in a state of romantic torment that fed directly into his art. His attitude toward women swung between worship and dread — he longed for them and feared they would consume him.

He was also becoming a formidable printmaker. Lithography and woodcut let him multiply and mutate his key images, and the Madonna prints show him pushing an idea past what a single painting could say, ringing the sacred body with the biological machinery of reproduction and decay. It was the most fertile and reckless decade of his life, and nearly everything for which he is now remembered took shape in it.

## Interesting Facts

- Munch originally called the picture *Loving Woman* and described it as the moment of conception, "when life reaches out its hand to death."
- He painted five versions between 1892 and 1895; the halo around her head is red, not gold — ecstasy and death rather than sanctity.
- One lithographic version is framed by a border of swimming spermatozoa, with a gaunt, foetus-like figure in the lower corner.
- The title deliberately provoked: draping Christianity's holiest name over a naked woman in erotic surrender struck many contemporaries as blasphemous.
- This Munch Museum version was stolen at gunpoint in 2004 along with a version of The Scream, and recovered in 2006 with a repairable tear in the canvas.
