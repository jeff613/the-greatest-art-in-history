## The Story

A red-haired girl props herself against a great white pillow, her face already turned toward something the grieving woman beside her cannot see. The woman's head is bowed, her hand clasped in the child's, unable to look up. There is almost nothing else — a glass on a table, a shrouded window — and that emptiness is the point. This is Edvard Munch painting the most unbearable memory of his life: the deathbed of his sister Sophie, who died of tuberculosis in 1877 at fifteen, while thirteen-year-old Edvard looked on. Their mother had died of the same disease years before. Death by consumption was the weather of his childhood.

Munch did not paint what a camera would have seen; he painted what grief had done to the memory. He built the picture up and then attacked it, scraping the paint back with a knife, scratching lines into the wet surface, letting drips run like tears down the canvas so that the whole image seems to be dissolving even as it forms. He worked on it for a year, calling it a struggle to recover a first, true impression. "I don't paint what I see," he said, "but what I saw." He came to regard it as the hinge of his whole career: "With The Sick Child I broke new ground — it was a breakthrough in my art. Most of what I did later was born in this picture."

When he showed it at Kristiania's Autumn Exhibition in 1886, simply titled *Study*, the public was appalled. Visitors jeered at what looked to them like a smeared, half-finished daub. Critics savaged it; the influential Andreas Aubert warned that Munch had genius but was in "danger of going to the dogs," and dismissed the canvas as little more than a rough sketch half scraped away. Only a few, like the bohemian writer Hans Jæger, defended it. Munch never let the motif go.

## The World Behind It

Tuberculosis stalked nineteenth-century Kristiania, and no family, rich or poor, was safe from it; there was no cure, and a diagnosis was a slow sentence. Munch's father was an army doctor whose faith curdled into a bleak, fearful piety, and the household treated illness and death as ever-present companions. The subject of a dying child would have been painfully familiar to every viewer at the 1886 exhibition.

That is partly why the painting enraged them. Norwegian art of the day prized polished, legible naturalism — a sad scene should be tenderly and clearly finished. Munch instead offered a surface that looked violent and incomplete, refusing to console. He was importing something new: the idea that a picture's job was to transmit raw feeling and memory rather than a tidy record of appearances. It was, in embryo, the argument that would make him a father of Expressionism and echo through the century that followed.

## The Artist at This Moment

Munch was only twenty-two or twenty-three, barely emerged from art school and still financially dependent, when he staked everything on this canvas. He had fallen in with the Kristiania Bohème, the anarchic circle around Hans Jæger who urged artists to mine their own lives without shame — permission, in effect, to paint his sister's death.

The hostile reception wounded him but did not deter him; if anything it confirmed that he was doing something the safe painters were not. He had himself nearly died of tuberculosis as a boy, and the guilt of the survivor runs through the picture. He would paint the same dying girl five more times over the next forty years, in versions scattered from Gothenburg to London, returning to the pillow and the bowed head again and again. The Sick Child was less a single picture than a wound he kept reopening, and the first proof that his own suffering could become his greatest material.

## Interesting Facts

- The dying girl is a memory of Munch's sister Sophie, who died of tuberculosis at fifteen in 1877, watched by the thirteen-year-old artist.
- Munch scraped, scratched, and let the paint run for a year, chasing not what he saw but what he remembered seeing.
- Shown as *Study* in 1886, it was jeered by the public and attacked by critics as an unfinished, botched sketch.
- The critic Andreas Aubert conceded Munch's genius but warned it was "in danger of going to the dogs."
- Munch painted six versions of the subject between 1885 and 1927, calling the first one the breakthrough of his entire career.
