## The Story

Leonardo painted The Last Supper on the end wall of a monastery dining room, so that the friars of Santa Maria delle Grazie would eat their own meals in the presence of Christ's. Duke Ludovico Sforza of Milan paid for it, and the subject was the oldest in Christian art. Leonardo chose to freeze the single most electric instant of the story: the moment Jesus says, "One of you will betray me," and thirteen men explode into shock, denial, and dread. Judas recoils into shadow, clutching his bag of silver; Peter reaches angrily for a knife; each apostle becomes a separate study of a soul caught off guard.

Getting there nearly drove the monastery's prior to distraction. Leonardo did not paint like other muralists. He would arrive, stare at the wall for an hour, add a single stroke, and leave. He roamed Milan's streets hunting for the right face for Judas, and when the prior complained about the delays, Leonardo dryly offered to use the prior's own head for the traitor. He worked slowly because he was inventing as he went — and because he insisted on a technique that let him revise.

That decision was the tragedy of the picture. A true fresco has to be painted fast, into wet plaster, in sections that can never be reworked. Leonardo wanted the depth and subtlety of oil, so he painted onto dry plaster instead, sealing the surface with his own experimental mixture. It let him blend and correct like a panel painting. It also doomed the work. Within twenty years the paint was flaking; a visitor in 1517 already called it "beginning to be ruined." What hangs on that wall today is a ghost — reassembled, retouched, fought over for centuries — and it is still, somehow, one of the most powerful images ever made. Its fragility is now part of its fame: a masterpiece that has been dying, slowly and in public, for five hundred years.

## The World Behind It

Milan in the 1490s was one of the richest courts in Europe, and Ludovico Sforza was a usurper who badly wanted legitimacy. He poured money into artists and engineers, and Santa Maria delle Grazie was his pet project — he meant the church to become the Sforza family mausoleum, and the refectory decoration was part of glorifying his dynasty. Leonardo was his star import, employed as much for festivals, fortifications, and hydraulic schemes as for painting.

The mural's later history reads like a catalogue of near-destruction. In 1652 the monks cut a doorway through the bottom of the wall, slicing off Christ's feet. Napoleon's soldiers reportedly stabled horses in the room and threw things at the apostles' heads. Then, on the night of 15 August 1943, Allied bombs flattened much of the refectory — but the wall bearing the painting, sandbagged in advance, was left standing amid the rubble under the open sky.

## The Artist at This Moment

Leonardo was in his forties and at the peak of his powers when he took on the commission, a decade or more into his long Milanese employment. He was juggling everything at once: designing the duke's pageants, dissecting corpses to understand how the body moves, drafting plans for a giant bronze horse in honor of Ludovico's father. The horse never got cast — the bronze was seized to make cannon when war loomed — and much of what he began in Milan ended in frustration.

The Last Supper was different because he refused to hurry it, and for three years he had the freedom to obsess. Then, in 1499, the world he had built in Milan collapsed. French armies invaded, Ludovico was toppled and eventually died a French prisoner, and Leonardo packed up and left the city. He never saw his greatest wall painting age gracefully; by the time he was an old man in France, word was already reaching him that it was falling apart.

## Interesting Facts

- Leonardo's decision to paint on dry plaster, rather than in true wet fresco, is why the work started deteriorating within his own lifetime — a witness described it as already spoiling in 1517, only two decades after completion.
- The most exhaustive restoration in its history ran for twenty-one years, from 1978 to 1999, led by Pinin Brambilla Barcilon, who worked to strip away centuries of grime and the repaints of earlier "restorers."
- In 1652 someone cut a new doorway through the wall, destroying the portion of the painting that showed Christ's feet.
- On 15 August 1943 an Allied air raid demolished much of the refectory; the painted wall survived only because it had been protected in advance with sandbags and scaffolding.
- When the prior of the monastery nagged him about how slowly he worked, Leonardo suggested he could model the face of Judas on the prior himself — and the complaints stopped.
- Only about a fifth of the paint now on the wall is thought to be Leonardo's own; the rest is the work of restorers across five centuries trying to hold the image together.
