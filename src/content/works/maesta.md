## The Story

When Siena commissioned Duccio to paint an altarpiece for the high altar of its cathedral, it was not ordering a picture so much as a portrait of its own soul. The city had dedicated itself to the Virgin Mary, and the Maestà — "Majesty" — shows her enthroned in glory, the Christ Child on her lap, ringed by tiers of angels, saints, and apostles who lean and gaze like a heavenly court. It was painted on both sides: the front a single monumental vision of the enthroned Virgin, the back a dense grid of small scenes from the life of Christ, so that the priests behind the altar had their own gallery of the Passion. Nothing so ambitious had been attempted in Italian painting.

We know almost exactly how it entered the world, because a Sienese chronicler recorded the day. On 9 June 1311 the finished panel was carried from Duccio's workshop to the Duomo in a great procession. The shops were locked shut; the bishop led a company of priests and friars; behind them came the government of the Nine, every civic official, and "all the populace," candles lit in their hands, women and children following "with much devotion." They wound the great panel in a loop around the Campo, "sounding all the bells in glory," with hired trumpeters, bagpipers, and castanet players. A whole city walked a painting to church.

Its later fate was crueller. For centuries the Maestà stood on the altar, until fashion turned against it and, in 1771, it was taken down and dismembered. To split its two painted faces apart, workers roughly sawed the great structure into pieces, sparing neither the crowning panels nor the narrow predella below. The fragments scattered. Today the altarpiece survives in more than thirty pieces across ten collections in five countries — the bulk reunited in Siena's cathedral museum, but stray predella scenes marooned in London's National Gallery, Washington's National Gallery of Art, and New York's Frick. The heaven Siena once carried through its streets now hangs in exile, room by room.

## The World Behind It

Siena in 1311 was a proud, wealthy republic of bankers and wool merchants, and the Virgin was quite literally its patron and protector. Half a century earlier, on the eve of the Battle of Montaperti, the city had formally dedicated itself to her and then routed its great rival Florence — a victory it credited to Mary herself. To be "the city of the Virgin" was Sienese identity, stamped on coins and carved over gates.

That is why an altarpiece could stop a whole town. The Maestà was not private devotion but public statement, paid for by a commune locked in perpetual rivalry with Florence for wealth, power, and artistic glory. Its ranks of saints include the four patrons of Siena kneeling in the foreground, pleading the city's case before the throne. When the Sienese paraded the panel around the Campo and sang it into the cathedral, they were parading themselves — their piety, their money, and their conviction that heaven was, in some sense, on their side.

## The Artist at This Moment

The Maestà was the summit of Duccio's life, and he was paid like it — a daily wage plus materials that added up, by the end, to a small fortune in florins. He worked on it for around three years, almost certainly leading a busy workshop of assistants under his design, since a structure so large and so uniformly refined could not have come from one pair of hands.

And yet the same records that track these grand payments also track his debts. Even as the city lavished money on him for its holiest commission, Duccio remained the man perpetually behind on what he owed, fined for old obligations, tangled in loans. By 1313, two years after the triumph of the procession, he still appears in the archives as a debtor. He died a few years later so encumbered that his heirs refused the estate. The painter who gave Siena its most exalted image of order and glory could never impose either on his own affairs.

## Interesting Facts

- A contemporary Sienese chronicle records that on 9 June 1311 the shops were shut and every bell rung as the whole city processed the panel to the cathedral, hiring trumpeters and pipers to accompany it.
- It was painted on both sides — a glorious enthroned Virgin at the front, dozens of small Passion scenes on the back — so it could be seen by congregation and clergy alike.
- In 1771 the altarpiece was taken down and roughly sawed apart to separate its two faces, cutting up even the small predella scenes.
- The Maestà now survives in more than thirty fragments spread across ten collections in five countries; stray predella panels ended up in London, Washington, and New York.
- Duccio's four foreground saints are Siena's own patrons, kneeling to intercede for the city before the Virgin's throne.
- The enormous fee did not save him from debt: within a few years of finishing it Duccio died owing so much that his children renounced their inheritance.
