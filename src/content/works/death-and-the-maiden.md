## The Story

A woman kneels on a crumpled white sheet and holds on with both arms. Her face is pressed to the chest of a man in a dark monkish robe, her red hair spread across him, her hands locked behind his back. He does not hold her. One of his hands rests on her head — the gesture of a priest, or of somebody steadying a thing he is about to put down — and his eyes are open, staring past her into nothing. His face is Schiele's own. The ground beneath them is not a bed but a landscape of stony, folded shapes that could be rock or bunched cloth, and the two figures lie in it as if they had already been dropped there.

Schiele painted this canvas, a metre and a half by nearly two, in 1915. He first called it *Man and Girl*, and also *Entwined People*; the title everyone uses now, borrowed from an old European motif of Death coming for a young woman, came later. The red-haired woman is Wally Neuzil, who had been his model and his companion since 1911, and the picture is what he made out of the end of it.

The end was his doing and it was cold. By early 1915 he had decided to marry Edith Harms, a respectable young woman from a family who lived across the street from his studio. In February he wrote to a friend that he intended to marry — "advantageously," and not to Wally. Then he arranged to meet Wally at the café where he played billiards most days and handed her a written proposal: that after his marriage the two of them should still take a holiday together every summer, without Edith. She read it, refused, and walked out. They never saw each other again.

He married Edith on 17 June 1915. Three days later the army called him up. Wally trained as a nurse, went to Dalmatia with the Red Cross, and died of scarlet fever on Christmas Day 1917, aged twenty-three; her grave was not identified until a century afterwards. Whatever the painting was meant to be when he started it, it turned into an accurate one.

## The World Behind It

Austria-Hungary had been at war for a year and was losing the shape of itself. The empire had gone into 1914 expecting a short punitive campaign against Serbia and was now feeding its men into the Carpathians and the Isonzo by the hundred thousand. Conscription reached deep: men who had been passed over in 1914 were being combed back through the medical boards by 1915, and Schiele, previously rejected on health grounds, was taken.

Vienna itself was not yet starving, but the mood had curdled. The Secession was old news, Klimt was in his fifties, and the young men who had made the noise before the war were in uniform or dead. Death as a subject stopped being a Symbolist affectation and became reportage.

The motif Schiele reached for was ancient — Death and the Maiden, the skeleton embracing a girl, worked over by German printmakers since the sixteenth century and set to music by Schubert. What he did with it was strip out the allegory. There is no skeleton here and no scythe, only a man with the painter's own face declining to hold the woman who is holding him.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was twenty-five, newly married, and about to be a soldier. Edith Harms and her sister Adele lived with their parents in the building across the street from his Vienna studio, and the Harms family took a dim view of the painter opposite; the courtship was carried on partly by note, and by most accounts Wally was the one who carried some of them. He got the wedding and lost the four-year relationship that had carried him through prison, poverty, and two towns that threw him out.

The war treated him better than it treated almost anyone. He was never sent to the front. He guarded Russian prisoners, escorted transports, worked as a clerk, and by January 1917 had been transferred back to Vienna with light duties and time to draw. His reputation kept growing while other men died. But the paintings of these years keep circling the same subject — bodies holding each other and not being held — and this is the first and hardest of them.

## Interesting Facts

- Schiele's own titles for the canvas were *Man and Girl* and *Entwined People*; the familiar title attaches it to a motif that runs from Renaissance woodcuts to Schubert's string quartet.
- The man's face is Schiele's, and the Belvedere identifies the woman as Wally Neuzil — making this the painter's own account of a breakup he chose.
- The proposal Wally refused was in writing: an annual summer holiday together after his marriage, without his wife.
- She died of scarlet fever on 25 December 1917 while nursing at a military hospital in Dalmatia, aged twenty-three. Her grave, in Sinj in what is now Croatia, was only identified in 2017.
- The picture is not a bed scene: the two figures lie on a stony, sheet-like ground that gives them nothing to rest on.
- The Belvedere acquired the painting in 1930, twelve years after Schiele's death, from an exhibition in Munich.
