## The Story

He has his back turned. That is the whole trick, and it is why the picture will not leave anyone alone.

A man stands on a knuckle of rock in a dark green coat, one boot forward, a cane in his right hand, reddish hair pushed sideways by the wind. Below him the valleys have filled with fog until only the tops of things break the surface — spurs of sandstone, a far blue ridge, one distant peak. He is looking at it. We are looking at him look at it, which means the one thing the painting is actually about, his face, is the one thing withheld.

The view is a fiction assembled from real stone. Every formation in it has been identified in the Elbe Sandstone Mountains southeast of Dresden: the Zirkelstein on the right, the Rosenberg or the Kaltenberg on the left, the Gamrig near Rathen in the foreground, the Kaiserkrone under his boots. But they do not stand in these relations to one another. Friedrich walked that country for years and drew it piece by piece, then built a summit that does not exist and put a man on top of it.

Who the man is has never been settled. Some point to the red hair and call it a self-portrait in disguise. The art historian Joseph Koerner argued for a specific dead man — Friedrich Gotthard von Brincken, a Saxon officer who appears to have been killed in the wars against Napoleon — which would turn the picture from a meditation into an epitaph. What is beyond argument is the coat. It is *altdeutsche Tracht*, old German dress, taken up by young patriots as a deliberate insult to French fashion and read by the authorities of the day as a political costume worn in public.

Then, for a century, nothing. The painting has no traceable history at all until 1939, when it turns up in a Berlin dealer's gallery. It reached the Hamburger Kunsthalle only in 1970, bought at the urging of the museum's director Werner Hofmann, who was in the middle of hauling Friedrich's reputation back out of the ground. Within a generation the man on the rock was on book jackets, record sleeves and coffee cups, and then on every screen in the world — the most reproduced image German Romanticism ever produced, and still nobody has seen his face.

## The World Behind It

The years around 1817 were a long national hangover. The armies that had thrown Napoleon out expected a unified Germany with constitutions to show for it; what the Congress of Vienna handed back was a loose confederation of thirty-nine princes, most of them determined to pretend nothing had happened. Students formed fraternities, marched to the Wartburg in October 1817 to mark the anniversary of Luther and of the victory at Leipzig, and burned reactionary books on a bonfire. Two years later a student murdered the playwright August von Kotzebue, and Metternich seized the excuse: the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 brought censors, informers and dissolved fraternities, and made old German dress a thing worth reporting a man for.

Saxony, where Friedrich lived, had backed Napoleon nearly to the last and lost a large part of its territory to Prussia in the settlement. Dresden was a diminished capital in a chastened kingdom, and its most peculiar painter was producing pictures of solitary figures looking out at country that did not quite exist.

## The Artist at This Moment

At forty-three he had done something nobody expected of him: he got married. On 21 January 1818 he married Caroline Bommer, twenty-five, the daughter of a Dresden dyer. Friends noticed the effect at once. His colours warmed, and women in long dresses began appearing in his landscapes, seen from behind like everyone else in his work. That summer he took Caroline north to Greifswald and Rügen to meet the family and show her the Baltic coast he had grown up on.

It was as close to a good period as he ever had. The Berlin Academy had elected him in 1810 after the royal purchase, the Dresden Academy had taken him in with a small annual stipend, and he had a circle — the Norwegian painter Johan Christian Dahl, the physician-painter Carl Gustav Carus, the poet Ludwig Tieck.

None of it made him easy. Visitors described a silent, gaunt, watchful man given to long depressions, who did not explain his pictures and did not much want them explained to him.

## Interesting Facts

- Every rock in the painting has been matched to a real formation in the Elbe Sandstone Mountains, but the summit the man stands on is an invention — the pieces have been rearranged.
- The picture has no documented history whatsoever before 1939; the Hamburger Kunsthalle only acquired it in 1970.
- Its date is genuinely unsettled: the museum labels it around 1817, most books say 1818, and there is no document to decide it.
- The green coat is *altdeutsche Tracht*, nationalist dress that the post-1819 authorities treated as a political statement.
- Joseph Koerner proposed that the figure is a memorial to a specific Saxon officer killed fighting Napoleon, which would make the painting a patriotic epitaph — a reading that remains a theory.
- The back-turned figure, the *Rückenfigur*, runs through Friedrich's entire body of work; here it became the single most copied gesture in European landscape painting.
