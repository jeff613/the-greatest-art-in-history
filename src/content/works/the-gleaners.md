## The Story

Three women bend over a shorn field, picking up the single stalks of wheat the harvesters left behind. Nothing happens. No one looks at us. And when Millet showed this quiet picture at the Salon of 1857, part of Paris reacted as if he had mounted a guillotine in the exhibition hall.

To understand why, you have to know what gleaning was. It was not a picturesque country custom but legislated poverty: an ancient right, regulated by royal edict since the sixteenth century, that allowed the certified poor of a village to enter the fields only after the harvest was cleared, only between sunrise and sunset, and take what the reapers had dropped. Gleaners were the bottom of the rural order — landless women gathering, stalk by stalk, enough for a loaf of bread. Millet knew them from childhood. In the sunlit distance of the painting he set the world they were excluded from: loaded wagons, fat haystacks, a crowd of harvesters, and a steward on horseback keeping watch over the master's grain.

The scandal was scale and dignity. A canvas this size was for gods, kings, and battles; Millet filled it with three monumental laboring bodies, painted with the gravity other artists reserved for saints. Nine years after the revolution of 1848, with the bourgeoisie still flinching at the word "socialism," that felt like a threat. Critics called the women ugly, pretentious, dangerous. Paul de Saint-Victor sneered that these gleaners posed as "the Three Fates of pauperism," and another writer saw behind them "the pikes of '93" — the Terror itself. Millet, who insisted he was painting the human condition rather than a manifesto, sold the picture for a modest 3,000 francs.

The ending is pure irony. In 1889, at the sensational Secrétan auction, the once-reviled canvas was bought for 300,000 francs by Jeanne-Alexandrine Pommery, the widow who ran the great Champagne house — and she left it to the Louvre. The painting that had frightened the rich became a national treasure, donated by one of the richest women in France.

## The World Behind It

France in 1857 was Napoleon III's Second Empire: authoritarian, prosperous on the surface, and haunted by the June days of 1848, when Paris workers had died on barricades. The propertied classes read every image of the poor as a political statement, and an art exhibition was one of the few public arenas where such statements could slip past the censors. At the same time the countryside itself was in crisis. Mechanization and the consolidation of farms were squeezing out the landless laborers at the bottom, and gleaning — that medieval safety valve — was being contested and restricted by landowners who saw no reason to leave grain in the field.

Millet's Barbizon neighbors lived this reality. The village sat among the great grain plains of the Chailly region, and the artist walked out to watch the harvest every year. What he painted was not nostalgia but reportage of a fading right. The critics who attacked the picture understood it perfectly: in an empire built on order and property, three women exercising the last legal claim of the poor on the land looked less like folklore than like an accusation.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1857 Millet was forty-three, eight years into his life at Barbizon, and still poor. He lived in a low farmhouse with his companion Catherine Lemaire — they had married in a civil ceremony in 1853 — and a house full of children, eventually nine. His days were split like a peasant's: painting in the morning, working his garden in the afternoon to feed the family. He owed money to his colorman, his baker, and his friends, and begged advances from dealers for pictures not yet painted.

The Gleaners was the summit of a decade of studies — he had drawn and etched gleaning women for years, refining the three poses until they moved like figures in a frieze. The hostile reception stung, and the painting's low sale price stung worse. Yet he did not soften his subject matter; within two years he finished The Angelus, another image of laborers in a bare field. Recognition, medals, and rising prices were still a decade away, and Millet spent this period doing exactly what his painting shows: gathering, patiently, what a hard system let fall.

## Interesting Facts

- Gleaning was a legal right of the certified rural poor, permitted only after the harvest, between sunrise and sunset — the mounted steward in the painting's background is there to enforce the rules.
- The critic Paul de Saint-Victor mocked the three women as posing like "the Three Fates of pauperism," and other reviewers linked the picture to the revolutionary Terror of 1793.
- Millet sold the painting for about 3,000 francs; thirty-two years later, at the 1889 Secrétan sale, it fetched 300,000 — a hundredfold increase he never lived to see.
- Its 1889 buyer was Jeanne-Alexandrine Pommery, the Champagne widow, who bequeathed it to the Louvre; it moved to the Musée d'Orsay in 1986.
- The composition inspired countless homages and parodies, and Agnès Varda built her acclaimed 2000 documentary The Gleaners and I around the painting.
