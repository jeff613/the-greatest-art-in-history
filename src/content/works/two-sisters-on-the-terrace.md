## The Story

A young woman in a dark blue dress and a red-trimmed straw hat sits on a terrace above the river, a small girl in blue leaning against her, a basket of brightly colored wool balls resting beside them like a spilled bag of sweets. Behind them, blurred into a haze of green and blue, is the foliage and water of the Seine. It is one of Renoir's most immediately charming pictures — and almost everything the title tells you about it is probably wrong.

The two are very likely not sisters. The elder was identified by the Renoir scholar François Daulte as Jeanne Darlot, an eighteen-year-old who went on to become an actress; who the little girl was, nobody knows — her name has simply vanished. Even the title seems not to be Renoir's own: the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel recorded the painting in his books as "Two Sisters," and the label stuck. Renoir painted the pair in the spring of 1881 on the balcony of the Maison Fournaise at Chatou — the same riverside restaurant where, at almost exactly the same time, he was assembling the crowd of his Luncheon of the Boating Party.

The picture is really about color and youth: the red of the hat, the blue of the dresses, the little heap of wool in scarlet, green, and blue, all set against the shimmering softness of the background. Durand-Ruel bought it on the 7th of July 1881, only weeks after Renoir finished it, and the following spring it hung in the seventh Impressionist exhibition. It has been delighting viewers ever since — a portrait of two people who were, in all likelihood, strangers to each other and to us.

## The World Behind It

Chatou, on the Seine just west of Paris, was a favorite Sunday escape for city people in the early 1880s, reached in minutes by train and given over to boating, lunching, and idling by the water. Renoir spent that spring working on its balconies, and the same warm riverside light that fills the Boating Party glows behind the two "sisters."

Just as important to this picture is the man who bought it. Paul Durand-Ruel was the dealer who gambled his fortune on the Impressionists when almost no one else would, buying their work in bulk through lean years and slowly building a market for it. His willingness to snap up canvases like this one, straight from the studio, is a large part of why Renoir and his friends survived at all — and why the movement eventually conquered the world's museums. Without dealers like him, pictures this cheerful might never have been painted at all.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1881 Renoir was forty and on the move, both literally and artistically. He was painting the sunlit riverside scenes that crowned his Impressionist years, yet he was also growing dissatisfied, and that same year he set off for Italy to study Raphael and the ancient frescoes of Pompeii — a journey that would harden his outlines and cool his color in the decade to come. Pictures like this one, fresh and softly brushed, belong to the last flowering of his purely Impressionist style.

They also belong to a hopeful moment in his fortunes. With Durand-Ruel buying steadily and his reputation slowly rising, the years of real poverty were finally ending, and Renoir could afford to travel, to experiment, and to take his time. Within the decade he would marry Aline Charigot, one of his models, and begin the settled family life that anchored his later years and gave him three sons to paint.

## Interesting Facts

- Despite the title, the two are almost certainly not sisters; the older girl was identified as the future actress Jeanne Darlot, and the younger child remains unidentified.
- The name "Two Sisters" appears to have come from the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel's records rather than from Renoir himself.
- It was painted on the terrace of the Maison Fournaise at Chatou, the same restaurant seen in Luncheon of the Boating Party, around the same time.
- The bright basket of wool balls beside the sitters is one of the picture's most memorable touches of pure color.
- Durand-Ruel bought the painting within weeks of its completion, in July 1881, and showed it at the seventh Impressionist exhibition the next spring.
- After passing through several collections it was bequeathed to the Art Institute of Chicago in 1933, where it remains one of the museum's best-loved paintings.
