## The Story

A wild-eyed giant, wreathed in darkness, has crammed a headless human body into his mouth and is biting down. Blood runs from the stumps. His eyes bulge with a madness that looks less like appetite than terror. This is Saturn — the Roman god the Greeks called Cronus — who, warned by prophecy that one of his children would overthrow him, devoured them one by one as they were born.

But the truly disturbing thing about Saturn Devouring His Son is where Goya put it. He did not paint it for a church, a palace, or a patron. Around 1819, ageing and deaf, he bought a country house outside Madrid and began covering its interior walls, directly on the plaster, with fourteen images of pure dread — witches, drowning pilgrims, a snarling dog vanishing in a void, and this cannibal god. We call them the Black Paintings. Saturn is thought to have been painted on the wall of a room he ate his meals in. They were never titled by him, never exhibited, never explained to anyone. They were private hauntings, made by a man who had stopped painting for the world.

Because they were painted on plaster, the works nearly died with the house. Half a century after Goya's death, an owner had them painstakingly transferred onto canvas — a brutal operation that damaged and altered them. Old accounts and an early photograph suggest the Saturn we see today lost details in the process; the figure may once have been even more explicit and grotesque. What survives is fragment enough. Rescued from a crumbling wall, the god eating his child entered the Prado, where it remains one of the most frightening images ever hung in a public museum.

## The World Behind It

By 1819 the Spain around Goya offered little comfort. The hopes of liberal reform had curdled under the restored absolute monarchy of Ferdinand VII, a vindictive king who revived the Inquisition and hunted down those who had cooperated with the French. Goya, who had served every side, had reason to feel watched and unsafe. He withdrew to the Quinta del Sordo — the House of the Deaf Man, a name it happened to carry before he arrived but which fit him exactly.

Cut off from sound and increasingly from society, Goya turned his home into a private theater of the mind. The Black Paintings share the mood of a country and a man exhausted by war, disease, and repression: they brood on death, superstition, folly, and appetite. Saturn, the old god who consumes his own future, can be read a hundred ways — as time destroying all it creates, as tyranny devouring its people, as an old man's dread of his own end. Goya left no key. He simply lived among these visions, ate beneath them, and said nothing.

## The Artist at This Moment

Goya was in his mid-seventies, stone deaf, and had recently survived a second near-fatal illness that left him weak and, by his own account, closer to death than life. He had largely given up his court duties. The buoyant, worldly painter of royal portraits had become a recluse working on his own walls with no thought of sale or display — perhaps the first artist in history to make major works purely for himself.

There is a raw, private violence to the Black Paintings that comes from exactly this freedom. No patron would have accepted them; no public was meant to see them. Goya painted fast and thickly, sometimes with palette knife and thumb, dragging figures out of blackness. Within a few years the atmosphere of Ferdinand's Spain would drive him out of the country altogether, into exile in France, where he would die. He left the nightmares behind on the plaster, unsigned and unspoken.

## Interesting Facts

- Goya painted it directly onto the plaster wall of his own house, not on canvas, and never intended anyone to see it.
- It is one of fourteen "Black Paintings" that covered the walls of his home, the Quinta del Sordo; none were titled by Goya himself.
- The murals were transferred from wall to canvas around 1874 by the restorer Salvador Martínez Cubells — a damaging process that altered the images.
- An early photograph and accounts from before the transfer suggest the original figure was even more graphic than the version now in the Prado.
- The subject comes from Roman myth, but Goya's raving, terrified god turned it into something closer to a study of madness itself.
