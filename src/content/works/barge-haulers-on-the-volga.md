## The Story

On a summer day around 1868 Repin was on a pleasure steamer going up the Neva with a fellow student when a dark clot appeared on the bank ahead. It resolved into men — a line of them, leaning into leather straps, hauling a barge against the current. Around him was a deck full of people on a day out. Repin, by his own account, could not stop staring. *People harnessed instead of cattle*, he remembered thinking.

He made sketches and threw them away; they were pictures of a shock, not of anyone in particular. So in May 1870, with two hundred roubles that the landscape painter Fyodor Vasilyev extracted from the Society for the Encouragement of Artists and travel money from the Academy, he went down the Volga with Vasilyev, another student and his own brother and stayed the summer at Shiryaevo, below the Zhiguli hills. He did not sketch the haulers from a distance. He drank with them, walked with them, and drew them one at a time.

That is why the picture is not a wall of misery but eleven separate men. At the front of the strap is Kanin, a defrocked priest who had been on the rope about ten years; Repin dated the study of his head 19 July 1870 and wrote later that he seemed a colossal mystery, a man of dignity with a rag tied round his head. Behind him a red-bearded bruiser leans in. The one bent lowest, staring straight out of the canvas with a look somewhere between exhaustion and contempt, was known as Ilka the Sailor. Near the middle, in a bright pink shirt, is the boy Larka, new to the work, skin not yet burned dark, shrugging at his harness — Repin thought him cleverer than the older men. One of them is a Kalmyk. One is a discharged soldier, the only man in boots.

Three years later the finished canvas made him famous. Grand Duke Vladimir Alexandrovich, the tsar's son, had seen the Volga studies in 1870 and commissioned the big picture on the spot; he paid three thousand roubles and hung it in his billiard room, which did not stop it going to the Vienna World's Fair of 1873 and taking a bronze medal. Dostoevsky wrote about it approvingly, and what he approved of was precisely that it did not preach: real barge haulers, he said, and nothing more. Repin also remembered a government official rounding on him for painting a mode of transport the ministry was busy abolishing — a story that comes from his own memoir, and reads like one.

## The World Behind It

Serfdom had been abolished nine years before Repin went to the Volga, and it had freed almost nobody. Former serfs came out of emancipation owing redemption payments on land they had worked for generations, and hundreds of thousands of them took whatever seasonal work the rivers offered. Hauling was the bottom of that market: a summer on the strap for wages that would not carry a family through a winter, done by men who had been discharged, disgraced, ruined or simply squeezed off the land.

The Volga was the empire's main artery, and steam tugs were already displacing the *burlaki* — which was part of the official embarrassment about them. They were a scene from a century that Russia wanted to declare over. Meanwhile the young intelligentsia was working itself up to go the other way, out of the universities and into the villages to find the real people, a movement that would break over the country a few years later.

The Academy of Arts, for its part, still ranked subjects. Peasants belonged in genre painting: small, tidy, picturesque. Repin gave them nine feet of canvas and painted them filthy.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was twenty-six when he went to Shiryaevo and still a student. He had grown up in a military settlement, apprenticed to an icon painter, and come to the capital on nothing; he did not need a theory to understand what he was looking at on that riverbank.

He was also, at exactly the same time, playing the Academy's game and winning it. In 1871 he took the Great Gold Medal with *The Resurrection of Jairus's Daughter*, a large, correct, biblical machine of the kind the professors wanted, and with it a six-year travelling stipend. In 1872 he married Vera Shevtsova and went back down the Volga with her for more studies. So the *Barge Haulers* was built in the gaps of an obedient academic career, and the year it went to Vienna he left for Italy and then Paris on the Academy's money. He came home three years later an Academician — and the picture everyone wanted to talk about was still the one with the rope.

## Interesting Facts

- Repin named the men. Kanin, the defrocked priest at the head of the strap, Ilka the Sailor staring out at the viewer, the boy Larka in the pink shirt and the old soldier Zotov in his boots were all real haulers he drew individually on the Volga.
- The 1870 expedition was funded by two hundred roubles that the painter Fyodor Vasilyev talked out of the Society for the Encouragement of Artists, plus travel money from the Academy.
- Grand Duke Vladimir Alexandrovich, son of Alexander II, paid three thousand roubles for it and hung Russia's most famous painting of destitution in his billiard room.
- It won a bronze medal at the Vienna World's Fair in 1873; a German critic called it the sunniest picture in the whole exhibition.
- Dostoevsky praised it in his *Diary of a Writer* for showing real barge haulers and nothing more, and counted that refusal to preach the artist's greatest merit.
- Far out on the water behind the men, a small steamer trails smoke across the horizon, doing the same job with an engine.
