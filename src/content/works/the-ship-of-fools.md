## The Story

The boat has no rudder, no sail and no course. It sits low in shallow green water at the edge of a flat landscape, and the people in it are too busy to notice. A monk and a nun lean across a table, mouths wide open, roaring a song at each other over a lute the nun is playing. A man behind them throws his head back and bawls along. Two more, a nun among them, have their hands out of the way and are lunging with open mouths at a pale wafer that hangs from a string — an old fairground game, played here in the middle of the water. On the table there is a dish of cherries and a tin cup, and that is the whole of the provisions.

Everything else in the picture is going wrong at once. One man leans right out over the gunwale to swing a pewter flagon down into the water. Two swimmers have come up to the hull and are holding out a bowl, begging. In the branches above, a jester in an eared hood sits with his back turned to the party, a fool's sceptre with a little carved head over his shoulder, drinking quietly from his own bowl — the one passenger who appears to know what kind of vessel this is. The mast is not a mast: it is a living tree, still in leaf, with a bough lashed across it. A dead fowl has been tied to the trunk, and a man has shinned up with a knife between his hands to cut it free.

And near the top, in the darkest part of the foliage, almost invisible until you find it, an owl is looking out of the leaves at you. Owls turn up all through Bosch's work, and what they mean there — wisdom, night, heresy, death, or simply a bird that watches — is still argued about.

The panel is a fragment. Some time after Bosch's death somebody sawed the picture up, and what hangs in Paris is roughly the top two-thirds of what was once the left wing of a triptych. The bottom third of that wing, a fat man riding a floating barrel while others tip drink into him, is at Yale. The right wing, *Death and the Miser*, is in Washington. The centre panel is gone. Whatever the whole thing said, we are reading two-thirds of one sentence of it.

## The World Behind It

The ship of fools was in the air. In 1494 a Basel lawyer named Sebastian Brant published *Das Narrenschiff*, a long verse catalogue of human idiocy in which the fools of the world board a boat for a paradise that does not exist. It was a runaway bestseller — reprinted, pirated, translated across Europe within a few years. Bosch's boat looks like an illustration of it, and may be. It may equally be that both men were drawing on the same well-worn stock of carnival imagery, in which mock ships were dragged through the streets on wheels and the feast of fools let servants, drunks and apprentices run the town for a day.

The joke about the monk and the nun singing over their wine had a long life too. Anticlerical humour was not a Protestant invention; the church's own preachers made it, loudly, and Bosch's town was full of clergy who ate well. What is missing from the painting is the sermon. Bosch shows you the boat and lets you work out that it is drifting.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was in his forties, and by the standards of his profession he had arrived. He had married into property, he sat among the sworn members of the Brotherhood of Our Lady with the town's leading men, and he had begun signing his panels with the name of his town rather than his family's.

He was also, by now, a supplier. Small moralising panels like this one — cheap enough to reach a merchant's house, sharp enough to be repeated — were exactly what the Netherlandish market wanted, and Bosch's workshop and a growing crowd of imitators produced them for decades after he died. A great deal of what once passed for Bosch is not by him, and the number of paintings scholars will still accept as his own hand has been falling for a century; roughly twenty-five survive.

This one was overlooked for a long time. It was in private hands and largely unremarked until the twentieth century, when a Louvre curator, Camille Benoît, acquired it and gave it to the museum in 1918.

## Interesting Facts

- The panel is a fragment: the lower third of the same wing is *Allegory of Gluttony* at Yale, the opposite wing is *Death and the Miser* in Washington, and the centre panel is lost.
- An owl is hidden in the foliage of the tree-mast, staring straight out — one of the birds that recur across Bosch's work with no agreed meaning.
- The mast is a living tree with a leafy bough for a yard-arm, and a dead fowl tied to it that one of the passengers is climbing up to steal.
- The wafer swinging on a string is a real game: hands behind the back, catch it with your teeth.
- Sebastian Brant's *Das Narrenschiff*, published in Basel in 1494, was one of the first international bestsellers of print; whether Bosch was illustrating it or drawing on the same carnival tradition is unresolved.
- The jester in the branches, back turned to the feast with his fool's sceptre over his shoulder, is the only figure not joining in.
- It came to the Louvre as a gift from the curator Camille Benoît in 1918.
