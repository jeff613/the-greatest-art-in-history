## The Story

For years Klimt worked on the portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer, filling more than a hundred preparatory sketches before he touched the canvas. When he finished in 1907, he had produced the ultimate statement of his Golden Phase: a young Viennese woman dissolving into a blaze of gold and silver leaf, her pale face and hands the only fully human notes in a shimmering field of eyes, spirals, and Egyptian symbols. Adele was the wife of Ferdinand Bloch-Bauer, a Jewish sugar magnate, and the only society woman Klimt ever painted twice; there is a persistent, unprovable rumor that the two were lovers.

Adele died young, of meningitis, in 1925. In her will she expressed a wish that her husband, on his own death, leave the family's Klimt paintings to the Austrian state gallery. It was a request, not a legal command — and history would turn on that distinction. When Nazi Germany annexed Austria in 1938, Ferdinand fled, and the Nazis looted his home and collection, seizing the golden portrait of his late wife. It was handed to the Belvedere, which quietly retitled it "Woman in Gold" to erase the Jewish name of its sitter. Ferdinand died in exile in 1945, leaving his estate to his nieces and nephew.

Decades later one of those nieces, Maria Altmann — by then an elderly refugee living in Los Angeles — set out to recover the paintings. Austria refused. With the young lawyer E. Randol Schoenberg she sued, and in 2004 the United States Supreme Court ruled in Republic of Austria v. Altmann that she could pursue her claim in American courts. Rather than face trial, Austria agreed to binding arbitration in Vienna, and in January 2006 a panel ordered five Klimts, including Adele Bloch-Bauer I, returned to the family. Later that year Ronald Lauder bought the portrait for his Neue Galerie in New York for a reported $135 million — at the time the highest price ever paid for a painting.

## The World Behind It

Adele Bloch-Bauer belonged to the world of assimilated, cultured Jewish Vienna that made Klimt possible. Families like the Bloch-Bauers and the Lederers were his great patrons; they bought his most daring work, sat for his portraits, and gave the Secession its money and its nerve. Their salons mixed industrial wealth with Mahler, Freud, and the avant-garde, and their commissions freed Klimt from ever needing the state again. Adele herself ran a salon, read philosophy, and moved among socialists and intellectuals — unusual for a woman of her class.

Within a single generation that entire world was destroyed. The same Vienna that had celebrated the golden portrait cheered the Anschluss of 1938, and the families who had commissioned Klimt were robbed, driven out, or murdered. The looting of the Bloch-Bauer collection was not an aberration but policy, repeated across Jewish Europe. When Austria fought for sixty years to keep the painting — even renaming it to hide who Adele was — it was defending a lie about its own past. Maria Altmann's victory forced a reckoning that reached far beyond one gilded canvas.

## The Artist at This Moment

Klimt painted Adele Bloch-Bauer I at the very center of his Golden Phase, the same years that produced The Kiss. He was in his mid-forties, rich, and in enormous demand from precisely the families whose wives he now immortalized in gold. The Faculty Paintings scandal was behind him; he had renounced public commissions and built a career answering only to private patrons like the Bloch-Bauers, who let him do exactly as he pleased.

The portrait shows how far he had traveled. He had visited Ravenna in 1903 and been overwhelmed by its Byzantine gold mosaics, and here he turned a living woman into something like a Byzantine empress or a secular icon, half-flesh and half-ornament. It was a way of painting he could not sustain forever — within a few years he would move toward looser, more colorful work influenced by younger artists — but in 1907 the gold was everything. Adele, who would die of meningitis in 1925, less than twenty years after the portrait was finished, was fixed here at the height of both their lives, unaware of the storm the picture would one day survive.

## Interesting Facts

- Klimt made well over a hundred preparatory studies and worked on the picture for roughly three years, from about 1903 to 1907.
- Adele is the only society woman Klimt painted twice; a second, less golden portrait followed in 1912.
- After seizing it, the Nazis retitled the work "Woman in Gold," stripping away the Jewish name of its Jewish sitter.
- In Republic of Austria v. Altmann (2004), the US Supreme Court let an Austrian-born refugee sue a foreign government in American courts; a Vienna arbitration panel returned five Klimts to the family in 2006.
- Ronald Lauder paid a reported $135 million for the portrait in 2006 — then the highest sum ever paid for a painting — and hung it in New York's Neue Galerie, where it remains.
- The saga became the 2015 film Woman in Gold, with Helen Mirren as Maria Altmann.
