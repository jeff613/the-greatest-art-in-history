## The Story

A rehearsal is winding down in a studio of the Paris Opéra. A cluster of young dancers fidgets, stretches, scratches, adjusts a slipper or a ribbon at the back — anything but the graceful poses the stage demands. At the center, leaning on a long white stick, stands the ballet master: Jules Perrot, a real and legendary choreographer of the Romantic era, here nearly sixty-five and painted from memory as a stern, weary authority presiding over the chaos of children. This is not the ballet the public paid to see. It is the work behind it: the drudgery, the boredom, the aching feet of a demanding trade.

The girls in the picture were known, without affection, as *petits rats* — "little rats" — the youngest students of the Opéra's ballet school, most of them drawn from the Parisian poor. Their families sent them to dance because it might, with luck, become a wage. Degas was fascinated by exactly this gap between the shimmering illusion onstage and the grinding reality that produced it, and he returned to the dancers again and again for the rest of his life, more than any other subject.

What the painting only whispers, its first viewers understood at a glance. The ballet world of the Opéra was also a marketplace. Wealthy male subscribers, the *abonnés*, bought the right to roam backstage and into the rehearsal rooms, where they watched, courted, and often "protected" the young dancers — a polite word for arrangements that shaded into prostitution. Degas usually keeps these men just out of frame, but their presence hangs over the scene. The exhausted children are on display for someone. That cool, knowing detachment — beauty and exploitation held in the same steady gaze — is what makes his dancers so different from anyone else's, and so quietly unsettling.

## The World Behind It

In the Paris of the 1870s the Opéra was a pillar of fashionable society, and its ballet was as much a social institution as an art form. The studios in this picture belonged to the old opera house on the rue Le Peletier, which burned down in 1873; soon after, the lavish Palais Garnier opened its doors. In both buildings the same system reigned. The *abonnés* — season-ticket holders, almost all of them men of money — enjoyed access to the *foyer de la danse* and the wings, mingling freely with performers in a way no ordinary spectator could.

For the *petits rats*, ballet was a rare ladder out of poverty, and also a trap. A dancer's tiny salary rarely covered a family's needs, and the attentions of a rich protector could mean the difference between hunger and security. Everyone knew it, and polite Paris preferred not to say it aloud. Degas, moving through this world on the strength of friendships with musicians and subscribers, saw it plainly and put it, unspoken, into paint.

## The Artist at This Moment

Degas painted this in his late thirties, in the years just after the Franco-Prussian War and his long visit to relatives in New Orleans. It was around this time that his father died and the family's banking fortune collapsed into debt, forcing a man raised in comfort to earn his living by his brush. The pressure seems only to have concentrated him.

He had recently found the subject that would define him. The ballet offered everything he wanted: modern life, artificial light, bodies caught in unglamorous motion, and endless chances to study the same gestures over and over. He worked not outdoors like his Impressionist friends but in the studio, from drawings and memory, arranging his figures with a draftsman's cold precision. His eyesight had already begun to trouble him during the war — a shadow that would slowly darken across the decades — which may have sharpened his hunger to record the fleeting, ordinary movements most people never bother to see.

## Interesting Facts

- The commanding figure with the stick is a real person: Jules Perrot, one of the great choreographers of Romantic ballet, painted well past his prime.
- The young dancers were nicknamed *petits rats* — "little rats" — and most came from poor families for whom the Opéra was a rare shot at a wage.
- Wealthy male subscribers called *abonnés* paid for the right to go backstage and mingle with the dancers, an open secret Degas hints at but never spells out.
- The studio shown is in the old opera house on the rue Le Peletier, which burned to the ground in 1873.
- Degas made the dancers his signature subject, returning to them in hundreds of paintings, pastels, and sculptures across his career.
- Unlike his Impressionist colleagues, he refused to paint from life outdoors, building these scenes in the studio from memory and careful drawing.
