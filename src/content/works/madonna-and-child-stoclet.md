## The Story

It is tiny — barely eleven inches on its longest side, small enough to hold in two hands. This was never an altarpiece but a private devotional panel, made for someone to pray before, alone, in a room. The Virgin holds her son, and he reaches up to push aside her veil and touch her face, while she gazes out at us with a tender, faintly sorrowful calm. Across the whole bottom of the picture Duccio painted a parapet — a little illusionistic ledge that the Virgin seems to sit behind, one of the earliest attempts in Western painting to open a real, measurable space between the holy figures and the person kneeling before them. It is a small revolution worked at a small scale.

You can still read its centuries of use in its wounds. Along the bottom edge of the original frame the wood is scorched and blistered — burn marks left by the devotional candles that generations of owners set beneath it, burning close, night after night. Few paintings carry such intimate evidence of the private prayer they were made to serve. When curators later examined a cluster of similar panels, it was partly this three-dimensional parapet, and this authentic history of wear, that marked Duccio's little Madonna out as the real thing among imitations.

Its modern story is nearly as dramatic. The panel was found, unattributed, in a dealer's shop by the Russian count Gregori Stroganoff, then passed to the Belgian collector Adolphe Stoclet, whose family kept it for over half a century — hence its nickname, the "Stoclet Madonna." In the autumn of 2004 the Metropolitan Museum of Art acquired it, and although the museum never officially confirmed a figure, the price was widely reported at around $45 million — which would make this palm-sized panel the most expensive single object the Met had ever bought. Its director called it one of the great acquisitions of the past half-century. Only around thirteen paintings by Duccio are thought to survive anywhere in the world.

## The World Behind It

Around 1300 a growing number of well-off lay people wanted the holy brought home — not the vast public altarpiece of a cathedral but a small, precious image for personal prayer in a private chamber. Duccio's little Madonna is exactly that: portable devotion for someone with the means to commission the best. The gold, the costly pigments, and the sheer refinement made it a luxury object as much as a spiritual one.

What makes it quietly radical is the parapet. For a thousand years sacred figures had floated in flat gold eternity, deliberately outside ordinary space and time. By setting the Virgin behind a ledge that projects toward the viewer, Duccio began to place her in the same world as the person praying — a first, tentative step toward the illusion of real space that would preoccupy Italian painting for the next two hundred years. In a picture you could cradle in your palms, one of the central problems of the coming Renaissance is already being posed.

## The Artist at This Moment

The panel is usually dated to the 1290s, placing it in Duccio's middle years — after the triumph of the Rucellai Madonna had established him, but before the vast Maestà that would consume the end of his career. It shows him experimenting: the same painter who could fill a cathedral wall was also thinking hard about intimacy, about the small format and the near view, about how to draw a single kneeling worshipper into the presence of the divine.

This was the height of his powers and, as ever, of his disorder. These were the years when the Sienese records catch him deserting the city's war in the Maremma and refusing to swear loyalty to its captain, racking up the fines and debts that trailed him to the grave. The man who painted this small, grave, tender image of a mother and child for someone's private prayers was, in his own civic life, chronically unreliable — a genius the authorities kept fining even as they kept hiring him.

## Interesting Facts

- The panel is only about eleven inches tall — a private devotional image, not an altarpiece.
- The bottom of its original frame is scorched from the candles owners burned beneath it over the centuries.
- Its painted parapet is among the earliest attempts in Western art to create a real sense of space between holy figures and the viewer.
- It was found unattributed in a dealer's shop by Count Gregori Stroganoff before passing to the Stoclet family, whose name it now carries.
