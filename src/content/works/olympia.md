## The Story

When Olympia went up at the Paris Salon of 1865, the crowd did not simply dislike it — it wanted to attack it. Visitors jeered, laughed, and, by the accounts that came down afterward, jabbed at the canvas with walking sticks and umbrellas, until the administration hung it high above a doorway, out of arm's reach, and reportedly posted guards to keep it safe. Manet's friend Antonin Proust later claimed the picture survived only because of those precautions. Some of this may be embroidered anecdote, repeated until it hardened into legend, but the core is beyond dispute: no painting of the century provoked a nastier public rage.

What had he done? On the surface, almost nothing new. A nude woman reclines on a bed while a servant brings flowers — a pose lifted directly from Titian's revered *Venus of Urbino*, a picture hanging safely in the museums. But Manet drained away every excuse the old masters had offered. His woman is not a goddess or a nymph. She is unmistakably a modern Parisian courtesan, named Olympia after a fashionable prostitute's alias, wearing a black ribbon at her throat, a bracelet, a silk slipper dangling from one foot. And she does not drowse or avert her eyes in ideal modesty. She looks straight out at the viewer — cool, appraising, entirely unashamed — as if pricing the gentleman who has come to gawk.

That level, businesslike stare was the true offense. A nude was acceptable so long as she pretended not to know she was being watched; Olympia knew exactly, and did not care. The bouquet at her side is the tribute of a client, delivered by her Black maid; the bristling black cat at the foot of the bed replaces the sleeping dog of fidelity in Titian's picture with something sharper and more knowing. Critics called the model a corpse, a gorilla, a female Yahoo. What genuinely unsettled them was that a bourgeois audience recognized the transaction on the wall — and recognized themselves as the buyers.

## The World Behind It

Second Empire Paris was a city being rebuilt for pleasure and display, and prostitution was one of its enormous, half-hidden industries, tolerated, taxed, and everywhere. Respectable men frequented courtesans as a matter of course; respectable society agreed never to say so aloud. Manet's crime was to say it aloud, in paint, at the most public art event in France. Olympia held up a mirror to the Salon's own well-dressed visitors and showed them the commerce they preferred to keep in the shadows.

The Salon was then the single great engine of an artist's career, drawing hundreds of thousands of visitors and making or breaking reputations. To be accepted was everything — and Olympia was accepted, which made the scandal official rather than fringe. That a jury had let such a thing in, and that the public then had to be protected from its own fury, turned a single canvas into a referendum on what modern painting was allowed to show.

## The Artist at This Moment

Manet was thirty-three, and the Olympia storm nearly broke him. He had wanted the Salon's approval more than almost anything, and instead he had become the most ridiculed artist in France, mocked in the papers and caricatured in the streets. Deeply shaken, he wrote to his friend the poet Charles Baudelaire almost pleading for reassurance; Baudelaire told him, bracingly, that he simply had to be equal to his own talent.

Soon after, Manet fled the city for Spain, where he steeped himself in Velázquez and Goya and gathered his nerve. He had painted Olympia two years earlier, in 1863, the same fertile year as his other great provocation, and had held it back, perhaps sensing what it would unleash. The abuse did not turn him into a rebel by temperament — he went on courting the Salon for the rest of his life — but it made him, against his own wishes, the reluctant figurehead of everything new in French art.

## Interesting Facts

- The model was Victorine Meurent, Manet's favorite sitter, who was herself a painter and later exhibited at the very Salon that had mocked her — in 1876 her own work was accepted in a year when Manet's was rejected.
- The pose is closely modeled on Titian's *Venus of Urbino* (1538), turning a beloved Renaissance nude into a pointed portrait of modern prostitution.
- "Olympia" was a stylish alias used by real Parisian courtesans, so the title itself signaled the woman's profession to contemporary viewers.
- Reports that the picture had to be rehung out of reach and guarded against umbrella-wielding visitors come largely from Manet's circle and may be partly exaggerated, but the ferocity of the crowd is well documented.
- The painting stayed in Manet's studio, unsold, until his death; admirers led by Claude Monet raised a public subscription in 1890 to buy it for France.
- The black cat, arched and staring, replaces the little sleeping dog of marital fidelity in Titian's original — a sly reversal that period audiences read at once.
