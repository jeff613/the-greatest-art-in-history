## The Story

Five naked women confront you from a shallow, splintered space of jagged blue and pink: two at the center stare out with blank, almond-eyed calm, while the figures at the edges wear faces that have curdled into masks — hacked, striped, inhuman. There is no soft flesh, no seductive curve, no comfortable place for the eye to rest. The bodies are chopped into sharp planes, the drapery shatters like broken glass, and a little still life of fruit juts up from the bottom as if the floor had tipped toward you. Picasso painted it in his Montmartre studio over the first half of 1907, working through hundreds of preparatory drawings, and its original title was blunter than the polite one that stuck: Le Bordel d'Avignon, "The Brothel of Avignon," after a street in Barcelona. The women are prostitutes; the picture is meant to unsettle, and it does.

What made it a hinge in the history of art was Picasso's raid on other traditions. The three left-hand faces borrow from ancient Iberian sculpture; the two on the right, slashed and scarified, come from African and Oceanic masks he had seen in the ethnographic museum in Paris. Perspective is abolished, beauty is refused, and the female nude — the most idealized subject in Western painting — is turned into something raw and confrontational. Cézanne had begun breaking form into facets; Picasso finished the job with a hammer.

The reaction was horror. When Picasso showed the canvas to his circle, even his boldest friends recoiled. Georges Braque said looking at it was like being made to drink petrol and eat rope — then went home and started painting the same way, and the two of them invented Cubism together. Henri Matisse was reportedly furious, taking it as a mockery of modern art. Stung, Picasso rolled the enormous canvas up and left it in the studio. It was not shown in public until 1916 and hung largely unseen for years, a rumor among painters, until the Museum of Modern Art acquired it in 1939 through the Lillie P. Bliss Bequest and made it the cornerstone of modern art itself.

## The World Behind It

Paris in 1907 was the laboratory of the modern. Cézanne had just died the previous autumn, and a memorial retrospective had put his radical, faceted landscapes in front of every ambitious young painter in the city. At the same time, France's colonial empire was pouring African and Pacific artifacts into Paris museums and curio shops, and the avant-garde — Picasso, Matisse, Derain — began collecting the masks and carvings with an appetite that was equal parts genuine wonder and colonial appropriation. Both currents run straight into this canvas.

Picasso was living in the Bateau-Lavoir, the ramshackle warren of studios in Montmartre, surrounded by poets and dealers and rival painters, all of them jostling to be the one who cracked open what came after Impressionism. Les Demoiselles was his answer, hurled down like a challenge — so extreme that even the people best equipped to understand it could not, at first, bear to look.

## The Artist at This Moment

Picasso was twenty-five, ferociously ambitious, and no longer poor enough to be ignored. The wistful acrobats of the Rose Period were behind him; he had been studying Iberian carvings and, that spring, had walked through the crowded halls of the Trocadéro's ethnographic museum and come away shaken by its African masks. He wanted to make a painting that would not charm but detonate.

He was also in fierce, unspoken competition with Matisse, twelve years his senior and the reigning king of the Paris avant-garde. Les Demoiselles was partly a bid to seize that crown. The cost was isolation: the picture was too much even for his allies, and he seems to have half-doubted it himself, keeping it out of sight for the better part of a decade. Only later did it become clear that in those months of 1907, in a cheap studio on a Montmartre hill, the twenty-five-year-old had opened the door through which the entire twentieth century would walk.

## Interesting Facts

- Its original title was Le Bordel d'Avignon ("The Brothel of Avignon"); the tamer name we use today was coined by the poet André Salmon.
- Georges Braque said the picture made him feel as if he were drinking petrol and eating rope — then adopted its ideas, and with Picasso invented Cubism.
- Matisse was reportedly enraged by it, taking the work as an assault on modern painting.
- The two right-hand faces were inspired by African and Oceanic masks; the others draw on ancient Iberian sculpture.
- Shaken by the hostility, Picasso rolled the canvas up and did not exhibit it publicly until 1916, nearly a decade after it was painted.
- The Museum of Modern Art acquired it in 1939 through the Lillie P. Bliss Bequest, and it is now often called the first truly modern painting.
