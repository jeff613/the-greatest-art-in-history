## The Story

A boy in red trunks sits at the very edge of the water, knees drawn up, staring at the river and saying nothing. Behind him a man lies stretched on the grass in a straw hat, and another sits upright further back, equally sealed off. Out in the shallows a boy in an orange cap holds his hands to his mouth and calls to someone across the water. Beyond them the Seine, a bridge, a sail, and — unmistakably, along the far bank — the chimneys and sheds of the factories at Clichy, pushing smoke into a pale sky.

Seurat was twenty-four and this was his bid for a reputation. He made it enormous, two metres by three, the size the Salon reserved for history painting and mythology, and he filled it with working men on their day off at Asnières, a suburb where Parisians went to swim in a river that the same factories were busy polluting. Nobody had given ordinary labourers that much wall before, or treated them with such gravity: the figures are simplified almost to sculpture, motionless, weighty, each one alone with his own thoughts. There is no anecdote, no incident, nothing happening. It is a picture about heat, water, quiet, and the fact that these particular bodies get one afternoon.

He prepared it the way he would prepare everything, with a stack of small oil panels painted on the spot and careful conté drawings of individual figures. He did not yet paint in dots. The surface here is built from short criss-crossed strokes, a technique he had learned from studying Delacroix, and only later — around 1887, after he had worked out pointillism on the Grande Jatte — did he go back into this canvas and rework passages with small coloured dots, most visibly the orange hat of the boy in the water.

The Salon rejected it in 1884. Seurat's response was not to try again but to help build an alternative: he joined the group of refused artists who founded a society with no jury, no prizes, and no authority to please, and showed the painting there in May of that year. The Société des Artistes Indépendants outlived everyone involved.

## The World Behind It

Asnières and Clichy were the edge of industrial Paris — the belt of factories, gasworks, and rail lines that the rebuilt city had pushed outward. The river there served simultaneously as a swimming spot, a sewer, and a shipping lane, and the men lying on Seurat's bank would have gone back to work in the buildings smoking behind them.

The 1880s were the decade in which the French Republic began to take the industrial working class seriously as a political fact, and painters were arguing about whether modern life meant Renoir's dancers or something harder. Seurat, who would later drift toward the anarchist circles around the critic Félix Fénéon, made the harder choice and then declined to preach about it. The chimneys are simply there, in the sunlight, part of the view.

## The Artist at This Moment

He had left the École des Beaux-Arts, served his year of military duty at Brest, and spent an extraordinary apprenticeship drawing in conté crayon — velvety black studies of solitary figures that are among the finest drawings of the century. He was ready to make a statement, and he had chosen the scale of one.

He was also, at this point, entirely unknown, living on an allowance from his family, and about to be told by the official art establishment that his three-metre canvas was not wanted. The rejection turned out to be the making of him: within two years the Indépendants he helped found would be the venue where the Grande Jatte, and the movement it launched, reached the public.

He returned to this painting years later, once he had a system, and quietly updated parts of it — a young man's masterpiece, revised by the man he became.

## Interesting Facts

- At two by three metres, it is Salon-history-painting scale, given over to working men on a day off.
- It is not, originally, a pointillist picture — Seurat painted it in criss-crossed strokes and only added dots to parts of it around 1887.
- The factories smoking on the far bank are at Clichy; the river the boys are swimming in was being poisoned by them.
- The Salon rejected it, so Seurat helped found the Société des Artistes Indépendants, which had no jury at all, and hung it there instead.
- The boy in the orange cap calling across the water is one of the most reproduced figures in French painting, though no one knows who modelled for him.
- Its companion piece is *A Sunday on La Grande Jatte* — the far bank of this very river, painted from the opposite direction.
