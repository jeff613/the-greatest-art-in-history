## The Story

A woman has a child on her lap and both of the child's feet in a shallow china basin of water. Neither of them looks up. One arm is locked around the child's waist; her other hand rests over a small foot in the water, half washing it and half holding it still. The child leans back into her and watches that foot with total concentration. A white pitcher painted with pink flowers stands on the carpet beside the bowl, waiting. It takes a second to notice the strangest thing about the picture, which is where you are standing. The floor has been tipped up toward you, the rug and the basin and the striped dress crowding the surface, so that you are not seated across the room like a guest — you are directly above them, close enough to touch, in the small hot circle of a private ritual.

Cassatt had learned that from the Japanese. In April 1890 the École des Beaux-Arts hung an enormous exhibition of ukiyo-e prints, more than seven hundred of them, and she went back again and again. She wrote to Berthe Morisot ordering her to come: "You who want to make color prints, you couldn't dream of anything more beautiful." What she took from Utamaro and the rest was not costume or cherry blossom but structure — the high viewpoint, the flat pattern, the willingness to let a floor rise like a wall and a striped robe run straight into a flowered carpet without any polite gap between them.

She also took the subject. Japanese printmakers had made whole careers of women washing, dressing, holding infants, doing the ordinary unwatched work of a household, and treating it as worth an artist's full attention. Cassatt did the same, and because she was a woman she could actually get into the rooms where it happened.

The picture was painted in 1893, the year she turned forty-nine, and shown that November at her first large solo exhibition, at the Durand-Ruel gallery in Paris, under the title *La Toilette de l'Enfant*. The dealer bought it outright that month and sold it on in January to a Connecticut collector; it came back to Durand-Ruel a few years later, and in 1910 the Art Institute of Chicago bought it. It has been one of the most loved pictures in the building ever since.

A note that changes how it reads: Cassatt never married and never had children. She decided early that a family and a career could not both be had, and she chose. The most famous painter of mothers and children in the nineteenth century was working from the outside — watching hard, and getting it exactly right.

## The World Behind It

Paris in 1893 was still digesting Japan. Trade had opened in the 1850s, prints had arrived as packing material and become an obsession, and by the 1890s japonisme had worked its way into everything from Whistler's interiors to shop wallpaper. The 1890 exhibition at the École des Beaux-Arts was the moment it stopped being decoration for European artists and became a set of tools.

For Cassatt the early nineties were the busiest stretch of her life. She produced a set of ten colour prints in 1891 that other artists — Pissarro among them — regarded as technically astonishing. Then Bertha Palmer, the Chicago socialite running the Woman's Building at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, asked her for a mural. Cassatt painted a fifty-eight-foot triptych called *Modern Woman*, the centre panel showing young women picking the fruit of knowledge, a community of women with no men in it at all. It hung over the Gallery of Honor, went to Palmer afterward, and vanished after her death in 1918. Nobody has found it since.

## The Artist at This Moment

She was forty-nine and at the height of her powers, and she had stopped caring what movement she belonged to. The Impressionists had held their last group show in 1886; she had drifted out of the label and into something plainer and more deliberate, all firm outline and broad flat colour.

She was about to have a house of her own: in March 1894 she bought the Château de Beaufresne, an eighteenth-century pile of red brick an hour north-west of Paris, close enough to visit Pissarro and Monet, and she put a print studio in its mill. She had become a fixture for young American painters who came to Paris for advice. And she was already deep into the other career that made her indispensable — steering her friend Louisine Havemeyer and her husband toward the French moderns nobody in America was buying yet. Much of what they bought on her advice hangs today in the Metropolitan Museum.

## Interesting Facts

- The tipped-up floor and flattened pattern come from Japanese woodblock prints — Cassatt saw more than seven hundred of them at the École des Beaux-Arts in 1890 and wrote to Berthe Morisot that she must not miss them.
- Cassatt never married and had no children; she had decided as a young woman that marriage was incompatible with a serious career.
- It was first shown in November 1893 at her first big solo exhibition, at Durand-Ruel in Paris, as *La Toilette de l'Enfant*.
- The Art Institute of Chicago bought it in 1910, and it has been among the museum's most popular paintings ever since.
- The same year she painted it, she finished a fifty-eight-foot mural, *Modern Woman*, for the Woman's Building at the Chicago world's fair. It disappeared after its owner's death in 1918 and has never been found.
- Critics of her day dismissed her subjects as suitably feminine. She objected all her life to being filed under "woman artist."
