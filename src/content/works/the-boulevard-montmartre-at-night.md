## The Story

On 8 February 1897 Pissarro wrote to his son Lucien that he had taken a room at the Grand Hôtel de Russie, on the corner of the rue Drouot, from which he could see the whole sweep of the boulevards. Over the next nine weeks he painted fourteen canvases from that window: the boulevard Montmartre in morning sun, in grey weather, in rain, under snow, choked with carnival crowds on Mardi Gras. Thirteen of them are daylight. This is the fourteenth.

He seems to have painted it in a single burst, and it is unlike anything else he made. The street runs away into a wet blue dark. There is no moon and no sky worth the name — just a mass of night pressing down on the rooftops — and everything that can be seen is artificial. Cool white blobs march down the middle of the boulevard where the city had strung up its new electric lamps. Warm yellow spills out of the shop and café windows on the right, doubling itself in the soaked asphalt. Small orange sparks in the left foreground are the oil lamps of cabs drawn up along the kerb. The people are barely there: a scatter of dark strokes and pale faces on the pavement, and a suggestion of traffic moving down the centre.

It is a painting made almost entirely of reflections, and it stands very close to what a photographer would find fifty years later. Pissarro, who had spent his whole life insisting that a painter works from the thing in front of him, had found a subject that only existed at all because the century had learned to light itself.

He never signed it. He never exhibited it. So far as anyone has been able to establish, it is the only nocturne he painted in a career of half a century. It stayed with his son Lucien until 1925, when it appeared in a London gallery show, was bought by the trustees of the Courtauld Fund and presented to the National Gallery, where it has hung ever since.

## The World Behind It

The grands boulevards were the thing Baron Haussmann's demolitions had been for: a straight, wide, brilliantly lit spine of theatres, cafés, newspaper offices, restaurants and department stores through the middle of Paris, and by the 1890s the place where the city went at night. Electric arc lamps had begun replacing gas along the main streets in the previous two decades, and this picture catches the changeover exactly — the cold new light down the centre of the road, the old warm light still burning in every doorway.

It was also a moment of ugly national temper. Within a year of this canvas the Dreyfus affair would tear France apart, and it took the Impressionists with it. Pissarro, Jewish and a Dreyfusard, found that Degas and Renoir would no longer greet him in the street. He kept working. The boulevard series was the beginning of a last great run of city pictures — the avenue de l'Opéra, the place du Théâtre-Français, the Tuileries, the quays of Rouen and Dieppe and Le Havre — all of them made through glass.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was sixty-six and his eyes had beaten him. Since the late 1880s a recurring infection of the tear duct had made his eye intolerant of wind, dust and cold, which ended the outdoor painting he had done for forty years. His solution was to rent top-floor rooms with a view and paint through the window, and he applied it with the stubbornness of a man who refused to accept that his working life was over.

The year cost him more than his sight. On 15 November 1897 his son Félix, twenty-three, died of tuberculosis in a sanatorium at Kew, outside London; Pissarro could not make the journey, and Julie went in his place. A month later, house-hunting for another window, he wrote to Lucien about a room at the Grand Hôtel du Louvre with a view down the avenue de l'Opéra. "It is not very aesthetic perhaps," he wrote, "but I am delighted to be able to try to do these Paris streets which are often called ugly, but which are so silvery, so luminous and so lively."

He had six more years. He spent them at the window.

## Interesting Facts

- Pissarro painted fourteen views of the boulevard Montmartre between 10 February and 17 April 1897, all from the same hotel window; this is the only night scene among them, and apparently the only one he ever painted.
- He left the canvas unsigned and never exhibited it in his lifetime.
- The cold white lamps down the middle of the street are electric; the warm yellow in the shopfronts is gas, and the orange specks at the kerb are the oil lamps of waiting cabs.
- A chronic tear-duct infection, not failing vision, is what drove him indoors — his eye could not stand wind and cold.
- The picture passed from his son Lucien to the National Gallery in 1925, bought and presented by the trustees of the Courtauld Fund.
- His son Félix died of tuberculosis in England in November of the year he painted it; Pissarro did not make the journey, and Julie went in his place.
