## The Story

It is a fish, split open and hung on a hook, and it appears to be smiling at you. Two dark holes sit where you expect eyes; below them the ray's mouth curves into a wide wet grin, and under that the whole belly has been opened into a red cave of gut and rib. It is a face and a wound at the same time, and Chardin has hung it dead centre and lit it like an altarpiece. To the left a cat steps across a heap of oysters with its back up. To the right, a bottle, a copper pot, a knife pushed half off the table's edge, a rumpled white cloth. Nothing in the room is doing anything. The fish does all the work.

Chardin was twenty-eight and nobody when he made it. He had no history paintings, no Roman training, no Latin, no patrons. What he had was a canvas of a dead ray so unnervingly alive that people kept mistaking it for the work of a Dutchman or a Fleming a century dead. His early biographer Charles-Nicolas Cochin the Younger tells the story that when Chardin was angling for the Académie's attention he propped this picture and its companion, *The Buffet*, in an outer room where the visiting academicians would pass them, and waited next door. The portraitist Nicolas de Largillierre stopped, looked a long time, then came through and told the young man he had some very fine paintings out there — they must be by some skilled Flemish hand. They were Chardin's. Largillierre embraced him and told him to present himself without hesitation.

On 25 September 1728 the Académie royale accepted him on the strength of these two canvases, on the same day he applied — an almost unheard-of speed. But it accepted him in the talent of animals and fruit, the bottom rung of a ladder whose top was reserved for painters of gods and battles. That phrase followed him for fifty-one years.

Diderot, in 1763, wrote the sentence everyone still quotes: the object is disgusting, but it is the flesh of the fish itself, its skin, its blood. He told young painters to go and copy it. They did. Matisse made a copy of it in the Louvre in the 1890s; Boudin and Soutine both circled back to the same dead fish. Proust said the ray hung there like the nave of a polychrome cathedral.

## The World Behind It

Paris in 1728 was a few years past the end of the Regency and settling in under a young Louis XV. Its art establishment was a strict hierarchy with a moral attached: history painting first, because it required learning and invention; then portraits; then landscape; then, at the bottom, animals, fruit, and dead things, which required only eyes and a hand. A painter admitted at the bottom stayed there. He could not teach the top subjects, and he could not expect the big royal commissions.

For an unknown, the only way to be seen at all was the Exposition de la Jeunesse, a one-morning open-air show in the Place Dauphine where young painters hung canvases on the walls and hoped somebody important walked past. Chardin put *The Ray* out there on 3 June 1728, three months before the Académie took him in. Exactly when he painted it is not settled — it was certainly finished by that morning, and some catalogues date it two or three years earlier.

The subject itself was ordinary Paris. A skate was cheap, ugly, Friday food, sold gutted off the slabs at Les Halles. Dutch and Flemish painters had put fish on canvas for a century, but nobody had hung one at this scale, at eye level, and asked a viewer to look at it for an hour.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was the son of a cabinetmaker in the rue de Seine — specifically, a maker of billiard tables, good enough at it to become head of his guild. Chardin had grown up in a workshop, not a library, and he had almost no literary education, which shut the mythological and biblical subjects out from the start. He had trained with the history painter Pierre-Jacques Cazes and then with Noël-Nicolas Coypel, and taken his master's papers in 1724 from the Académie de Saint-Luc, the painters' guild rather than the royal school. He was working on decorative jobs and restorations for other men.

He had signed a marriage contract with Marguerite Saintard in 1723 and could not afford to go through with the wedding; he would not marry her until 1731. He had never been to Italy and never would go. He would never leave Paris for any length of time at all.

What he had, at twenty-eight, was an eye and a kitchen. *The Ray* is the moment he found out that was enough.

## Interesting Facts

- Cochin the Younger tells the story that Chardin left this canvas where visiting academicians would stumble on it, and that Largillierre took it for the work of a Flemish master before learning who painted it.
- The Académie admitted Chardin on 25 September 1728, the same day he applied, but registered him "in the talent of animals and fruit" — a low classification that capped his status for the rest of his life.
- Diderot praised it in his Salon of 1763 with the line "the object is disgusting, but it is the very flesh of the fish," and told young painters to copy it.
- Henri Matisse painted a copy of it in the Louvre in 1896; Soutine and Boudin both made their own versions of the hanging fish.
- Marcel Proust compared the ray's split body to the nave of a many-coloured cathedral.
- Its companion reception piece, *The Buffet*, hangs in the same museum — the safe, conventional picture that came with the shocking one.
