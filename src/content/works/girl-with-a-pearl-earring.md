## The Story

She turns toward us out of a black void, lips just parted, eyes wide and shining, a single fat pearl catching the light at her ear — and for three and a half centuries people have wondered who she is and what she is about to say. The answer is that we have no idea, and that Vermeer almost certainly intended it that way. This is not a portrait. It is a *tronie*, a Dutch type of picture that was a study of a face, an expression, or an exotic costume rather than a likeness of a specific, named person. The girl was never meant to be identified. She is a mood made flesh: a fleeting glance, frozen and eternal.

Everything in the painting is stripped down to intensify that glance. There is no room, no furniture, no story-telling detail — only the girl, an oriental turban of luminous blue and gold, and the darkness she emerges from. Vermeer built her almost entirely from a few pure notes of color: the ultramarine of the headscarf, the warm yellow of its trailing cloth, the pale gold of her skin, the wet gleam on her lips and eyes. The pearl itself is a small miracle of painterly bluff — up close it is just two strokes, a soft smear of white for the reflected window and a duller one for the glow bouncing off her collar, yet it reads as a solid, hanging drop of light.

And here is a quiet joke that hangs over the whole famous picture: the pearl may not be a pearl. A gem that size and shape, if genuine, would have been implausibly enormous and fabulously valuable — far beyond the reach of the modest girl who wears it. Many experts think Vermeer painted a piece of polished glass or varnished tin, or simply exaggerated a smaller pearl for effect. The most photographed jewel in art history might be a fake, dreamed up to catch the light.

## The World Behind It

Vermeer worked in Delft, a prosperous town in the Dutch Republic, at a moment when even middle-class households bought paintings by the dozen. But he was an oddity in that busy market: painfully slow, producing only a couple of pictures a year, and known to hardly anyone beyond his own city. Where his contemporaries churned out cheap genre scenes for a mass audience, he made small, jewel-like works of obsessive refinement, using pigments so costly — genuine ultramarine ground from imported lapis lazuli — that some scholars suspect he was quietly ruining himself to make them.

Tronies like this one were popular precisely because they were not commissions; a painter could make them speculatively and sell them to whoever was charmed. The turban gestures at the era's fascination with the exotic East, feeding a Dutch taste for costume and faraway luxury brought home by the country's trading fleets. Yet Vermeer drains away the anecdote and leaves only the encounter. In a culture flooded with pictures, he made one that does almost nothing — and has held viewers spellbound longer than nearly any other.

## The Artist at This Moment

Around 1665 Vermeer was in his early thirties, at the height of his quiet powers. He lived in his mother-in-law's house in Delft with his wife Catharina and a growing brood of children — the couple would eventually have eleven who survived infancy — and he supported them precariously by painting, dealing in other artists' work, and leaning on the household's wealth. He had been elected a head of the local painters' guild, a sign of real standing among his peers, even as his name meant nothing in the wider art world.

This was his best decade, the years of the luminous interiors and the handful of masterpieces on which his fame now rests. He could not have known that the good times were running out. Within a few years the French invasion of 1672 would shatter the Dutch art market, plunge him into debt, and hasten his early death. The girl's expression — poised, expectant, on the verge of something — belongs to this narrow window when everything was still possible, before the collapse that would end his life at forty-three and bury his reputation for two hundred years.

## Interesting Facts

- It is not a portrait but a *tronie* — a study of a face and costume — so the girl was never meant to represent an identifiable person. Her identity is unknown.
- The pearl is probably not a real pearl: a genuine one that size would be improbably huge and priceless. Vermeer likely painted glass, tin, or an exaggeration.
- The famous pearl is rendered with just two strokes of paint — a bright dab for the window's reflection and a softer one for light bouncing up from her collar.
- In 1881 the painting sold at auction for about two guilders plus a small premium — a pittance — before its buyer bequeathed it to the Mauritshuis in 1902.
- Recent scientific study found the girl once had faint eyelashes and stood before a green curtain, now darkened to an empty black background.
- Often called "the Mona Lisa of the North," it inspired Tracy Chevalier's 1999 novel and the 2003 film starring Scarlett Johansson.
