## The Story

At first glance it looks like the hundreds of gold-ground Madonnas that came before it: the Virgin enthroned, the Christ child on her knee, angels and saints crowding in, the whole thing blazing against a flat field of gold leaf. But stand in front of Giotto's version and something is different, almost unsettling. This Mary has a body. Beneath the blue robe you can feel the weight of her knees, the swell of her chest, the solidity of a real woman sitting down. Her throne is not a flat emblem but a structure built in space, with steps and a canopy and sides that recede, so that the angels kneeling at the front seem genuinely to be in front of it and the saints behind genuinely behind. You could, it seems, walk around it.

That sense of a real figure in real space is the quiet revolution of the panel. Where earlier painters had draped their Madonnas in gold-lined patterns that denied the body underneath, Giotto lets soft cloth fall over a form that has heft and gravity. And where the older Madonnas gazed out with the fixed, remote stare of an icon, this one is human. Look at her mouth: there is the barest hint of a smile, the first flicker of an inner life on the face of a Madonna, as if she were a person with thoughts of her own rather than a symbol to be worshipped.

The picture is large — well over three meters tall — and was made to sit high on a church altar, seen from below and from a distance. Even so, Giotto packs it with human touches: the two angels at the foot of the throne offering a crown and a vase of flowers, the ranks of saints peering over one another's shoulders to see the child. It is a devotional image that has begun, gently, to behave like a portrait of living people.

## The World Behind It

The panel was painted for the church of Ognissanti — "All Saints" — in Florence, run by an austere lay order, and it takes its nickname from that church. Florence around 1310 was one of the richest cities in Europe, a banking and wool-trading powerhouse whose citizens poured money into churches and the art that filled them. A great altarpiece was a serious public statement, and to be handed such a commission was a mark of the highest standing.

It also landed Giotto in the middle of a rivalry the whole city could see. The dominant style still came out of the icon tradition and out of Siena, where Duccio was painting Madonnas of exquisite, gilded refinement. Giotto's Ognissanti Madonna answered that tradition with something heavier, plainer, and more human. Today the panel hangs in the Uffizi, and it is generally displayed in the same room as two other great Madonnas of the age — one by Giotto's teacher Cimabue and one by Duccio — so that visitors can stand in a single space and watch, across three enormous panels, the exact moment when painting turned from gold-ground symbol toward flesh and weight.

## The Artist at This Moment

By around 1310 Giotto was back in Florence, the most famous painter in Italy, fresh from the triumph of the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua. He had a large workshop, a growing fortune, and commissions arriving from across the peninsula. The Ognissanti Madonna is, in a sense, him planting his flag at home — bringing the full force of what he had learned about weight, space, and human feeling to the most traditional format there was, the enthroned Virgin, and quietly outdoing the masters who had defined it.

This was the mature Giotto, confident enough to take the most conventional of subjects and make it new without any showy break — no strange composition, no shocking gesture, just a Madonna who, for the first time, seems to be a real person sitting in a real chair. Within a few years Dante would name him the reigning painter of the age, and later Florentines would look back on this panel as one of the founding works of their city's art. Giotto would spend his last years as master of the cathedral works, designing its bell tower, dying in 1337 as the most honored artist Florence had produced.

## Interesting Facts

- The painting's name comes from the Florentine church of Ognissanti (All Saints), for which it was made; its subject is the Virgin enthroned in majesty.
- At more than three meters tall, it was built to be seen high above a church altar, yet Giotto still gave the Virgin a convincingly solid, weight-bearing body.
- Mary's faint smile is often singled out as one of the first genuine hints of inner life and emotion on the face of a Madonna.
- The throne is constructed as a believable three-dimensional space, with angels clearly in front of it and saints behind — an early step toward pictorial depth.
- In the Uffizi the panel is usually shown alongside Madonnas by Cimabue and Duccio, letting viewers compare three generations of the same subject side by side.
- The work is a touchstone of the shift art historians call the move from the "Greek" (Byzantine) manner toward the naturalism that would define the Italian Renaissance.
