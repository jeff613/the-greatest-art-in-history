## The Story

A man in a white satin suit stands on a low mound of grass, life-size, facing straight out. His sleeves are too long, his trousers are too short, his ruff is absurd, and his hat sits flat on his head like a plate. His arms hang at his sides. He is not doing anything. Behind and below him, four other players carry on without him: a bearded doctor rides in on a donkey that has turned its head to leer at us, and three more actors gossip on the right, one of them in a well-cut red suit that makes the white one look like a sack. Not one of them is looking at him. He is the only thing in French painting of this period that stops dead and stares back.

Nobody knows why it exists. The picture appears in no document until 1826. It is missing from the vast eighteenth-century catalogue of engravings after Watteau assembled by his friend Jean de Jullienne, which was meant to record everything. The most popular guess is that it was a shop sign — perhaps one of a pair painted for a retired actor named Belloni, whose speciality had been Pierrot and who opened a café — but that is a guess, and the Louvre says so.

What the picture became is a different story. In the nineteenth century it was renamed *Gilles*, after a French fairground clown who had got tangled up with the Italian Pierrot, and it was read as a portrait of loneliness: the sad clown, the artist isolated among people who do not see him. Erwin Panofsky was among the art historians who read the clown as a reflection of Watteau himself. Poets adopted it, Verlaine named a book of poems after the genre Watteau had invented, and by the twentieth century Picasso had absorbed it and the makers of *Les Enfants du Paradis* had drawn on it. Donald Posner spoiled the party by pointing out that in Watteau's day Pierrot was not a melancholy figure at all but a simpleton, a comic idiot, and that the whole reading of tragic solitude is something the nineteenth century put there.

Which leaves the face. It has been called stupid, mournful, dreamy, and blank, and the honest answer is that it holds all four at once and will not settle. The costume is ridiculous. The stance is monumental. Watteau gave a clown the scale and the frontality of an altarpiece saint and then declined to tell anyone why.

## The World Behind It

The Italian players had been thrown out of Paris in 1697 — Louis XIV's court took offence at a satire and the Comédie-Italienne was shut down and expelled. For the next twenty years the commedia survived in France at the fairgrounds, in the *théâtre de la foire*, where troupes worked around the ban with mime, placards, and songs the audience was invited to sing. Watteau grew up as a painter inside that world; his first master in Paris, Claude Gillot, painted almost nothing else.

The Regent brought the Italians back in 1716, and Paris got its masks again just as Watteau was reaching the end of his short working life. His costumed figures are usually taken for straightforward theatre pictures, but they are not scenes from plays. He borrowed the wardrobe and threw away the plot. A Watteau actor is almost never performing; he is standing about between things, half in character and half out of it, which is a far stranger thing to paint than a comedy.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was around thirty-four, and this is the only life-size figure he ever painted. Everything else he made is small, quick, populated, and slightly distant — figures seen from across a garden. Here he walked right up to one.

He was also, by now, visibly ill. Within a year he would sail to London to put himself in the hands of the English physician Richard Mead and come back no better. Friends who wrote about him afterwards described a man who was difficult to live with, endlessly dissatisfied with his own work, and casually indifferent to whether he had money or a roof. He was in the habit of moving on — from Gillot, from Audran, from lodging to lodging, from patron to patron — and of leaving pictures behind without much apparent interest in where they went. This one he left so completely that it vanished from the record for a hundred years.

## Interesting Facts

- The painting is not mentioned in any surviving document until 1826, and it was left out of the Recueil Jullienne, the exhaustive print catalogue of Watteau's work published in the 1730s.
- Early in the nineteenth century a dealer who could not sell it chalked a sales pitch along the bottom of the canvas: "How happy Pierrot would be, if he had the gift of pleasing you."
- Napoleon's museum director, Vivant Denon, bought it for 150 francs. It reached the Louvre in 1869 as part of the bequest of the collector Dr Louis La Caze.
- It was called *Gilles* for most of its famous life — a nineteenth-century muddle between the Italian Pierrot and a French fairground clown. The Louvre has since restored the name Pierrot.
- A restoration completed in 2024 unfolded canvas edges that had been turned under and hidden during an eighteenth-century relining, widening the picture from about 149 to nearly 156 centimetres and giving Watteau back paint no one alive had seen.
- Stripping the oxidised varnish at the same time returned the colour: the sky behind him is not the dull grey of a century of reproductions but a cool, luminous blue.
