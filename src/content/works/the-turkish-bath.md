## The Story

Two dozen women in a hot, shallow, airless room, packed into a circle: sprawling, dozing, embracing, one dancing at the back, one in the middle with her back to us playing a long-necked lute. There is no exit visible and no scale to hold on to. It is less a scene than a pile — bodies arranged into a single mass of pale flesh and blue-green shade, filling every inch of the disc.

Ingres worked on it for years. A rectangular version existed by the late 1850s and was sold to a relation of Napoleon III, who sent it back within days because his wife found it *peu convenable* — unsuitable. Ingres took it back, kept going, and in 1862 changed the format entirely, converting the rectangle into a tondo. A photograph taken by Charles Marville records the earlier state, so we can see what he cut away. Then he signed it and added, in Latin, AETATIS LXXXII: aged eighty-two.

The source was a book. Ingres had copied out, decades earlier, a passage from the letters of Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, an English ambassador's wife who in 1717 had actually been inside a women's bathhouse at Adrianople and described some two hundred women bathing, undressed and unembarrassed, sitting and talking and drinking coffee. Ingres never went east. Every Turkish detail here is imagination, props, and reading — an old man in Paris building a room he had only ever heard about.

He also filled it with himself. The lute player at the centre is the *Valpinçon Bather* of 1808, the same back at the same angle, fifty-four years later. The woman holding a cup at the right is drawn from his *Madame Moitessier*. Figures from other pictures recur throughout. It is a summary — every nude he had ever painted, gathered into one room.

It took a long time to become respectable. The Turkish diplomat Khalil Bey bought it in 1865 for a collection of erotica. The Louvre turned it down twice in the early twentieth century before accepting it in 1911 as a gift from the museum's friends' society. It is now one of the most reproduced paintings in France.

## The World Behind It

Nineteenth-century Europe had an appetite for the harem that had almost nothing to do with any actual place. Painters supplied odalisques, slave markets, and baths by the hundred; the setting made the nudity respectable by locating it somewhere safely foreign, and the same public that would have been scandalised by a French woman undressed could contemplate an imagined Ottoman one at leisure.

Ingres had been working this vein since the *Grande Odalisque* of 1814 and never once travelled to see any of it. Meanwhile France under Napoleon III was expanding in North Africa, and the fantasy and the empire fed each other. Whatever else this painting is, it is a document of how a culture pictured a place it was busy colonising.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was eighty-two, and he wrote the number on the canvas. It reads as defiance: the leader of French classicism, decorated, academic, endlessly caricatured as the dry apostle of correct drawing, producing at the very end the most unrestrained thing he ever made.

His second marriage, to Delphine Ramel in 1852, had left him working with renewed appetite. He had outlived his rival Delacroix's generation of arguments and most of his own critics.

He had five years left. He died of pneumonia in January 1867 at eighty-six, and left his studio — four thousand drawings and his violin — to the museum in the small town where he was born.

## Interesting Facts

- Ingres signed it with his age in Latin — AETATIS LXXXII — eighty-two years old.
- It began as a rectangle and he cut it into a circle in 1862; a photograph by Charles Marville records the original rectangular composition.
- The first buyer, a relation of Napoleon III, returned it within days after his wife judged it unsuitable.
- The central lute player is the *Valpinçon Bather* of 1808, reused from behind at exactly the same angle fifty-four years later.
- Ingres never travelled to the Ottoman world; the scene comes from the 1717 letters of Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, which he had copied out by hand.
- The Louvre rejected the painting twice before finally accepting it in 1911.
