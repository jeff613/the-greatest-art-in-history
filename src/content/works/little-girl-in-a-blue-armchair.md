## The Story

The child has been dressed up and put in a chair, and she has given up. She is slumped so far down that her back is nearly horizontal, one arm flung behind her head, legs apart, white skirt shoved up over a plaid sash, black stockings and buckled shoes dangling into the middle of the room. Her expression is the specific, bottomless boredom of a small person made to sit still in a good frock. Beside her, in a matching blue armchair, a scruffy brown dog is asleep and taking no responsibility.

Nobody had painted a child like this. Children in pictures sat up, folded their hands, held a hoop or a doll, and looked out at you as evidence of their parents' good fortune. Cassatt let this one collapse. The furniture — four great slipcovered lumps of floral blue, crowding in from every edge — is drawn from a low viewpoint, roughly the height of the child's own eyes, so the room looms exactly as it does when you are five.

The girl was a daughter of friends of Degas, and her name has not survived. The dog is a Brussels griffon, the breed Degas favoured; he gave Cassatt a puppy of his own, procured from another painter in the circle who bred them, and she kept griffons for the rest of her life.

Degas did more than supply the dog. In 1903 Cassatt wrote to the dealer Ambroise Vollard about this picture: "I had done the child in the armchair and he found it good and advised me on the background and he even worked on it." Cleaning and infrared examination at the National Gallery have since confirmed his hand — the strange wedge of floor opening at the back of the room, and the light coming in from a window you cannot see, are credited to him.

That made what happened next worse. Cassatt sent the painting to the American section of the 1878 Exposition Universelle, and the American jury refused it while accepting another of her canvases. "I was furious," she wrote, "all the more so since he had worked on it." She noted, with a precision that has outlived the jurors, that there had been three of them, and that one was a pharmacist. The painting was almost certainly shown the following spring at the fourth Impressionist exhibition instead.

## The World Behind It

The 1878 Exposition Universelle was France announcing that it had recovered. Eight years earlier the country had lost a war to Prussia and then torn itself apart in the Commune; now Paris covered the Trocadéro hill with pavilions and invited the world to look. Visitors could walk through the gardens and stare up at the head of Bartholdi's Statue of Liberty, still waiting for its body and its harbour.

The American section was run by Americans abroad, and it wanted the kind of art that would make the young republic look cultivated: polished, finished, unembarrassing. A slouching child in a blue chair, brushed in fast, was not that. The Impressionists themselves had planned their own exhibition for 1878 and postponed it a year rather than compete with the fair. Cassatt spent the year working toward that postponed show, in an apartment on the Avenue Trudaine where her parents and her sister Lydia had just arrived from Pennsylvania to live with her.

## The Artist at This Moment

She was thirty-four and had just cut herself loose. For ten years she had submitted to the Salon, been accepted, been refused, been accepted again once she darkened a background to please the jury. In 1877 both her entries were thrown out and she had nothing in the Salon for the first time in seven years. That was the year Degas asked her to join the Impressionists.

The effect on her painting was immediate. She had been a studio artist; now she carried a sketchbook out of doors and into the theatre. Her father, who had opposed the whole enterprise, was in Paris keeping accounts and insisting that her studio and materials be paid for out of her sales, which were thin. She needed strong pictures for the next group show, and in one year she made three of her best: this one, a self-portrait, and a painting of her mother reading Le Figaro.

## Interesting Facts

- Cassatt admitted in a letter that Degas "even worked on" the canvas; National Gallery conservators, using infrared imaging, later identified the odd wedge of floor at the back of the room as his.
- The American jury that rejected it had three members, one of whom, Cassatt pointed out, was a pharmacist.
- The sleeping dog is a Brussels griffon. Degas gave her one as a gift, and she kept the breed all her life.
- The whole room is painted from about the height of a child's eyes, which is why the blue furniture feels enormous.
- The little girl was a daughter of friends of Degas; her name was never recorded.
- The painting was given to the National Gallery of Art in 1983 by Paul and Bunny Mellon, as part of a gift of more than eighty Impressionist and Post-Impressionist works.
