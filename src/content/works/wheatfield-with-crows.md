## The Story

A field of ripe wheat churns under a sky of violent, bruised blue. Three paths cut into the grain and lead nowhere — one runs straight at the viewer and simply stops, the others swerve off the edges of the canvas. A scatter of black crows rises out of the wheat toward the storm. It is one of the most emotionally overwhelming landscapes ever painted, and for more than a century people have read it as Vincent van Gogh's farewell to the world.

He painted it in July 1890, in the last weeks of his life, at Auvers-sur-Oise just north of Paris. The format is unusual — a long double-square canvas, wide as a cinema screen — one of a group of stretched-out landscapes he made that final summer to capture "vast fields of wheat under troubled skies." In a letter to Theo, Vincent described such canvases directly: he had tried, he wrote, to express "sadness and extreme loneliness," but also insisted that he found something "healthy and fortifying" in the countryside. The picture holds both at once: dread and beauty, a storm coming and the grain still gold.

Then the legend took over. On 27 July 1890, Van Gogh walked into a field with a revolver and shot himself in the chest; he died two days later with Theo at his side. Ever since, Wheatfield with Crows has been cast as his final painting — the black birds as omens, the dead-end path as despair, the whole thing a coded suicide note. It is an irresistible story, and almost certainly false. Scholars now believe Vincent painted several works after this one in his last fortnight. The crows are not the end of the sentence. But the painting is so raw, so charged with foreboding, that the world has never quite been able to let the myth go.

## The World Behind It

By 1890 Van Gogh had left the south and moved to Auvers-sur-Oise, a village of thatched cottages and wheat fields that was quietly filling up with painters drawn by its cheap rooms and easy train link to Paris. He came to be near Theo and to be watched over by Dr. Paul Gachet, an eccentric homeopath and amateur artist who cared for nervous patients and befriended the Impressionists. It was meant to be a place of recovery.

But the calm was thin. Van Gogh's whole existence rested on his brother, and that summer Theo's world was shaking: the baby was sick, Theo was quarreling with his employers and thinking of striking out on his own, and money was suddenly frightening. Vincent, who had lived his entire adult life on Theo's support, was terrified of being a burden just as his lifeline wavered. The wheat fields of Auvers, which he painted again and again in his final weeks, were the backdrop to a man trying to hold himself together while the ground shifted under everyone he depended on.

## The Artist at This Moment

Van Gogh spent only about seventy days in Auvers, and in them he painted at a superhuman rate — some seventy-odd canvases, roughly one a day. It was the same desperate productivity that had carried him through Arles and the asylum: work as the only reliable medicine, the thing that held the darkness at bay. He painted the village church, Dr. Gachet's melancholy portrait, cottages, gardens, and the endless surrounding wheat.

He was thirty-seven, and running out of everything. The letters of his last weeks swing between fragile calm and deep anxiety about Theo, about money, about whether his life had come to anything. On 27 July he shot himself in a field outside the village and staggered back, wounded, to his room; he died there on 29 July with Theo beside him. His brother, shattered, followed him to the grave within six months. Wheatfield with Crows was painted in that final, fraying stretch — not, as the legend insists, as a literal last testament, but as one more attempt to wrestle a storm-lit field onto canvas by a man who had almost no time left and knew it.

## Interesting Facts

- Despite the enduring myth, this was probably not Van Gogh's last painting — art historians think he completed several other canvases in his final two weeks at Auvers.
- The canvas is a "double square," about twice as wide as it is tall, one of roughly a dozen elongated landscapes Vincent painted in his last summer to convey the sweep of the wheat fields.
- In a letter, Van Gogh said his wheatfields under troubled skies expressed "sadness and extreme loneliness" — yet in the same breath he called the countryside "healthy and fortifying."
- The three paths that lead nowhere have been endlessly interpreted, but Van Gogh left no explanation; the reading of them as symbols of hopelessness is the world's invention, not his.
- The painting has hung in the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam since it opened, built on the collection Theo's widow, Jo van Gogh-Bonger, preserved and championed after both brothers died.
