## The Story

At first glance it looks simple: a five-year-old princess and her little retinue, caught in a big shadowy room. The Infanta Margarita stands at the center in a stiff pale gown, flanked by two maids of honor — the *meninas* who give the picture its name — one dipping into a curtsy, the other offering a little red jug. Around them cluster a dwarf, a solemn-faced child resting a foot on a drowsy mastiff, a chaperone and a bodyguard murmuring in the gloom. And off to the left, brush in hand, stands the painter himself, stepping back from a huge canvas whose face we will never see.

Then the puzzle opens up. Everyone seems to be looking out at us — but who are we? On the far wall hangs a dim mirror, and in it float two ghostly figures: King Philip IV and his queen, Mariana. They are standing exactly where you, the viewer, stand. So is Velázquez painting the royal couple, with the princess and her court having wandered in to watch? Are the king and queen merely reflected as they pass? Or are we, impossibly, seeing the scene through their eyes? The painting refuses to settle the question. It has been called a picture about the act of looking itself, and philosophers from the nineteenth century onward have written whole books trying to stand in the right spot.

There is one more quiet act of daring. On the painter's black doublet glows the red cross of the Order of Santiago — the knighthood Velázquez craved and did not receive until 1659, three years after the date on the canvas. According to a tradition first recorded by the painter Antonio Palomino, the cross was added later, after Velázquez's death, on the king's own orders; some even claimed Philip painted it himself. Whether literally true or not, the story captures the picture's real subject: a tradesman with a brush who painted his way into the royal family, and made the king complicit in the boast.

## The World Behind It

Velázquez painted this in 1656, deep inside the Alcázar palace in Madrid, in the very room where he worked. Spain by then was an empire in slow decline — its silver fleets less reliable, its armies bleeding, its European wars grinding on — but the court still wrapped itself in suffocating ceremony, and no ceremony mattered more than the person of the royal child. Margarita was a prized dynastic asset, destined for a Habsburg marriage, and images of her were sent abroad like diplomatic dispatches.

The picture is soaked in the strange world of that court: the maids of honor bound by minute rules of precedence, the dwarves and jesters kept as companions and curiosities, the chamberlain pausing on the lit stair at the back. Velázquez knew this world from the inside not just as its painter but as a palace official, obsessed like everyone around him with rank and access. That is partly why he put himself in it — not as a servant in the corner, but standing tall at the heart of the royal household.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1656 Velázquez was in his late fifties and near the summit of both his art and his court career. He had returned from his second Italian journey, painted the pope, and been handed the demanding post of palace chamberlain, which left him less and less time to paint. Las Meninas belongs to this late, sovereign phase, when his brushwork loosened into the shimmering near-abstraction that later painters would revere.

It is hard not to read the painting as a summing-up. He gives himself pride of place, the keys of the palace at his waist, the (eventual) knight's cross on his chest — a man declaring, in the most sophisticated picture he ever made, exactly how far he had climbed. Within four years he would be dead, worn out by the court offices he had chased so hard.

## Interesting Facts

- The mirror on the back wall is only about a foot wide, yet its ghostly reflection of the king and queen has generated more scholarly writing than almost any other detail in Western art.
- The red cross of Santiago on Velázquez's chest was, by tradition, added after his death by royal order — possibly, it was said, by King Philip IV himself.
- In 1734 a fire gutted the Alcázar palace; Las Meninas was reportedly saved by being thrown from a window, and the Infanta's cheek was later repainted to cover scorch damage.
- The Spanish call the painting's alternate reading "the theology of painting"; the philosopher Michel Foucault opened his book *The Order of Things* with a long analysis of it.
- Its original title was simply *The Family of Philip IV*; the nickname *Las Meninas* — the maids of honor — only stuck in the nineteenth century.
