## The Story

A woman lies flat on a strip of bare ground in her striped robe, eyes shut, a mandolin and a clay water jar beside her. Behind her the land runs out into low hills and a band of still water. Above, a full moon in a black sky. And standing over her, close enough to touch, is a lion — mane bristling, tail up, nose lowered to her shoulder, one round eye rolled toward the viewer.

Nothing happens. That is the point, and Rousseau said so himself. Sending the picture out into the world, he explained that the traveller had lain down overcome with fatigue, and that a lion chances to pass by, picks up her scent, and does not devour her. No struggle, no rescue, no moral. Just an animal deciding, in the middle of a landscape that corresponds to no place on earth, not to.

He showed it at the Salon des Indépendants in 1897, where it sold to nobody. The following July he wrote to the mayor of Laval, the town he had been born in, offering the canvas to the municipality for something in the region of two thousand francs — roughly what a successful academic painter might ask, and a preposterous sum for a retired toll clerk whose work Paris found funny. He wanted his home town to keep a souvenir of one of its children. Laval declined.

The painting then simply vanished into a private collection — a Paris charcoal merchant's, by most accounts — and stayed lost for a quarter of a century. In 1924 the critic Louis Vauxcelles came across it again, fourteen years after Rousseau's death, at exactly the moment the avant-garde was deciding he had been a genius. The dealer Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler bought it, and an argument promptly broke out over whether it was a forgery, the picture being too strange and too accomplished to fit anyone's idea of the naive painter. It survived the doubt. Passing through the estate of the American collector John Quinn, it reached New York, and in 1939 Olga Guggenheim gave it to the Museum of Modern Art, where it has been one of the most reproduced paintings in the building ever since. The Surrealists, when they arrived, claimed him as an ancestor. He had been dead for years and had never heard of them.

## The World Behind It

France in 1897 was an empire that advertised itself. Colonial troops, Saharan expeditions and North African subjects filled the illustrated press and the walls of the official Salon, where painters who had actually travelled produced immaculate desert scenes for a public that had not. Rousseau's desert belongs to none of that. His sand is a flat brown shelf, his water sits like a mirror, and his lion is stuffed — the eyes and the mane come from a museum case, not from life.

Paris itself he knew from the inside out: he had spent more than twenty years in the toll booths of the octroi, at the seams where the city met everything outside it. Wandering musicians, travellers and Roma families were part of the street population of the period, and were routinely policed and moved on. Rousseau put one of them under a moon, gave her the loveliest robe he could paint, and let a lion stand guard.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was fifty-three and four years into a retirement he could not afford. The pension from the octroi was small; he made up the shortfall giving violin and drawing lessons, taking occasional odd jobs, and selling almost nothing. He had spent his working life in a booth at the edge of Paris and had been painting seriously for barely a decade. His first wife had died in 1888; he would marry again in 1899, and be widowed a second time within a few years.

He was also, by now, a fixture of the annual mockery. Newspapers reviewed the Indépendants partly for the pleasure of getting to Rousseau. He did not appear to take it as an argument. He wrote to mayors, he priced his canvases like a Salon star, he signed himself a painter, and he continued to produce work of a stillness that nobody else in Paris was attempting. Whatever else his self-belief was, it was not performance.

## Interesting Facts

- Rousseau described the encounter himself: the lion, he wrote, "chances to pass by, picks up her scent yet does not devour her."
- In July 1898 he tried to sell the painting to the mayor of his home town, Laval, for a sum reported at around two thousand francs. The town said no.
- The picture disappeared into a private collection and was not seen again until the critic Louis Vauxcelles rediscovered it in 1924 — fourteen years after Rousseau's death.
- When it resurfaced, a controversy broke out over whether it was a forgery; scholarship has since settled firmly on Rousseau.
- Olga Guggenheim — Mrs. Simon Guggenheim — gave it to the Museum of Modern Art in 1939, where it remains one of the collection's signature works.
- The desert corresponds to no real place, and the lion was almost certainly studied from taxidermy — Rousseau never left France.
