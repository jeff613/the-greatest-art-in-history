## The Story

Fresh from the uproar of 1905, Matisse spent that winter on a single enormous canvas meant to answer his critics not with another shocking portrait but with a vision of paradise. *The Joy of Life* shows a golden clearing where naked figures loll, embrace, play pipes, and gather flowers, and in the far distance a ring of tiny dancers wheels in a circle — the seed of the great *Dance* he would paint four years later. There is no story, no myth to decode; there is only pleasure, rendered in flat pools of rose, orange, and green, the bodies drawn in long, boneless, arabesque lines. Matisse had thrown out modelling, perspective, and consistent scale, letting each figure obey the rhythm of the picture rather than the rules of the eye.

When he unveiled it at the Salon des Indépendants in the spring of 1906 — the only major work he showed that year — it detonated. Even fellow radicals recoiled. Paul Signac, the elder statesman of Pointillism who had championed Matisse's earlier work, decided the younger man had "gone to the dogs"; others muttered that this was the end of French painting altogether. What offended them was exactly what makes the picture a landmark: colour and line set loose to build a world of their own, obedient to feeling rather than fact.

Its afterlife was as dramatic as its debut. Leo Stein bought it almost at once and hung it in the rue de Fleurus, where the young Picasso studied it with envy and answered, the next year, with the savage *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*. By 1914 the canvas had passed to the Danish collector Christian Tetzen-Lund, and in 1922 the American chemist and collector Albert C. Barnes acquired it through a Paris dealer for a small fortune. Barnes carried it home to his foundation in suburban Merion, Pennsylvania, where it hung, jealously guarded and rarely reproduced in colour, until the whole collection moved into central Philadelphia in 2012.

## The World Behind It

Matisse was reaching back into one of the oldest dreams in Western art: the pastoral golden age, the Arcadia of Poussin, Titian, and Ingres, where naked figures live at ease in an eternal landscape. He had studied Cézanne's rugged bathers and Ingres's cool, sinuous nudes, and he wanted to fuse that grand tradition with the raw colour of the new century. The result belongs to a Paris fizzing with argument about where painting should go next. Impressionism was already history; Pointillism, which broke colour into scientific dots, had its dogmatic defenders in Signac and his circle; and a younger crowd wanted colour to be emotional and decorative rather than optical. *The Joy of Life* was Matisse's manifesto in that debate — a deliberate rejection of the fussy, systematic brushwork he had himself once practised, in favour of broad, singing fields of tone. It scandalised precisely because it looked, to trained eyes, both primitive and knowing at once.

## The Artist at This Moment

The winter of 1905–06 was the most ambitious of Matisse's life. The Salon d'Automne had made him famous and infamous in the same stroke, and he understood that a reputation built on a scandal could evaporate just as fast. Rather than repeat himself, he set out to make a large, considered masterpiece that would prove Fauvism was not a stunt but a serious new language. He worked from sketches made the previous summer at Collioure on the Mediterranean, translating that sunlit coast into an imaginary Eden. He was in his mid-thirties, still far from wealthy, and staking his standing on one grand gesture. The gamble worked, though not comfortably: the picture confirmed him as the leader of the Paris avant-garde and, in doing so, lit a fire under a rival. When Picasso saw *The Joy of Life* at the Steins', he set out to make something that would eclipse it — and the two men's decades-long duel was truly under way.

## Interesting Facts

- In the distant background, a ring of dancers foreshadows Matisse's famous *Dance*, painted four years later for a Russian collector.
- Paul Signac, once an admirer, was so appalled that he declared Matisse had "gone to the dogs"; some critics feared the work signalled the end of French painting.
- Leo Stein bought the canvas soon after it was shown, and the young Picasso is said to have studied it with envy before painting *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* in response.
- Albert Barnes bought it in 1922 through the dealer Paul Guillaume; it became one of the treasures of his Pennsylvania foundation.
- Barnes kept his collection closed and for decades restricted colour reproductions of the painting, adding to its mystique.
- The Barnes Foundation moved from suburban Merion to a new building in central Philadelphia in 2012, bringing the long-hidden picture to a far wider public.
