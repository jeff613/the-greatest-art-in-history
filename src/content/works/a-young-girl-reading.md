## The Story

Beside the swings and bolted bedrooms, here is a different Fragonard entirely: quiet, tender, and utterly still. A young woman sits in profile in a lemon-yellow dress, a small book held lightly in her fingers, her whole attention poured into the page. There is no wink to the viewer, no hidden lover in the bushes, no scandal — only a private moment of absorption, painted with such warm, buttery confidence that the picture feels dashed off in a single sitting. It belongs to a group of works Fragonard called *figures de fantaisie*, "fantasy figures": rapid, bravura studies of imagined characters rather than portraits of real, named people, brushed with an ease that looks almost careless and is in fact supremely controlled.

But the calm surface hides a secret. When conservators X-rayed the canvas, they found another person underneath. Beneath the serene reader lies an earlier head — a woman turned outward, gazing directly at the viewer, wearing a feathered and beaded headdress. Fragonard had painted her first, facing us, and then, remarkably, let the picture dry for months before coming back to it. Only later did he paint out that bold, confronting face and replace it with the downcast profile we see now, softening the features, calming the gaze, and turning an outward challenge into inward quiet.

The revision left a faint trace. The repainted head is slightly small for the body beneath it, which was designed for the first, larger face — a subtle mismatch that the eye barely registers but the science reveals. It is a rare window into how even the fastest, most instinctive painter of his age actually worked: not always in one inspired burst, but returning, reconsidering, and quietly transforming a picture into something gentler than he first intended. The result is one of the most beloved images Fragonard ever made — the pure, unshowy pleasure of a girl lost in a book.

## The World Behind It

The *figures de fantaisie* were something new: not commissioned likenesses but character types, painted for the sheer virtuoso pleasure of it and prized by collectors precisely for their spontaneity. A legend grew up that Fragonard could knock one off in a single hour, and whether or not that is literally true, the pictures were built to look effortless — all flashing brushwork and swagger. This one trades the swagger for serenity, but the speed and freedom are the same.

It also catches a wider mood. The eighteenth century was an age of reading, when books, pamphlets, and novels were spreading through French society and literacy was climbing, especially among women. The image of an absorbed female reader was becoming a favorite subject across European art — a sign of refinement, interior life, and the quiet self-possession of a woman with a book. Fragonard, for all his reputation as the painter of naughty gardens, here gives that new ideal one of its loveliest and most enduring forms.

## The Artist at This Moment

This is Fragonard at the peak of his fluency, working in the private, speculative way that suited him best — for himself and for collectors, free of the Academy's demands and the labor of grand commissions. He had married Marie-Anne Gérard and settled into a productive Parisian life, turning out cabinet pictures and decorative panels for a fashionable clientele that could not get enough of his sparkling touch.

The very qualities on display here — the astonishing speed, the confidence, the refusal to fuss — were what made him both admired and, in the eyes of the rising Neoclassical critics, suspect. To them his brilliance looked like frivolity, mere dash without high moral purpose. Yet in a picture like this the dash is the point: it captures a fleeting, ordinary human moment with a lightness that more laborious painters could never touch. Within two decades the Revolution would end the market for such intimate delights, but for now Fragonard was simply the most gifted brush in France, painting a girl and her book for the joy of it.

## Interesting Facts

- An X-ray revealed an entirely different head beneath the surface: a woman in a feathered headdress facing straight out at the viewer, which Fragonard later painted over.
- Analysis of the paint layers showed the first version had dried for at least six months before he returned to repaint the head — this was a deliberate reworking, not a quick correction.
- Because the new head was painted smaller than the original one, it sits slightly out of proportion on a body designed for a larger face.
- The picture belongs to Fragonard's *figures de fantaisie*, dashing "fantasy figures" that a persistent legend claimed he could each paint in about an hour.
- The National Gallery of Art bought the painting in 1961 with funds given by Ailsa Mellon Bruce, daughter of the museum's founder Andrew Mellon, and it has become one of the collection's most cherished works.
- Unlike Fragonard's flirtatious garden scenes, the girl gives the viewer nothing — she is entirely, contentedly lost in her reading.
