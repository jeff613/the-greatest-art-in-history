## The Story

A Virgin in deep blue sits on a wooden throne with the child on her arm, six angels packed around her in two stacks of three, everything set against unbroken gold. It is more than four metres tall. It was painted for the church of San Francesco at Pisa, and it is the picture that shows most clearly what Cimabue was and what he was not.

What he was not: a naturalist. The proportions are governed by rank, not by anything the eye reports. The Virgin is enormous, the angels are interchangeable, the child is a small adult, the gold behind them is not sky or wall but the flat eternal nothing of Byzantine tradition. Faces are assembled from inherited components — the long nose running straight from the brow, the shadowed hollows under the eyes, the tiny mouth. Cimabue had learned a system, and here he is using it at full stretch.

What he was: a man leaning on that system until it creaked. The throne is a real object, built in receding planes, its steps and columns arranged so the eye can walk into them. The angels stand behind one another rather than beside; the ones at the back are genuinely further away. The Virgin's mantle falls in folds that follow a body underneath. And the faces, formulaic as they are, have started to differ from one another. One or two of the angels look like they might be somebody in particular.

That is the whole story of Cimabue in one panel. He did not invent a new way of seeing. He took a manner that had been stable for centuries and applied so much pressure to it — bigger figures, deeper thrones, more weight, more individuality — that the thing began to give way. Within about twenty years a painter who trained in this tradition would step through the gap and start painting people who stand on floors in rooms.

Napoleon's armies took the panel out of Pisa in 1813, and unlike much of what they carried off, it never went back. It has hung in the Louvre ever since, in the room where the museum's Italian primitives begin.

## The World Behind It

Pisa in 1280 was a maritime republic with an empire in the western Mediterranean, a cathedral complex already famous, and a leaning bell tower that had been tilting for a hundred years. It was also running out of time: within a decade the Genoese would destroy its fleet at Meloria, and Pisa would begin a decline it never reversed.

The Franciscans who commissioned this panel were the growth industry of thirteenth-century religion — an order barely fifty years old, spreading across Italy, building large churches in every city and filling them with images meant to move ordinary people rather than instruct scholars. A gigantic gold Virgin above the altar was both a devotional object and a statement about how much the order now mattered.

## The Artist at This Moment

Cimabue was around forty. He had been documented in Rome eight years earlier, which is the first hard date in his life, and had presumably learned there whatever the surviving late-antique and early Christian work of the city had to teach.

He was operating at the top of his trade: only an established master got a four-metre altarpiece for a major Franciscan church in a wealthy republic. Ahead of him lay Assisi, the Santa Croce crucifix, and the Santa Trinita panel. Behind him lay a training whose details are entirely lost.

Pisa would also be where he finished. Twenty-one years after this, he came back to the city to make a mosaic for the cathedral — the one job we can prove he had — and died there in 1302.

## Interesting Facts

- It was painted for the Franciscan church of San Francesco in Pisa, the city where Cimabue would eventually die.
- French troops removed it from Pisa in 1813, and it never returned; it has been in the Louvre ever since.
- At over four metres tall it is among the largest surviving panel paintings of the thirteenth century.
- The throne is built in receding planes with a visible structure — the clearest sign of what Cimabue was pushing against in the Byzantine manner.
- Like nearly everything attributed to him, it carries no signature or document; the attribution is stylistic.
- The only work Cimabue can be documented as having made is a mosaic in Pisa Cathedral, contracted in 1301, the year before his death.
