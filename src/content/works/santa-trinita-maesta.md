## The Story

The Virgin sits enthroned with the child on her knee, and eight angels are ranged up the sides of the throne in two columns of four, their haloes overlapping like scales. Below, in the arcade beneath her feet, four half-length figures look out: two Old Testament prophets and, in the middle, Jeremiah and Abraham holding scrolls. The whole thing is nearly four metres high and was made for the high altar of the church of Santa Trinita in Florence.

Look at it beside the altarpieces of fifty years earlier and the difference is in the furniture. The throne is not a flat gold pattern any more. It has a front and sides, an arcade you can see through, a structure that recedes; it occupies space. The angels are not floating in an abstract field but standing behind and beside it, one behind another, in an order the eye can follow. Under the Virgin's blue mantle there is a body with knees.

None of this is realism. The gold ground still swallows everything, the faces are still built from the inherited Byzantine formulas, the scale is still governed by holiness rather than distance. Cimabue was not trying to paint the world. But he was pushing hard on a system that had held steady for centuries, and the pressure shows.

Attribution here is the usual Cimabue problem. Nothing survives that says he painted it. It is given to him on style and on old tradition, and its date is argued — most now put it around 1290 to 1300, which would make it late, possibly his most ambitious surviving panel.

It hung in Santa Trinita until the eighteenth century, moved to the Accademia, and finally reached the Uffizi, where it now hangs in a room with Duccio's *Rucellai Madonna* and Giotto's *Ognissanti Madonna* — three enormous enthroned Virgins made within about forty years of each other, the whole argument of early Italian painting laid out on three walls. Cimabue's is the oldest of the three, and the one visitors walk past on their way to the others.

## The World Behind It

Florence in the 1290s was booming and unstable in equal measure. Its bankers were financing half of Europe, the gold florin was becoming the continent's reliable currency, and the city was tearing itself apart between Guelf and Ghibelline and then between factions of Guelfs — the quarrel that would exile Dante in 1302, the same year Cimabue died.

All that money went into building. The new cathedral was begun in 1296; Santa Croce and Santa Maria Novella were rising for the Franciscans and Dominicans; and every one of those churches needed a monumental altarpiece. The huge enthroned Virgin, the *Maestà*, was the format of the moment, and painters competed for the commissions on a scale that made panel painting a serious industry for the first time in Italy.

## The Artist at This Moment

If the late dating is right, Cimabue was around sixty and near the end. He had worked in Rome, at Assisi, and across Tuscany, and he had one last commission ahead of him — the mosaic of Saint John the Evangelist in Pisa Cathedral, contracted in 1301, which is the only work anyone can document as his.

He was also, by now, being overtaken. Giotto was in his thirties and doing things with weight and human expression that would make this manner look archaic within twenty years. Dante, writing a decade or so after Cimabue's death, put the demotion into a single famous couplet about how Cimabue thought he held the field and then Giotto took the cry.

Whether Cimabue lived long enough to feel that happening, nobody knows. He died at Pisa in 1302.

## Interesting Facts

- The throne is the point: it has depth, sides, and an arcade you can see through, at a moment when most sacred figures still sat on flat gold pattern.
- The four figures in the arcade below are prophets, two of them holding scrolls that point to the birth of Christ.
- Nothing documents Cimabue as its painter — the attribution rests on style and long tradition, like almost everything given to him.
- It hangs in the Uffizi alongside Duccio's *Rucellai Madonna* and Giotto's *Ognissanti Madonna*, three giant enthroned Virgins painted within about forty years of one another.
- Cimabue's is the earliest of the three, and the one that makes the other two look modern.
- Its date is disputed; most scholars now place it around 1290–1300, near the end of his life.
