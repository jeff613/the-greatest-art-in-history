## The Story

In the spring of 1863 the jury of the Paris Salon was in an unusually savage mood, throwing out more than half of the thousands of works submitted. Manet had three pictures refused, among them a large canvas he then called *Le Bain* — The Bath. So many artists were rejected, and complained so loudly, that word reached the top. Emperor Napoleon III, sensitive to public opinion even when his own taste ran to the conventional, decreed that the rejected works should be shown in their own exhibition nearby so the public could judge for itself. This was the celebrated Salon des Refusés, the Salon of the Refused.

There Manet's picture became the sensation of the season — the thing everyone went to sneer at. The scene is odd enough to explain the fuss. Two young men in the frock coats and student caps of contemporary Paris lounge on the grass of a wood, deep in idle conversation. Between them sits a completely naked woman, utterly at ease, chin on hand, gazing out of the canvas straight at us as though nothing could be more natural. A fourth figure, a woman in a thin shift, bathes in the stream behind. A picnic is scattered in the foreground; her discarded clothes spill beside it.

Viewers could not decide whether it was incompetent or indecent, and mostly settled on both. The nudity was not the real problem — the Salon was full of nude goddesses. The problem was the mixture: a bare woman who was clearly no goddess but a real, nameless Parisian, sitting brazenly among clothed men in modern dress, staring back without a trace of shame. It looked less like myth than like something glimpsed in the Bois de Boulogne. Manet, characteristically, was quoting the old masters — the grouping comes almost directly from a Renaissance engraving after Raphael, and the theme of nudes and clothed figures in a landscape from Venetian painting. But by dressing the men in the clothes of 1863, he stripped the alibi from the whole tradition and handed the public a scandal.

## The World Behind It

The Salon was the beating heart of French artistic life, a vast state-run exhibition that could crown or ruin a painter in a season. To be refused was a professional disaster; to be refused in a year of mass rejections felt, to many, like an injustice worth protesting. Napoleon III's decision to mount the Salon des Refusés was a shrewd political gesture — letting the public play judge diffused the anger of the excluded — but it accidentally created the founding event of modern art, a stage where outsiders could be seen precisely because the establishment had shut them out.

Napoleon III himself is said to have judged Manet's canvas an offense against decency, and the crowds largely agreed, filing past to laugh. Yet the exhibition drew enormous numbers and made the refused painters famous in their notoriety. For a rising generation, the lesson was intoxicating: the official jury was not the only door, and rejection could itself be a kind of arrival.

## The Artist at This Moment

Manet was thirty-one and, in the same charged year of 1863, doing two things at once that would define his life. He married Suzanne Leenhoff, the Dutch piano teacher who had long been part of his household, quietly regularizing a private life he kept discreet. And he painted the two pictures — this luncheon and Olympia — that would make his name a byword for outrage.

He had not set out to lead a revolution. A polished, sociable bourgeois who wanted the Salon's respect, he genuinely seems to have expected admiration for his sophisticated reworking of the old masters. The models were people close to him: the nude was Victorine Meurent, his favorite sitter, and the two lounging men were drawn from his own brother and his new brother-in-law, Ferdinand Leenhoff. It was a family picnic, in effect, turned into the most talked-about painting in France — the first great shock of a career that would never stop delivering them.

## Interesting Facts

- Manet originally titled the work *Le Bain* (The Bath); the name by which we know it, *Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe*, came later.
- The central grouping of three figures is borrowed almost pose-for-pose from a 16th-century engraving of river gods after Raphael, and the theme echoes Venetian paintings of nudes in a landscape.
- The naked woman is Victorine Meurent, the same model who would sit for the even more scandalous Olympia.
- The two clothed men were posed by Manet's brother Gustave and his future brother-in-law, the sculptor Ferdinand Leenhoff.
- Napoleon III created the Salon des Refusés in 1863 to quiet artists' protests, then reportedly pronounced Manet's picture indecent when he saw it.
- The painting is deliberately large — over two meters wide — the scale usually reserved for grand history painting, which made its everyday, improper subject all the more provocative.
