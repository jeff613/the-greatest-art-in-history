## The Story

At first glance it is simply a busy Flemish village: houses, a tavern, a river, a strip of sea, and dozens of people all frantically doing something. Look longer and you realize that every last one of them is a joke. This is a painting of proverbs — more than a hundred folk sayings, each acted out literally by the figures in the scene, packed edge to edge into one teeming panorama of human folly.

A man in armor bites into a grindstone; another bangs his head against a brick wall. Someone tosses roses — the older form of "pearls" — before a herd of pigs. A figure carries daylight out to the field in a basket, another tries to catch a fish with his bare hands, a third holds a slippery eel by the tail. On the roof a fool cuts loose the tiles; in the water a man swims against the tide. At the very center, in a red dress, a woman drapes a blue cloak over her husband's shoulders — she is "hanging the blue cloak on him," that is, cuckolding him and making a fool of him to his face. So central is that image that the painting's oldest known title is simply *The Blue Cloak*.

Almost none of these people are behaving sensibly, and that is exactly the point. The world Bruegel shows is the world turned upside down — an inverted globe even hangs on one of the buildings as a sign. It is a vast comic sermon on the absurdity and wickedness of human beings, who bang, cheat, gossip, overreach, and deceive one another in a hundred small ways at once. But there is no preacher's finger-wagging in it. Bruegel clearly relishes the whole spectacle, and hides his sayings so cleverly that viewers have been hunting them down and arguing over the count for four and a half centuries.

## The World Behind It

Bruegel painted this in Antwerp in 1559, at a moment when educated Europeans were mad for proverbs. The great humanist Erasmus had published his *Adagia*, a hugely popular collection of classical sayings, earlier in the century, and gathering, quoting, and illustrating folk wisdom was a fashionable pastime. Proverbs were seen as little nuggets of shared truth — and also, increasingly, as material for comedy. Bruegel's genius was to take the whole genre and stage it all at once, cramming an entire encyclopedia of sayings into a single street.

The painting sits at the start of the run of works that made Bruegel's name. He was still working closely with the Antwerp print publisher Hieronymus Cock, and the crowded, catalogue-like design — dozens of separate incidents, each legible on its own — comes straight out of that world of moralizing prints, where a single sheet had to carry a whole lesson. It was the same year, 1559, that he changed how he signed his name, dropping the "h" to become "Bruegel."

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1559 Bruegel was a rising figure in Antwerp, a few years back from Italy and established as a master with a growing reputation. He was not yet the painter of quiet peasant seasons; he was making dense, witty, faintly moralizing pictures full of proverbs, children's games, and battling figures — the work of a sharp urban observer cataloguing the whole comedy of human behavior.

He was also still a bachelor with his big life changes ahead of him. Marriage, the move to Brussels, and the great late masterpieces were all still to come; his sons, who would turn out copies of his work by the dozen, were not yet born. *Netherlandish Proverbs* catches him at his most encyclopedic and playful, delighting in the sheer variety of ways people manage to make fools of themselves — a delight he would carry, softened and deepened, into everything he painted afterward.

## Interesting Facts

- Scholars count the proverbs and idioms hidden in the scene at somewhere between about a hundred and a hundred and twenty-five; the exact number is still argued over, partly because some sayings have been lost to time.
- Many are still in everyday use: "banging your head against a brick wall," "armed to the teeth," "casting pearls before swine," "swimming against the tide," and "the big fish eat the little fish."
- Its oldest title is *The Blue Cloak*, after the woman at the center draping a blue cloak on her husband — the period's image for a wife deceiving him.
- Bruegel's son Pieter Brueghel the Younger and his workshop made many copies of the painting, a sign of how popular the crowded, puzzle-like design remained for generations.
- The panel entered Berlin's Gemäldegalerie in 1914 and hangs there today, still drawing visitors who stand in front of it playing spot-the-proverb.
