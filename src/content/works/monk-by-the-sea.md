## The Story

In October 1810 the Berlin Academy opened its exhibition, and in one of the rooms hung two canvases by a Dresden painter almost nobody had heard of. Friedrich had asked for them to be hung as a pair, one above the other. Below: a ruined Gothic abbey in a winter oakwood, a funeral procession creeping through the snow toward it. Above: this.

A strip of pale sand. A dark low band of sea. And then sky, taking up almost the whole canvas, shading from a bruised green-black at the horizon up into cold, thin blue. Standing on the sand, no taller than a finger joint, is one figure in a robe, back turned, hands at his head.

What made it a scandal was everything Friedrich had left out. A landscape was supposed to invite you in — a tree at the edge, a path, a foreground where the eye could plant itself before setting off. This offered no way in and nothing to hold. Technical examination has since found two small sailing ships painted on the horizon and then deliberately painted out: he had put company into the picture and taken it away again.

Berliners argued in front of it. The poets Clemens Brentano and Achim von Arnim wrote up a review, four handwritten pages between them, and handed it to Heinrich von Kleist, who was editing the *Berliner Abendblätter*. Kleist cut it to shreds, rewrote it as his own, and ran it on 13 October under Brentano's initials — which left Brentano furious. But the line Kleist put in has outlived everyone's temper: standing before the picture, he wrote, is as if one's eyelids had been cut away. Goethe, who had handed Friedrich a prize five years earlier, was unimpressed, and is reported to have said that one ought to break Friedrich's pictures over the edge of a table.

Then came the ending nobody predicted. King Frederick William III bought both paintings straight out of the exhibition — reportedly at the urging of his fifteen-year-old son, the future Frederick William IV. Friedrich, thirty-six and provincial, was suddenly a painter the Prussian court collected. Within months the Berlin Academy elected him a member.

## The World Behind It

Prussia in 1810 was a beaten state. Napoleon had destroyed its army at Jena and Auerstedt four years earlier, ridden into Berlin, and stripped away roughly half the kingdom at the peace. The court had fled east; Queen Luise, who had become the emotional centre of Prussian resistance, died that July at thirty-four, and the country went into real mourning. The same year Berlin opened a new university, an act of national self-repair carried out because there was little else left to do.

Into that room came a picture of a man standing alone at the edge of an indifferent sea with nothing behind him and nothing coming. Nobody had to be told what the emptiness might mean.

Friedrich himself was not a Prussian subject. He worked in Dresden, capital of Saxony, which had thrown in with Napoleon and would stay loyal to him almost to the end — a choice that cost the kingdom dearly in 1815. The painter whose lonely canvases the Prussian king bought was living in an allied French satellite, saying nothing in public.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was thirty-six, twelve years into Dresden, and only just becoming visible. Two years earlier he had put a landscape on an altar — *The Cross in the Mountains*, a mountain crucifix in evening light, made for a chapel — and the critic Basilius von Ramdohr had attacked it in print, asking whether landscape painting had any business creeping onto altars. Friedrich answered with a written explanation of his own picture, the only time in his life he ever did so. It was his introduction to the German public, and it came as a fight.

His father had died in November 1809, the last of the Greifswald household he had grown up in. He worked in a studio so bare that visitors remarked on it, and his friend Georg Friedrich Kersting painted the room twice: one window, one easel, a ruler and a palette on the wall, nothing else. Friedrich did not paint from nature at the easel. He walked, drew, came home, shut the door, and assembled.

## Interesting Facts

- Technical examination revealed two small sailing ships on the horizon that Friedrich painted out, stripping the last companionship from the picture.
- At Friedrich's request it hung directly above its companion, *The Abbey in the Oakwood*, in Berlin in 1810; the two still hang together in the Alte Nationalgalerie.
- Kleist rewrote Brentano and Arnim's review beyond recognition and published it under Brentano's initials — an editorial hijacking that has become part of the painting's legend.
- Goethe, an early supporter, is reported to have said that Friedrich's pictures ought to be broken over the edge of a table.
- Both paintings were restored between 2013 and 2016; stripping away two centuries of yellowed varnish brought the sky back to blue.
- The lone figure is often called a self-portrait, though nothing in the record confirms it.
