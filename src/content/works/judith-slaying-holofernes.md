## The Story

The maid has her whole weight on him. She is kneeling across his chest with both arms locked on his right wrist, and he has a fistful of her hair and is going nowhere. Judith stands over the bed with one arm braced, pushing his head down and away from her the way you would hold something you did not want splashing you, and with the other she is drawing a long sword through his throat. Blood is running down the white sheets in three straight lines. Neither woman is screaming. Neither is looking away. Their sleeves are pushed up past the elbow.

The Book of Judith tells a siege story. The Assyrian army has surrounded Bethulia and the wells are running dry. Judith, a rich widow, dresses, walks out to the enemy camp with her maid, and lets the general Holofernes believe she has come to him. She waits four days. On the fourth night he drinks himself unconscious in his own tent, and she takes his sword off the bedpost.

Caravaggio had painted the identical instant in Rome around 1599, and Artemisia grew up in a house where his pictures were the thing to beat. In his version Judith leans back from the blood with her arms straight, her face caught between disgust and effort, while an ancient servant waits with a cloth. In this one there is no distance. There are three people on one bed and two of them are winning.

She painted a first version of this composition in Rome around 1612–13; it hangs in Naples. This is the second, made in Florence about 1620, close to life size. Cosimo II de' Medici commissioned it for a group of biblical heroines at the Palazzo Pitti, at a price of fifty scudi. The Grand Duke died in 1621 and the money did not come. A letter she wrote in 1635 thanks Galileo Galilei — an acquaintance from her Florentine years — for helping her prise the payment out of the Medici court, fifteen years late.

The Uffizi's own account says the painting was denied a place in the gallery when it arrived, and a story has followed it ever since that a grand duchess found it too gruesome and had it shifted somewhere out of the way. Since the 1970s it has most often been read as revenge — Judith as Artemisia, Holofernes as the man she prosecuted in 1612. It may well be right, but it is a reading, and she never said so. The subject was standard, chosen by her patron, painted by half of Baroque Europe. Recent scholarship pushes back hard at treating her forty-year career as an answer to a single week of her life.

## The World Behind It

Florence between 1613 and 1620 was the most useful place in Italy for an ambitious painter to be. The Medici court under Cosimo II bought aggressively; Galileo had been the Grand Duke's mathematician since 1610, having named the moons of Jupiter after the family; and the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno, founded by Vasari, decided which artists counted.

In 1616 the Accademia admitted Artemisia — the first woman in its history. Membership was not honorary decoration. It meant she could buy her own materials, sign her own contracts and take commissions without a man's countersignature, which is to say it meant she could run a business. She also learned, in these years, to read and write. Michelangelo Buonarroti the Younger hired her to paint a figure for the ceiling of the gallery he was building to glorify his great-uncle, and is reported to have paid her several times what the other painters on that ceiling received.

It did not last. She and her husband spent faster than the commissions came in, and by 1620 the creditors had made Florence impossible.

## The Artist at This Moment

She was about twenty-seven and outwardly settled: married to a Florentine painter named Pierantonio Stiattesi, mother of five children of whom only a daughter, Prudenzia, would survive to grow up.

The private version came to light in 2011, when an archive in Florence gave up thirty-six letters written between 1616 and 1620. They are between Artemisia, her husband, and a Florentine nobleman called Francesco Maria Maringhi, who was her lover and also, conveniently, the couple's creditor. Her letters to him are jealous, furious and unguarded. Her husband wrote to him too, praising his wife's success and asking for money; whatever the arrangement was, both of them were in it.

By the time she finished this canvas, the debts had closed in and the marriage was effectively over. She left for Rome and never lived with Stiattesi again. She was twenty-seven, and had been carrying a household on her own painting for most of a decade.

## Interesting Facts

- The canvas is 199 by 162.5 centimetres — the figures are close to life size, which is much of the reason it is hard to stand in front of.
- She painted an earlier version of the same composition around 1612–13, now in the Museo di Capodimonte in Naples; this Florentine one is the larger and later of the two.
- Her fee was fifty scudi, and she had to write to Galileo Galilei in 1635 for help extracting it from the Medici court.
- The Uffizi says the painting was refused a place in the gallery when it arrived, and tradition holds that a grand duchess had it moved somewhere less visible because she could not stand to look at it.
- Her father Orazio was close enough to Caravaggio to be pulled into a libel suit with him in 1603, where he testified to lending the painter a Capuchin habit and a pair of stage wings.
- In 1616 she became the first woman ever admitted to Florence's Accademia delle Arti del Disegno, which gave her the legal standing to trade as a painter in her own name.
