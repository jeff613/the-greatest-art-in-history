## The Story

A wagon piled with hay is moving across the middle of the picture, and the crowd around it has lost its mind. Men throw ladders against the load and climb; a woman is hauled up by the arm; two men go at each other with knives over an armful of dry grass. Under the wheels, a body is being crushed and nobody has stopped to look. Behind the cart, at a careful distance, ride the powers of the earth — a column under an imperial eagle banner, a mitre, a turbaned prince — following the hay without soiling their hands. In front of it, the wagon is being drawn by a team of demons: a fish walking upright, a stag's head on a human body, a green-cloaked thing with webbed feet, hauling the load on into the right-hand panel, where hell is under construction.

The joke, and it is a bitter one, comes from an old Netherlandish saying: the world is a haystack, and each man pulls from it what he can grab. Hay is worth nothing. That is the point. Everything the crowd is killing for will be fed to animals or left to rot.

On top of the load, above the scrum, a couple kiss behind a bush while a woman sings from a songbook and a man plays a lute. An angel with pink wings kneels among them with her hands folded, looking up. On the far side of the same bush, a blue winged demon with a long snout plays music of his own. Directly overhead, in a cloud, Christ holds out his arms and shows the wounds in his hands, and in the entire painting only the angel is looking his way.

The left wing runs the story backwards to its source: rebel angels tumbling out of a bright sky as insects, God drawing Eve out of the sleeping Adam, the serpent at the tree, and the pair driven out of the garden by an angel with a sword. The right wing is what the cart is heading for — a round tower going up on a burning plain, hauled into place by demons, the damned carried in on the backs of monsters.

Bosch signed it, in plain letters, on the front of the central panel. He signed very little.

## The World Behind It

The Low Countries in Bosch's last years were rich, crowded and anxious. His own town lived on cloth, brewing, metalwork and pilgrims, who came to Saint John's church for a miracle-working statue of the Virgin. Money moved, and preachers said what preachers say about money.

The haystack image was already in circulation before Bosch used it — it turns up in a moralising Netherlandish poem of around 1460 to 1470, and behind that lies the older line from Isaiah that all flesh is grass. Bosch's contribution was to make the proverb enormous and to put a pope in it.

That is not yet Protestantism. Luther's theses were a year away when Bosch died, and there is nothing in the picture that a devout Catholic of 1516 could not have said out loud: the clergy are as greedy as everyone else, and the world is a scramble that ends badly. Later, in Spain, that was exactly the debate the painting caused — whether the man who made it was a moralist or a heretic.

## The Artist at This Moment

This is late Bosch. Study of the panels puts the wood and the painting close to the end of his life, around 1516, the year the Brotherhood of Our Lady recorded his funeral mass in Saint John's. He was somewhere near sixty-five.

By then he was a man of standing in a small town — married into property, a sworn member of the Brotherhood, painting for patrons who lived far outside Brabant. Whatever nervous energy is in the pictures, the life around them looks stable and comfortable: a prosperous burgher on the market square who spent forty years painting the end of the world.

He also had imitators. A workshop copied him, Antwerp dealers sold pictures in his manner, and within a generation Bosch was a brand rather than a man. A second version of the Haywain, almost identical, ended up in the Escorial, and scholars argued for a century over which of the two came first.

## Interesting Facts

- Bosch signed the front of the central panel *Jheronimus Bosch* — a rare thing in his surviving work.
- Two versions of the Haywain both belonged to Philip II of Spain; the Bosch Research and Conservation Project concluded that the Prado panel is the original and the Escorial one a later copy.
- Philip II acquired it in a group of six works in 1570 and had them shipped to El Escorial four years later.
- Shut, the triptych shows a shabby wayfarer on a road, dogs snapping at his heels, a robbery in one corner and a gallows in the distance.
- The panels were separated at some point and only put back together as one triptych at the Prado in 1914.
- Of the hundreds of figures below Christ in the cloud, only the small pink-winged angel on top of the hay is looking up at him.
