## The Story

The tiger is caught mid-spring, ears flat, teeth bared, eyes wide with something closer to alarm than appetite. The grass has been flattened sideways by wind. Silver lines of rain fall in a hard diagonal across the entire canvas, over leaves and trunks and the animal itself, drawn in one direction as though somebody had ruled them. Whatever the tiger has surprised is outside the picture. That is the whole trick of the thing: the title says *Surpris!*, and it is never clear who is surprising whom.

Rousseau sent it to the Salon des Indépendants in the spring of 1891. He was forty-seven, still working shifts for the Paris toll service, and this was his first jungle — the picture that started the strain of work he is now known for and that he would return to, on and off, for the last twenty years of his life. He had never seen a jungle, a tiger in the wild, or any country outside France. The leaves came from the hothouses at the Jardin des Plantes, the animals from the taxidermy cases at the natural history museum a short walk away and from illustrated books and prints. He built the foliage up in layer on layer of separate greens, and dragged the rain across the finished surface in thin metallic strokes.

Most of the critics who bothered to write about it treated him as a joke — the amateur, the Sunday painter, the man who could not draw. Then a young Swiss painter named Félix Vallotton reviewed the Indépendants and said the opposite. Rousseau's tiger surprising its prey, he wrote, ought not to be missed: it was the alpha and the omega of painting. It was the first serious notice Rousseau ever received, and he did not get much more of that kind for another fifteen years.

The National Gallery in London bought the picture in 1972, and it now hangs in the same building as Titian and Rembrandt — an outcome nobody at the 1891 Indépendants would have predicted for a canvas by a customs clerk.

## The World Behind It

Paris in 1891 was in the middle of a long infatuation with the tropics. France was building an empire across West Africa and Indochina, the great colonial expositions were pulling in millions, illustrated magazines ran engravings of big game and dense forest, and the Jardin des Plantes kept both a menagerie and a set of vast glasshouses where anyone with the price of admission could stand among plants from the equator. Rousseau, who lived nearby, was one of them. He told the critic Arsène Alexandre that when he went into the glasshouses and saw the strange plants of exotic lands, it seemed to him he was entering a dream.

The Salon des Indépendants, founded in 1884, was the one place that would take him. It had no jury and awarded no prizes: pay the fee and your canvas goes on the wall. That policy is the reason a toll clerk's tiger hung in Paris at all, and also the reason the fashionable public came partly to laugh.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was still employed. Retirement was two years off, and his days were spent at a barrier on the edge of the city recording casks and carcasses as they came in. He painted in the evenings and on his days off, in a rented room, and had been showing at the Indépendants every year since 1886 to steady public amusement.

His first wife, Clémence, had died in 1888; most of the children they had together did not survive childhood. He was a widower in his late forties with a modest salary, no training beyond some advice from a couple of academic painters, and an absolute conviction that he belonged in the company of the men whose work hung in the Salon proper. Nothing in his circumstances supported that conviction, and he held it anyway. He signed the tiger in the bottom left corner, dated it 1891, and went back to the gate.

## Interesting Facts

- Rousseau exhibited it as *Surpris!* — Surprised! — and never showed what the tiger had surprised, leaving the object of the ambush permanently offstage.
- The rain is trailed across the whole finished surface in fine metallic strokes, applied over the leaves and the animal alike.
- The Swiss painter Félix Vallotton, reviewing the 1891 Indépendants, called the tiger "the alpha and the omega of painting" — the first serious praise of Rousseau's career.
- Rousseau let people believe he had seen such landscapes on military service in Mexico. He never left France; historians have found nothing to support the story.
- His jungles were assembled from the glasshouses of the Jardin des Plantes, stuffed animals in the natural history museum, and illustrated books.
- The National Gallery acquired the painting in 1972, eighty-one years after critics used it as a punchline.
