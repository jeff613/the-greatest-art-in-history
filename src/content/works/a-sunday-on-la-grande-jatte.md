## The Story

The island of La Grande Jatte sits in the Seine on the northwestern edge of Paris, and in the 1880s it was where the city went on Sunday — clerks, shopgirls, soldiers, nursemaids, a few of the fashionable and a good many of the not. Seurat went there for two years with a small panel under his arm, sat on the grass, and studied it.

Then he built the picture. He worked on the vast canvas from May 1884 to March 1885, put it aside, and came back to it from October 1885 to May 1886, laying in a fabric of small, separate touches of unmixed colour that were meant to fuse in the viewer's eye rather than on the palette. He made dozens of preparatory drawings and oil studies first — a whole apparatus of research behind a scene of people doing nothing in particular.

And that is the strange part. This is a painting of leisure in which nobody looks like they are enjoying themselves. The figures stand and sit in profile or dead frontal, spaced out along the bank like pieces on a board, stiff as Egyptian reliefs. A woman on the right holds a monkey on a leash. A little girl in white stares straight out at us, the only one who does. Nobody talks to anybody. Critics have argued ever since about whether Seurat was mocking the bourgeoisie, mourning modern loneliness, or simply pursuing an idea about colour and structure so far that human warmth got squeezed out on the way. He left no explanation.

It went up in May 1886 at the eighth and final Impressionist exhibition — an event that half tore the group apart, since Monet, Renoir, and Sisley all stayed away rather than hang alongside it. The younger critics understood immediately that something had happened. Félix Fénéon named the new manner Neo-Impressionism, and Seurat, at twenty-six, found himself the leader of a movement.

## The World Behind It

Paris in the 1880s had just been rebuilt and was still deciding what the new city meant. Haussmann's boulevards had cut the medieval town apart; the railways had put the countryside within an afternoon's reach; and a new class of salaried people had acquired something their parents had not — a free Sunday. The Grande Jatte was one place they spent it, along with the riverside cafés and boating spots that Renoir and Monet had been painting for a decade.

Seurat's version has none of Renoir's flushed sociability. The people on his island are precisely dressed, precisely spaced, and utterly self-contained, and it is easy to read the picture as a comment on a society organising itself into types. The island itself did not stay bucolic: it was industrialised within a generation, and is now public gardens.

## The Artist at This Moment

Seurat was in his mid-twenties and working with a discipline that unnerved people. He had come out of the École des Beaux-Arts, done his military service, and then spent years reading colour theory — Chevreul, Charles Blanc, the physicist Ogden Rood — with the seriousness of a man assembling a system rather than a style.

The rejection of *Bathers at Asnières* by the Salon in 1884 had pushed him, with others, into founding the Société des Artistes Indépendants, a body with no jury and no prizes. The Grande Jatte was his proof that the new method could carry a picture of any ambition.

He was also, characteristically, saying almost nothing about himself. He lived with his mother, worked in a studio nearby, dressed like a notary, and let his friends assume there was nothing to tell. There was: within a few years he would be keeping a partner and a son entirely hidden from them.

## Interesting Facts

- Seurat worked on it in two long stretches across two years and made dozens of preliminary drawings and oil studies first.
- In 1889 he restretched the canvas and painted a border of dots directly onto its edge, to bridge the picture and its frame — the border you can still see.
- The zinc yellow he used for the sunlit grass has chemically degraded and darkened to brown, so the painting is measurably less brilliant than when he made it.
- The woman at the right is walking a monkey on a leash; no one has ever fully explained her.
- Monet, Renoir, and Sisley refused to take part in the 1886 exhibition where it debuted, partly because of work like this.
- The Art Institute of Chicago bought it in 1924 for $24,000 — roughly a third of a million in today's money, for one of the most famous paintings on earth.
