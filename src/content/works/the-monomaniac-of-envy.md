## The Story

She is not posing. Someone has sat her down in front of a painter — white bonnet, ribbons hanging loose and untied, a red shift under a dark shawl — and she is looking slightly past him with an expression that refuses to resolve into anything you can name. Her eyes are rimmed with red. Her mouth is set. Behind her there is nothing at all: no room, no props, no story.

Around 1822 Géricault painted ten heads like this, of patients in the Paris asylums — the Salpêtrière, which held women, and Bicêtre, which held men. They were made for Étienne-Jean Georget, a young physician at the Salpêtrière and a pupil of Esquirol, who had coined the term monomania: an insanity confined to a single fixed idea, leaving the rest of the mind apparently sound. Georget's patients were catalogued by their obsessions, and the paintings came down to us catalogued the same way. This one is La Monomane de l'envie, the monomaniac of envy, and it carries a crueller old nickname too: the Hyena of the Salpêtrière.

What is startling is not the subject but the treatment. There is no caricature here, no grotesque, no moral, no wagging finger. The woman gets the dark ground, the close framing and the unhurried attention of a commissioned portrait of somebody important. Géricault does not tell you what is wrong with her. He puts a face in front of you and lets you fail to read it — which is, more or less, what the doctors were also doing.

Then the pictures disappeared. Georget kept them until his death in 1828, at thirty-three, and afterwards they scattered and were lost for a generation. In 1863 five of them turned up, the story goes, by chance in an attic in Baden-Baden. The critic Louis Viardot described them in the Gazette des Beaux-Arts in January 1864, and that is how they re-entered art history. The five are the monomaniac of envy in Lyon, of gambling in the Louvre, of child abduction in Springfield, Massachusetts, of military command in Winterthur, and of theft in Ghent. The other five have never been found.

## The World Behind It

Paris in the 1820s was where the modern idea of mental illness was being built. A generation earlier Philippe Pinel had begun treating the inmates of Bicêtre and the Salpêtrière as patients rather than prisoners; his successors, Esquirol and then Esquirol's own pupils, set about sorting what they saw into categories. Monomania was the most useful of those categories and the most dangerous, because it implied that a person could be entirely lucid, entirely responsible-looking, and still not answerable for a single act.

Georget cared about that implication. He divided monomania into kinds — religious, erotic, homicidal — and argued that some criminals belonged in an asylum rather than under the blade. That argument needed evidence, and portraits of patients who looked like anybody's aunt or neighbour made it better than any description could: here is what madness looks like when it does not look like anything.

Underneath all of it sat the century's confidence that character is legible on a face — physiognomy, and in its harder form phrenology, then near the height of its prestige. These paintings belong to that confidence and quietly defeat it. You can look at this woman for a long time and learn nothing whatever about envy.

## The Artist at This Moment

He had about eighteen months to live, and something was already wrong.

Géricault had come back from nearly two years in England in 1821 and had not been properly well since finishing the Raft — fevers, collapses, and, by some accounts, a nervous breakdown severe enough to frighten his friends. Whether he was ever Georget's own patient has been suggested often enough that it now circulates as fact; there is no good evidence for it, and it should be treated as a suggestion.

What is certain is that the enormous public pictures had stopped. In his last two or three years the work got smaller and more private: horses, lithographs of the London poor, studies of severed limbs, and these ten heads. Then he was thrown from a horse onto a pile of stones and hurt his spine. He got back on. An abscess formed on his back, swelled, burst when he collided with another rider, and spread infection through a body that already had tuberculosis in it. He spent his last months wasting in bed, in a great deal of pain, and died on 26 January 1824 at thirty-two.

## Interesting Facts

- Géricault painted ten of these portraits. Five survive — envy in Lyon, gambling in the Louvre, child abduction in Springfield, Massachusetts, military command in Winterthur, and theft in Ghent — and five have never been traced.
- None of the sitters is named. They are recorded only by the obsession the doctors assigned them.
- Dr Georget, who had the pictures, died in 1828 aged thirty-three; the portraits scattered with his estate and dropped out of sight for thirty-five years.
- They resurfaced in 1863, reportedly in an attic in Baden-Baden, and the critic Louis Viardot wrote them up in the Gazette des Beaux-Arts in January 1864 — the article that put them back into art history.
- The Lyon painting also goes by the far less clinical nickname the Hyena of the Salpêtrière.
- Géricault seems to have given the series to Georget rather than sold it, probably at the beginning of 1823, about a year before his own death.
