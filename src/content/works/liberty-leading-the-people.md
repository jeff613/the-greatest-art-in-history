## The Story

A woman strides forward over a heap of rubble and dead bodies, the tricolour flag of France streaming from her raised right hand, a bayoneted musket in her left. Her dress has slipped from her shoulders, baring her breast; a Phrygian cap — the ancient badge of the freed slave — sits on her head. She is not a real woman but Liberty herself, made flesh, and around her surges a ragged army of Parisians: a top-hatted bourgeois with a hunting gun, a factory worker with a sabre, a wide-eyed boy brandishing two pistols. Behind them the towers of Notre-Dame rise through the smoke.

Delacroix painted it in the autumn of 1830, in the white heat of very recent history. That July, over three violent days Parisians remembered as les Trois Glorieuses — the Three Glorious Days — the people of the city had risen against the reactionary King Charles X, thrown up barricades in the streets, and toppled him from the throne. Delacroix, thirty-two, watched the upheaval more than he fought in it, but he was fired by it. "I have undertaken a modern subject, a barricade," he wrote to his brother, adding that if he had not fought for his country, at least he would paint for it.

What he produced was revolutionary in every sense. He fused raw, contemporary street-fighting — real workers, real corpses, a real Paris — with the timeless allegory of Liberty as a goddess, something no one had dared combine so boldly. The bare breast and the coarse, earthy realism of the figures shocked polite viewers as much as the politics did.

The new regime bought the painting in 1831, then lost its nerve. A picture glorifying insurrection was dangerous to a monarchy that had itself come to power on the barricade, and for years Liberty was kept out of public view, returned to Delacroix, hidden away, deemed too inflammatory to hang. Only decades later did it take its permanent place in the Louvre, where it became exactly what the government had feared: the enduring emblem of the French Republic, and of revolution everywhere.

## The World Behind It

The revolution of 1830 was the aftershock of a much larger one. Since the great Revolution of 1789 and the fall of Napoleon, France had been ruled again by the restored Bourbon kings, who tried to turn the clock back toward absolute monarchy and the power of the old aristocracy. When Charles X issued decrees muzzling the press and gutting the vote in July 1830, Paris exploded. In three days of street fighting the king was gone — replaced not by a republic but by the "Citizen King" Louis-Philippe, a compromise that satisfied few of the workers who had actually died on the barricades.

That unfinished quality is written into the painting's fate. It celebrated a popular uprising for a government that wanted the uprising forgotten as quickly as possible. Delacroix had caught the raw energy of the crowd — and the crowd, the new regime knew, was a force that could just as easily turn on it. The image was too true, and too combustible, to be safely displayed.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1830 Delacroix was already notorious. He had stunned and scandalized the Paris Salon through the 1820s with The Massacre at Chios and the lurid Death of Sardanapalus, and he was recognized — not always admiringly — as the standard-bearer of the new Romantic painting, all color, passion, and movement against the cool, correct classicism of his rival Ingres. He was cultured, ambitious, and hungry for a subject worthy of his powers.

The July Revolution handed him one. Liberty Leading the People let him pour his Romantic fire into a cause his whole generation felt in its blood. It is telling that he inserted himself, by some accounts, into the picture — the top-hatted man with the gun has often been read as a self-portrait, the artist joining the fight on canvas even as he stood back from it in life. The painting sealed his reputation as the great history painter of his age, the man who could make current events feel like myth.

## Interesting Facts

- Liberty is an allegory, not a portrait, but Delacroix painted her with such earthy realism — bare-breasted, dirty-footed, with visible underarm hair — that genteel critics were scandalized.
- The government bought the painting in 1831 and then kept it hidden for years, judging it too likely to incite further revolution to be shown in public.
- The pistol-waving boy on the right is often said to have inspired the character Gavroche in Victor Hugo's Les Misérables.
- The top-hatted man holding a gun beside Liberty is frequently identified as Delacroix himself, though this remains debated.
- The image has appeared on French banknotes and stamps, and underwent a major cleaning and restoration completed in 2024.
