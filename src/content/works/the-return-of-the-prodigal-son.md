## The Story

A ragged man kneels and buries his shaved head in his father's belly. His clothes are worn to threads, one foot bare, the other in a split-open shoe; the sole of the naked foot is turned toward us, cracked and dirty from the road. He has come home with nothing. And the old man simply lays both hands on his back and bends over him — not a word of reproach, not a question, only the weight of two hands and the whole of his stooped body folding down in welcome.

It is the parable from the Gospel of Luke: the younger son who demanded his inheritance, squandered it in a far country, and crawled back expecting to be treated as a servant, only to be met at the gate by a father who runs to embrace him. Rembrandt strips the story to its last essential gesture. The prodigal is faceless, generic, everyman; the drama lives entirely in the father's hands and in the ring of silent witnesses who watch from the shadows, chief among them a tall, stern figure on the right — the dutiful elder brother, arms folded, unable to understand why such a homecoming deserves joy.

Look closely at the father's two hands and they are not the same. One is broad and muscular, a working man's hand; the other is slimmer, softer, almost a woman's. Whether Rembrandt intended it or not, generations have read into that difference a father who is also a mother, a single embrace holding every kind of love at once. The whole picture glows from within — the son's rags and the old man's red cloak burning out of an enveloping brown darkness — as though forgiveness itself were the only light in the room.

Rembrandt returned to this parable again and again across his life, in drawings and etchings and an early, swaggering painting of himself as the prodigal carousing in a tavern with a girl on his knee. This is the other end of that life: no swagger left, only the coming home. It is widely believed to be among the very last things he painted, perhaps unfinished on the day he died — his final word, and it is a word of mercy.

## The World Behind It

By the 1660s Amsterdam had cooled toward its greatest painter. The city was still Europe's richest, but taste had swung to bright, smooth, elegant pictures, and Rembrandt's dark, thickly worked canvases looked to many buyers like the work of a man out of step with the times. The blockbuster commissions dried up; he worked for a narrow circle and for himself.

Religious painting of this depth was also, in a sense, out of season. The Dutch Republic was staunchly Calvinist, its whitewashed churches bare of altarpieces, and there was no market of grand ecclesiastical patrons such as fed painters in Catholic Rome. A vast, inward meditation on divine forgiveness like this one was not a commission to satisfy a client; it was something closer to a private reckoning, made by an old man for reasons of his own. The Republic's flood of pictures ran mostly to cheerful genre scenes and crisp portraits — which makes this enormous, hushed act of mercy all the stranger, and all the more personal.

## The Artist at This Moment

No painter ever had more reason to understand the prodigal's homecoming than the Rembrandt of these final years. Bankruptcy had stripped him of his house and treasures in 1656. Hendrickje Stoffels, the companion who had stood by him through the ruin, died in 1663. Then in 1668 his only surviving child, Titus — married just months before — died too, and Rembrandt buried the last of the people he loved. He would follow them in 1669.

Into that wreckage he painted a father taking back a lost son without conditions. It is impossible not to feel the old artist on both sides of the embrace at once: the ruined prodigal who has lost everything and come home in rags, and the aged father whose only remaining power is to forgive. Whether he finished it before he died is uncertain; parts look complete, others barely brushed. Either way it stands as the summit of his late style and, many feel, the most moving thing he ever made — the work of a man who had been emptied out by grief and had, astonishingly, nothing left in him but tenderness.

## Interesting Facts

- The painting is widely believed to be one of Rembrandt's last works, possibly still unfinished when he died in 1669.
- The father's two hands are subtly different — one strong and masculine, the other slender and soft — and are often read as combining a father's and a mother's love.
- The prodigal's near foot is bare and its cracked sole turned toward the viewer, a small, brutal detail of how far he has fallen.
- Rembrandt painted the same parable as a young man, casting himself as the prodigal squandering his fortune in a tavern — the mirror image of this late homecoming.
- Catherine the Great of Russia acquired the picture around 1766, which is how it came to hang in the Hermitage in St Petersburg.
- Rembrandt made it after losing his home to bankruptcy and burying both his companion Hendrickje and his son Titus.
