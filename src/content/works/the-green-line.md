## The Story

Matisse looked at his wife's face and painted a bar of pure green straight down the middle of it, from hairline to chin. On one side of that stripe her skin is warm — ochre, rose, terracotta; on the other it is cool green and grey; and the whole is set against a background split into slabs of pink, green, and orange. There is no soft shading anywhere, none of the gentle modelling that portraiture had used for centuries to turn a flat face into a rounded head. Instead Matisse lets colour alone do the work: the green line stands in for the shadow that would ordinarily fall along the bridge of the nose, dividing light from dark not by tone but by hue. It is one of the boldest single decisions in the history of portraiture, and Amélie Matisse — patient, devoted, and here almost mask-like — is its subject.

Painted in 1905, the year the Fauves scandalised Paris, the portrait pushes their logic to an extreme. If *Woman with a Hat* had shocked viewers with arbitrary colour, this went further, treating a beloved face as a field for chromatic experiment. Contemporaries were baffled and often hostile; a friend of the painting's early owners reportedly called it "a demented caricature of a portrait," and in 1910 the American critic Gelett Burgess wrote that Matisse seemed to be punishing his wife, compelling the viewer to see something strange and terrible in her. Matisse, who adored Amélie, meant no such cruelty. He was after a deeper truth than likeness — the sensation of a face, built from colour as music is built from sound.

The picture's travels mirror the map of early modern collecting. It was first owned by Michael and Sarah Stein, the American expatriates who, with Michael's siblings Leo and Gertrude, were buying the most advanced art in Paris. It passed to the Danish collector Christian Tetzen-Lund, and after his death the Statens Museum for Kunst in Copenhagen bought it from his estate in 1936, where it remains a cornerstone of the museum's modern holdings.

## The World Behind It

The green line belongs to the same revolutionary moment as *Woman with a Hat* and *The Joy of Life*: the eighteen months around 1905 when Matisse and his fellow Fauves tore colour loose from its old job of describing the world. For four hundred years, European painters had built faces and bodies out of graduated light and shade, a technique so ingrained it seemed like nature itself. The Fauves proposed something heretical — that a painter could set down colours because of how they felt and how they answered one another, not because the eye "really" saw them there. A face could be green if green was what the picture needed. Paris was the only city where such an idea could be launched and argued over in public, in its rival salons, its dealers' galleries, and the apartments of adventurous collectors like the Steins. To conservative viewers it looked like the collapse of skill; to the young painters watching, including Picasso, it was a door swinging open.

## The Artist at This Moment

By late 1905 Matisse was riding the strange wave of his Salon d'Automne notoriety — famous, mocked, and suddenly collectible. The Stein family had begun buying his work, easing years of hardship, and he responded with a burst of the most daring painting of his life. Amélie was his constant model and his fiercest supporter; she had run a hat shop to help fund his career and endured the ridicule aimed at his pictures of her without complaint. To turn her familiar face into a laboratory of colour was an act of confidence, even intimacy: he could take the person he knew best and remake her entirely in paint. He was in his mid-thirties, no longer a struggling unknown but the acknowledged leader of the avant-garde, and he knew every canvas was now watched — not least by the young Picasso, who was beginning to see in Matisse the rival he had to overthrow.

## Interesting Facts

- The green stripe down Amélie's face replaces conventional shadow: Matisse divides the face into a warm side and a cool side using colour alone, not light and dark.
- The sitter is the artist's wife, Amélie, his most frequent early model and the subject of *Woman with a Hat* that same year.
- In 1910 the American critic Gelett Burgess wrote that the portrait looked like Matisse "punishing" his wife, forcing viewers to see something terrible in her.
- The painting was first owned by Michael and Sarah Stein, part of the American family whose Paris apartments were a headquarters of early modern art.
- It later belonged to the Danish collector Christian Tetzen-Lund, who also once owned Matisse's *The Joy of Life*.
- The Statens Museum for Kunst in Copenhagen bought the portrait from Tetzen-Lund's estate in 1936.
