## The Story

The horse is doing two things at once. It is rearing and wheeling away, hindquarters swinging out of the frame, one eye rolling white. The officer on its back has twisted almost fully round in the saddle to look behind him, sabre curving back and down, and everything under them is smoke, churned ground and the litter of a battlefield. Nothing in the picture is settled. Every part of it is mid-movement, which is precisely what nobody was painting in 1812.

Géricault was twenty-one and had exhibited nothing. He had spent two years in the studio of Pierre-Narcisse Guérin, a strict classicist, and had been an indifferent pupil; what he actually cared about was Rubens in the Louvre and horses anywhere he could find them. For the Salon that opened in November 1812 he produced this — eleven feet of it — in about five weeks.

He entered it modestly, not as a battle piece but as a portrait: Equestrian portrait of Monsieur D…, Lieutenant of the Emperor's Guards. The sitter is usually taken to be an officer named Dieudonné whom Géricault knew, though the picture is barely a likeness — the face is small, shadowed and turned away, and the man is up there mainly to be carried. Dominique Vivant Denon, Napoleon's director of museums, awarded the unknown painter a gold medal.

The timing was extraordinary and terrible. While the canvas hung in the Louvre, the Grande Armée was dying in the snow on the retreat from Moscow. Géricault had painted the Empire at its most magnificent — the leopard-skin saddlecloth, the gold braid, the animal energy of the horse — in the exact months the Empire stopped being magnificent.

He worked out the answer himself. Two years later, with Napoleon on Elba and the Bourbons restored, he sent The Wounded Cuirassier to the Salon of 1814, where this painting hung again beside it: nearly the same size, a soldier of the same army, walking his horse down a slope away from the fire. The critics savaged the second picture. Géricault, humiliated, gave up painting for a while and joined the army instead.

## The World Behind It

The chasseurs à cheval of the Imperial Guard were Napoleon's own escort — the regiment whose undress coat he habitually wore himself. To paint one of their officers in 1812 was to paint the Empire's self-image at its highest pitch.

The Salon was where that self-image got manufactured. Under Vivant Denon, the archaeologist-courtier who ran Napoleon's museums, French painting had been organised around the regime, and the model was Jacques-Louis David: calm, statuesque, frontal, the Napoleon Crossing the Alps who points the way forward on a horse that rears like a monument. Géricault keeps the propaganda subject and throws out the pattern. His officer has his back half-turned and no direction at all; the twisting rider on a rearing horse comes not from David but from Rubens, whose Saint George Géricault had studied in the Louvre.

Visitors saw it through the winter of 1812 and 1813, while the dispatches came in from Russia. Half a million men had marched east in June. What came back through the snow that December was a remnant. Within eighteen months there were foreign troops in Paris and the Emperor was gone.

## The Artist at This Moment

Géricault had money, and money is the fact that shapes his entire career. His father was a Rouen lawyer with an interest in the family tobacco business, and the son never had to take a commission, please a patron, or finish anything he had lost interest in.

What he had instead was horses, to a degree that worried his family. At sixteen he went into the studio of Carle Vernet, a painter of races and hunts whose son Horace became his lifelong friend, and from then on he learned as much in stables as in studios. He rode hard. It eventually killed him.

In 1812 he was nobody: no exhibitions, no reputation, living under his father's roof and, at twenty-one, still a student in a studio he was not enjoying. The gold medal made him overnight the most promising young painter in France. What is striking is what he did with that reputation — almost nothing for six years, until he shut himself in a room with a shipwreck.

## Interesting Facts

- It was the first painting Géricault ever exhibited, and it won a gold medal at the Salon of 1812 from Vivant Denon, Napoleon's museums director. He was twenty-one.
- By his own account the whole eleven-foot canvas took him roughly five weeks.
- It was entered not as a battle scene but as a portrait, under the title Equestrian portrait of Monsieur D…, Lieutenant of the Emperor's Guards; a related study is inscribed with the name Dieudonné.
- The pose — a rider twisting backwards on a rearing horse — derives from Rubens's early Saint George, which Géricault had copied in the Louvre.
- The picture hung again at the Salon of 1814, this time next to its deliberate opposite, The Wounded Cuirassier: same army, same scale, retreating instead of charging. The critics loathed the new one and Géricault enlisted in disgust.
- Both canvases are in the Louvre today — the Empire arriving and the Empire leaving, painted two years apart by the same man.
