## The Story

Three nude women stand in a loose ring, arms draped across one another's shoulders, a wisp of gauze looping between them like a shared secret. They are the Graces of ancient myth — companions of Venus, goddesses of charm, beauty, and joy — but there is nothing chilly or classical about them. Rubens paints warm, soft, dimpled flesh, real bodies pressed close in the open air, with a garland of roses hanging overhead and a fountain trickling at the right. It is a hymn to abundance, to friendship, to the pleasures of the body, painted by a man in the contented autumn of his life.

What makes the picture unusual is that Rubens made it for no one but himself. There is no known commission, no preparatory sketch to show a patron, and no record of a buyer — because there wasn't one. The painting simply stayed in his house until he died in 1640, hanging in his private rooms among the works he loved best. It was among the paintings listed in his estate, and only then did it enter the wider world.

And the Grace on the left, blonde and luminous, is his wife. In 1630 the fifty-three-year-old widower had married Helena Fourment, a girl of sixteen whose full, fair-skinned figure became his ideal of beauty in every late work. A long tradition holds that his beloved first wife, Isabella Brant, dead of plague in 1626, looks out from the group as well — so that the two women he loved most stand joined forever in a single embrace. Whether or not both faces are truly theirs, the painting glows with a personal warmth that no commission could have bought. This was Rubens painting for pleasure, at home, for the joy of it.

## The World Behind It

By the 1630s Rubens had spent a decade at the center of European affairs, painting for the courts of France, Spain, and England while carrying secret diplomatic messages between them. He was rich, ennobled, and knighted, with a grand house in Antwerp and, from 1635, a country estate where he could retreat. In these last years he increasingly painted for himself — landscapes of his own fields, portraits of his young wife and children, and mythologies like this one, made not to a patron's brief but to his own delight.

The subject reached back to the antiquity he had studied in Italy, where sculpted and painted Three Graces survived from the ancient world. But Rubens rejected the cool marble ideal. His Graces are Flemish women of flesh and blood, generous and alive, and their frank sensuality reflects both his private happiness and a Catholic culture that, unlike its stern Protestant neighbors to the north, still embraced beauty and the body in its art.

## The Artist at This Moment

Rubens painted the Three Graces near the end of his life, a man who had achieved everything and now wanted mostly to be left alone with his family. His marriage to Helena Fourment had opened a second springtime; their children were being born, and his brush returned again and again to her likeness with unmistakable tenderness. Gout was beginning to twist his hands, and he knew his working years were numbered, but the pictures of this final decade are among the freest and happiest he ever made.

Keeping the Three Graces for himself was of a piece with that mood. He no longer needed to sell everything he touched; he could afford to hoard a masterpiece purely because it pleased him. It hung in his home as a private celebration of love and beauty until his death — and only then, at his estate sale, did it pass into the hands that would carry it to a king.

## Interesting Facts

- Rubens never sold the painting: it stayed in his own house until he died in 1640, one of the most personal works he ever made.
- The blonde Grace on the left is widely identified as his young second wife, Helena Fourment, whom he married when she was sixteen and he fifty-three.
- Tradition holds that his first wife, Isabella Brant, who died of plague in 1626, also appears among the three — uniting both wives in one embrace.
- After Rubens's death, King Philip IV of Spain — a passionate collector of his work — bought the picture from the estate sale for the royal collection.
- The nudity was too frank for some later Spanish tastes; such paintings were kept in a private "reserved room" and nearly destroyed under a proposed royal purge of indecent art.
- It hangs today in the Prado in Madrid, whose unrivalled collection of Rubens was built largely on the appetite of Philip IV.
