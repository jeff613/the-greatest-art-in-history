## The Story

A young woman stands behind a marble bar, facing us, her expression somewhere between boredom and quiet melancholy. In front of her the counter is laid with a small still life of Parisian pleasure — bottles of champagne and English beer, a bowl of oranges, two roses drooping in a glass. Behind her stretches a vast mirror, and in it the whole glittering theater of the Folies-Bergère swims into view: chandeliers, the crowd on the balconies, and, in the top left corner, the green-slippered legs of a trapeze artist swinging above it all. It is one of the most beguiling pictures of modern nightlife ever painted — and it does not add up.

Look at the reflection. On the right, the mirror shows the barmaid from behind, leaning forward to attend to a top-hatted customer who looms close to her face. But turn to the woman herself, standing frontally before us, and there is no such man — only us, the viewer, occupying the exact spot he must be standing in. Her reflected back is pushed far to the right, at an angle no ordinary mirror could produce, and the man she is serving in the glass is nowhere in the "real" space of the painting. The geometry is, by the strict rules of perspective, impossible.

Critics pounced on it as soon as the picture appeared at the Salon of 1882, accusing Manet of not knowing how to draw. But scholars have argued ever since that the distortion is deliberate and profound. By placing us where the reflected customer stands, Manet quietly casts the viewer as that man — the buyer leaning in, and perhaps not only for a drink. The barmaid facing us is guarded and remote; her reflected self bends forward, attentive, almost willing. We are made to feel the gap between what such a woman must perform and what she actually feels, caught in a shimmer of glass that refuses to resolve. The "mistake" is the meaning.

## The World Behind It

The Folies-Bergère was one of the great pleasure palaces of the modern city, a dazzling music hall where Parisians came for acrobats, singers, gaslight, and the frank buzz of a crowd on display to itself. Its barmaids sold drinks — and, it was widely understood, were often available for other arrangements too, part of the same discreet commerce that Manet had exposed twenty years earlier in Olympia. The counter of oranges and champagne is a spread of temptations, and the woman behind it is one of them, whether she wishes to be or not.

This was the Paris of electric novelty and packed spectacle, rebuilt in the previous decades into a city of wide boulevards and blazing nightlife, and Manet had spent his whole career painting exactly this world of cafés, theaters, and half-lit encounters. The Folies gave him his final subject: modern loneliness set inside modern glamour, a single tired face held at the center of a whirling, reflected mob.

## The Artist at This Moment

Manet painted this while he was dying. For years a disease of the nervous system — then called locomotor ataxia, and linked to syphilis — had been attacking his legs, leaving him in pain and increasingly unable to walk or stand for long. He could no longer haul himself to the Folies-Bergère night after night, so he did the next best thing: he had a real barmaid from the hall, a woman named Suzon, come to his studio, where he built a mock bar with bottles and fruit and posed her behind it. The grand, teeming mirror-world of the painting was assembled from an invalid's quiet room.

The recognition he had chased all his life had finally arrived. In 1881 the Salon at last awarded him a medal, and he was named to the Légion d'honneur — official honors, granted just as his body failed. He submitted this bar to the Salon of 1882, his last. Within a year the disease forced the amputation of his gangrenous left leg, and in April 1883 he died, aged fifty-one, days after the operation. This luminous, unsolvable painting of a Paris night is the parting work of a man who could barely stand.

## Interesting Facts

- The barmaid was posed by a real Folies-Bergère employee named Suzon, who came to Manet's studio, where he reconstructed the bar with actual bottles and fruit.
- In the top-left corner, easy to miss, hang the green-shod feet of a trapeze artist mid-performance above the crowd.
- The reflection is geometrically impossible: the barmaid's mirrored back and the customer she serves cannot coexist with her frontal pose, and the debate over whether it is an error or a deliberate device began at the picture's first showing.
- By putting the viewer where the reflected top-hatted client stands, Manet casts us as the customer leaning in toward her.
- The bottles on the bar are painted with real specificity — including a distinctive red-triangle label of Bass Pale Ale, an English import.
- It was Manet's last major painting; he exhibited it at the Salon of 1882 and died the following year.
