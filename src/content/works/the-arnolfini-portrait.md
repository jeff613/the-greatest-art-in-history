## The Story

A richly dressed couple stand in a Bruges bedroom. He raises a hand as if in oath; she rests hers on her swelling gown. A dog sits between them, a single candle burns in the chandelier, and on the back wall a convex mirror catches the whole room in miniature. Above that mirror, in a looping, showy script, van Eyck wrote something no painter of his time thought to write: *Johannes de eyck fuit hic 1434* — "Jan van Eyck was here, 1434." Not merely a signature but a statement, planted like the flourish of a legal witness on a document.

For generations the picture was read as a wedding, the man's raised hand and the woman's joined fingers taken as vows exchanged before the artist himself. The couple were long identified as the Italian merchant Giovanni Arnolfini and his bride. But the neat story has quietly fallen apart. A document surfaced in the 1990s proving that the Arnolfini everyone had settled on did not marry until 1447 — six years after van Eyck was dead. The sitters are now thought to be a different Giovanni Arnolfini and his wife Costanza Trenta. And Costanza, the records suggest, died around 1433, perhaps in childbirth — a year *before* the date on the wall.

That single fact turns the room eerie. If the woman had already died, this is not a marriage but a memorial: a husband keeping his lost wife beside him in paint. Look again with that in mind and the details rearrange themselves. On the man's side of the chandelier the candle still burns; above the woman it has guttered out. The mirror's frame is ringed with scenes of Christ's Passion, the living husband beside the moments of Christ's life, the wife beside his death. Nothing here is careless. Van Eyck loaded a quiet bedroom with meanings we are still arguing over six hundred years later — and left his own name hanging over all of it, insisting that he, at least, was really there.

## The World Behind It

Bruges in the 1430s was one of the great money-cities of Europe, a Flemish port thick with foreign merchants who ran the wool, silk and banking trades that made the Low Countries rich. Among them were the Arnolfini, a family from Lucca in Tuscany who had lived and traded in Bruges for years. Men like them were exactly the new kind of client the Northern painters served: not popes or princes but wealthy laymen who wanted their own faces, their own rooms, their own dogs recorded with the reverence once reserved for saints.

The picture is a catalogue of that wealth. The oranges on the sill were costly imports. The woman's gown uses yards of expensive cloth, its green a deliberate display of dye. The brass chandelier and the convex mirror — larger than any glass then made — announce a household with money to burn. In a trading city, a portrait like this was both a keepsake and a quiet advertisement of standing.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1434 van Eyck was settled in Bruges, at the height of his powers and his prestige. He was court painter and trusted servant to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, well paid, well travelled, and free to take private commissions from the city's rich foreigners on the side. He had just completed the colossal Ghent Altarpiece two years earlier; his reputation was enormous.

He was also, by the evidence of this panel, a man delighting in his own skill. The looping wall inscription, the impossible detail of the mirror, the two figures reflected in its glass — one of them perhaps the painter himself — read like a virtuoso showing exactly what oil paint could do in his hands. No artist before him had signed a work so boldly, or so playfully. Van Eyck was not just recording a couple; he was signing his presence into history.

## Interesting Facts

- The wall inscription — *"Jan van Eyck was here, 1434"* — is one of the most famous signatures in art, written like a witness's testimony rather than a mere autograph.
- The old "wedding portrait" reading collapsed when a document proved the presumed groom didn't marry until 1447, six years after van Eyck had died.
- The sitters are now thought to be Giovanni di Nicolao Arnolfini and Costanza Trenta — and Costanza appears to have died around 1433, a year before the painting's date, suggesting a memorial to a dead wife.
- The candle in the chandelier burns only on the husband's side; above the woman it has gone out — one of several clues cited for the memorial theory.
- The convex mirror reflects the whole room and two figures entering through the door — possibly van Eyck and a companion, the witnesses his inscription names.
- The little dog, painted hair by hair, is a symbol of fidelity; the woman is not pregnant but gathering the heavy folds of a fashionable gown.
