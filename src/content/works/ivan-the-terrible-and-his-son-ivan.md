## The Story

The staff is on the carpet where it fell. The old man is on the floor with his son in his arms, one hand clamped over the wound in the boy's temple, blood coming through his fingers anyway. His eyes are the whole painting: enormous, blown wide, the eyes of a man watching the consequence arrive about four seconds after the act. The son is already dying and is the calmer of the two — his hand rests on his father's arm, and his expression, if it is anything, is forgiveness.

Repin later listed what pushed him into it, and none of it was history. In March 1881 Alexander II was blown apart by a bomb in a St Petersburg street, and Repin went to watch the conspirators hanged in public that April. He called those nightmare times. Then in Moscow, listening to a new orchestral piece by Rimsky-Korsakov — a vengeance movement from *Antar*, which he remembered simply as "Vengeance" — he felt the sound take possession of him. Then, travelling in Spain in 1883, he went to the bullfights and came away describing blood as a hole that draws you in by force. He got home and started the picture.

He built the two heads out of four living men. The tsar's came from the painter Grigory Myasoyedov and the composer Pavel Blaramberg. The son's came from the landscape painter Vladimir Menk and the writer Vsevolod Garshin, of whom Repin said there was a predestination in his face that struck him — the face of a man irreparably condemned to perish. Three years after the picture was finished, Garshin threw himself down the stairwell of his building and died at thirty-three.

The state's reaction was immediate. Konstantin Pobedonostsev, Ober-Procurator of the Holy Synod and the tsar's old tutor, told Alexander III how repellent he found it, and on 1 April 1885 public display was forbidden — the first painting ever censored in the Russian Empire. The ban lasted three months and was lifted on 11 July after the painter Alexei Bogolyubov interceded.

That was only the beginning of what people would do to it. In January 1913 a young icon painter named Abram Balashov, from an Old Believer family, walked up to the canvas and cut it three times with a knife, ruining both faces, shouting *Enough blood! Down with blood!* He was found insane and committed. Repin helped repair his own painting. The Tretyakov's curator, Georgy Khruslov, went out and threw himself under a train.

## The World Behind It

Russia in the 1880s was a country in reaction. The assassination of Alexander II had ended two decades of cautious reform overnight; his son Alexander III came to the throne convinced that any concession was a step toward the bomb, and handed the moral policing of the empire to Pobedonostsev, who believed a free press and a parliament were among the great lies of the age. Censorship tightened everywhere.

Which made Ivan IV a dangerous man to paint. The killing of the tsarevich in November 1581 was three hundred years old and still not a safe story, because it said the thing no autocracy can afford to have said out loud: that absolute power is held by a person, and a person can lose his temper. Repin had painted an anointed tsar as a murderer with his hands full of his son's blood — in a decade when the throne was insisting it was the only thing standing between Russia and chaos. Officials also understood something the artist may not have intended: the picture had appeared two years after a tsar's violent death, and it looked less like history than like a warning about where violence ends up.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was thirty-nine when he began and at the peak of his powers. The Paris years were behind him, the Volga picture had made him a name a decade earlier, and he was working through the great sequence of the 1880s at a pace he would never match again — the murdered tsarevich in one studio corner, the political exiles of his *narodniki* cycle in another, and the Zaporozhian Cossacks, already three years in and eight from finished, laughing on a canvas nearby.

He was also using the same faces across the whole decade: Vsevolod Garshin, condemned-looking, sat for the dying tsarevich and then went into the exile picture as well. Repin's own marriage was quietly coming apart; he and Vera would separate in 1887.

Pavel Tretyakov bought the canvas early in 1885, weeks before the ban came down, reportedly in the same purchase as *They Did Not Expect Him*. Both are still in his gallery.

## Interesting Facts

- Repin said three things drove him to it: the public hanging of Alexander II's assassins in 1881, a vengeance movement of Rimsky-Korsakov's *Antar*, and the Spanish bullfights he watched in 1883.
- Two men sat for each head. The writer Vsevolod Garshin, one of the models for the dying son, killed himself three years later at thirty-three by throwing himself down a stairwell.
- It was the first painting ever censored in the Russian Empire — banned from public view on 1 April 1885 and released on 11 July.
- In 1913 the icon painter Abram Balashov slashed it three times with a knife, shouting "Enough blood!" The paint layer had to be transferred to a new canvas, and Repin worked on the restoration himself.
- The Tretyakov curator responsible for the room, Georgy Khruslov, killed himself under a train when he heard.
- On 25 May 2018 a visitor named Igor Podporin broke the protective glass with a metal stanchion and tore the canvas in three places. He was sentenced to two and a half years; the painting went back on display in December 2024, sealed in a climate-controlled anti-vandal case.
