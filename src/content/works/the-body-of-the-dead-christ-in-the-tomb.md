## The Story

In August 1867 a Russian novelist on the run from his creditors stopped in Basel to see one picture. Fyodor Dostoevsky stood in front of it, then climbed onto a chair to get his face nearer the glass, and stayed there for a quarter of an hour. His wife Anna recorded the scene in her diary: his expression frightened her, she had seen that look before the onset of his seizures, and she took him by the arm and led him out of the room. What he said about the painting she also wrote down — that a man could lose his faith from looking at it. Two years later he put the sentence into *The Idiot*, in Prince Myshkin's mouth, in front of a copy hanging in Rogozhin's dark house.

The panel is thirty centimetres high and two metres long — the exact proportions of a coffin, seen through its open side. Inside is a man who has been dead long enough for it to show. The chest has fallen in over the ribs. The mouth hangs open, the eye is rolled back and unclosed, the fingers of the right hand have stiffened into a claw, and the flesh at the wound in the side and along the feet has gone green. The hair and one hand touch the front edge of the stone, as though the body has been pushed into a space slightly too small for it. There is no halo, no angel, no mourning Virgin, nothing to soften it. There is no promise, in the picture itself, that this man is going to get up.

A legend says Holbein worked from a corpse fished out of the Rhine. It is repeated everywhere and rests on nothing solid, and the painting hardly needs it — what is certain is that a painter in his early twenties studied real death very carefully and refused to look away.

He was less certain about the date. The inscription at the feet reads MDXXI, 1521, with his initials beside it. Infrared and X-ray examination shows that the last numeral was painted out: the panel originally said 1522, and Holbein backdated his own work while it was still on the easel. Nobody knows why.

## The World Behind It

Basel in 1521 was a small, rich, argumentative city on the Rhine, full of printing presses and the men who fed them. Erasmus was living there, seeing his editions through the press. Luther's writings were being read and reprinted; the city would go over to the Reformation within a decade, and in 1529 crowds would haul the images out of its churches and burn them.

That makes the timing of this panel strange and pointed. What it is for has never been settled — the base of an altarpiece, an independent devotional picture, the decoration of a mock sepulchre used in Holy Week. The likeliest patron was Bonifacius Amerbach, the young jurist and humanist who became Holbein's friend and collector. When the Amerbach family's cabinet of curiosities was inventoried, the picture was listed without ceremony as a painting of a dead man. The city of Basel and its university bought that whole collection in 1661, which made Basel the owner of the first publicly held art collection in Europe — and is why the panel has never left the city.

## The Artist at This Moment

Holbein was about twenty-four and had been in Basel roughly six years. He had arrived as a journeyman from Augsburg with no connections and worked his way up through the printers, designing title pages and woodcut alphabets. In 1519 he had joined the painters' guild, bought his citizenship, and married Elsbeth Binsenstock, a tanner's widow with a young son; their own first child was born that year. Public work had started to arrive — he was painting murals in the council chamber of the town hall, and a house facade that people came across the city to look at.

He was, in other words, an ambitious young craftsman establishing himself, and this is the picture he made. Two years later he painted the portraits of Erasmus that would make his name across Europe and, eventually, get him out of Basel altogether. Nothing else he did in his short career is as bleak as this one.

## Interesting Facts

- The panel is 30.5 cm high and 200 cm long — a slot the size and shape of a coffin, which is part of why the body looks trapped.
- Dostoevsky saw it in Basel in 1867 and climbed onto a chair to look closer; his wife's diary records that she pulled him away fearing an epileptic seizure.
- He gave his reaction to Prince Myshkin in *The Idiot*: a picture like that could make a man lose his faith.
- X-rays and infrared show the date was changed: Holbein painted out the final numeral so that 1522 became 1521. The reason is unknown.
- The story that he painted from a body pulled out of the Rhine is often repeated but has no reliable source behind it.
- The Amerbach inventory described it plainly as a picture of a dead man, not as a Christ.
- Basel and its university bought the Amerbach collection in 1661, creating what is generally counted as the first public art museum in Europe — with this panel in it.
