## The Story

The young woman turning her head as if someone has just entered the room is Cecilia Gallerani, sixteen or seventeen years old and the mistress of Ludovico Sforza, the ruler of Milan. Leonardo painted her around 1489–91, when Cecilia was the duke's favorite — clever, musical, able to hold her own with the scholars at court — and probably pregnant with his child. The white animal she cradles is an ermine, and it is the key that unlocks the whole picture.

Nothing about the ermine is decoration. Ludovico had recently been made a knight of the Order of the Ermine, so the creature stands in for the absent lover. The Greek word for the animal, *galé*, echoes Cecilia's surname, Gallerani, making the beast a pun on her name. And the ermine was a symbol of purity — a small act of flattery for a mistress in a delicate position. Leonardo has folded an entire relationship into one animal: the duke, the woman, and the discretion required of both. Her long fingers spread across its back with startling tenderness and control.

What makes the portrait feel alive, though, is the movement. Cecilia's body faces one way and her face turns another, her gaze caught by something outside the frame, so that we seem to have interrupted a private moment. Leonardo had thrown out the frozen profile that Italian portraits had used for a century and replaced it with a living, twisting presence. The ermine turns with her, alert, its little muscles tense — animal and woman sharing the same startled instant. It is one of the first portraits in Western art that behaves less like an icon than like a snapshot of a mind at work.

## The World Behind It

Milan under Ludovico Sforza was a court obsessed with cleverness, and a portrait like this was meant to be read as much as admired. Educated courtiers delighted in *imprese* — emblems and puns that hid a message in plain sight — and Cecilia's ermine is exactly that kind of insider's riddle, legible to anyone who knew the duke's honors and the lady's name. She herself was no ornament: she wrote poetry, ran a salon, and later corresponded with Isabella d'Este, one of the most formidable women of the age.

The painting's modern history is a thriller in itself. A Polish prince, Adam Jerzy Czartoryski, bought it in Italy in 1798 as a gift for his mother, and it became the treasure of the family collection. During the Second World War the Nazis looted it; for a time it hung in the Kraków residence of Hans Frank, the German governor of occupied Poland, before being recovered by Allied forces in 1945 and returned home.

## The Artist at This Moment

Leonardo painted Cecilia in the thick of his Milanese years, before The Last Supper, when he was Ludovico's all-purpose genius — court painter, engineer, and impresario of the duke's spectacles. Portraits of the ducal circle were part of the job, and this was a commission with a personal edge, since the sitter was the duke's own lover. Leonardo, ever the naturalist, seems to have relished the ermine as much as the woman; his notebooks are full of animals, and he drew this one with the exact attention he gave to muscles and machines.

He was still in his thirties, experimenting hard, with the greatest works of his life still ahead of him. Here he was working out the turning pose, the soft modeling of light on skin, and the psychological charge of a caught glance — everything he would later perfect in the Mona Lisa. In Cecilia Gallerani you can watch him teaching himself, in real time, how to make paint seem to think.

## Interesting Facts

- The ermine is a triple pun: it evokes the Order of the Ermine that Ludovico Sforza had just joined, it echoes Cecilia's surname through the Greek word *galé*, and it symbolizes the purity expected of a nobleman's mistress.
- It is one of only about fifteen surviving paintings generally accepted as Leonardo's, and one of just four portraits of women he is known to have made.
- The words *"LA BELE FERONIERE / LEONARD D'AWINCI"* painted in the top-left corner are a later, mistaken addition — an old misidentification scrawled onto the panel by someone other than Leonardo.
- The Nazis seized the picture during the occupation of Poland, and it spent part of the war hanging in the home of Hans Frank, the notorious governor-general, before being recovered in 1945.
- In 2016 the Polish state bought the entire Czartoryski collection — some tens of thousands of objects, with this painting as its jewel — for 100 million euros, a small fraction of the Leonardo's estimated worth.
