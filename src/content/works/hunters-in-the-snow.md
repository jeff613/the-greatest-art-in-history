## The Story

Three hunters climb a ridge and start down toward their village, their backs to us, dogs slinking along beside them with their heads down. It is one of the most famous winters in the history of art, and the first thing to notice is that the hunt has failed. All these men have to show for a day in the frozen woods is a single scrawny fox slung over one shoulder. The dogs are gaunt. The men are trudging, not striding. Everything about their posture says cold, hunger, and a long day that came to little.

And yet the picture opens out beneath them into pure delight. Past the ragged line of trees the land falls away to a valley of frozen green-black ponds, where tiny figures skate, slide, sweep, and play a game like curling on the ice. A bird — a magpie or a crow — hangs in the still air. Jagged Alpine peaks rise in the distance, mountains Bruegel had seen in Italy and never forgot, transplanted here to a Flemish plain. At the left, outside an inn whose signboard dangles from a single hook, a family has lit a roaring fire to singe a slaughtered pig, the one blaze of warmth in a world of blue-grey ice.

The painting is one of a series Bruegel made in 1565 depicting the turning year — a cycle of large panels, one for roughly every two months, of which five survive. *Hunters in the Snow* is the depths of winter, December into January. What makes it unforgettable is the god's-eye distance from which Bruegel surveys it all. We stand above the hunters on the hill, taking in the whole valley at a glance, and the effect is to fold together hardship and beauty in a single sweep: the failed hunt and the merry skaters, the killing cold and the ordinary human business of getting through it. Nobody had ever painted winter like this, and arguably nobody has since.

## The World Behind It

Bruegel painted this in the grip of what climate historians now call the Little Ice Age, a long cooling that gripped Europe from the sixteenth century onward. The winter of 1564–65, the year of this panel, was ferociously cold — rivers froze, harvests failed, and the frozen landscape Bruegel shows was not a fantasy but recent memory. His snow-bound scenes helped invent a whole genre; the "winter landscape" as a thing worth painting begins essentially here.

The series was commissioned by Nicolaes Jonghelinck, a wealthy Antwerp financier who wanted a set of the seasons for the dining room of his country villa outside the city, a house called 't Goed ter Beke. These were pictures to be lived with, to hang around a room where a rich merchant ate his dinners while the painted year turned on the walls around him. Antwerp in the 1560s was the richest port in Europe, and this cycle is one of its supreme luxuries: the labours of poor country people, rendered for the pleasure of a banker.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1565 Bruegel had left Antwerp and settled in Brussels, married two years earlier to Mayken Coecke, the daughter of his old teacher. He was at the height of his short career, working for serious collectors — Jonghelinck alone owned sixteen of his paintings — and turning out the great panels on which his fame now rests. His first son, the future painter Pieter Brueghel the Younger, was an infant.

He could not have known how little time remained. Within four years he would be dead, probably in his mid-forties, leaving two small children and a body of work small enough to count. The Months cycle belongs to this final burst, when a city intellectual who had crossed the Alps and studied the Italians turned his whole attention to the plainest subjects imaginable — hunters, harvesters, herdsmen — and made of them something monumental.

## Interesting Facts

- The hunters' entire catch is one thin fox. Bruegel even shows the tracks of a hare that got away, quietly underlining what a poor day it has been.
- It is one of a cycle of six panels representing the year, of which only five survive. The others hang mostly in Vienna alongside this one, with one in New York.
- The mountains on the horizon are Alpine, not Flemish — memories of the crossing Bruegel made into Italy around 1552, imported into a Low Countries winter.
- After Jonghelinck's fortunes collapsed, the paintings passed to the city of Antwerp, which in 1594 presented them to the Habsburg governor Archduke Ernst of Austria, carrying them into the imperial collection in Vienna.
- The panel has become visual shorthand for winter itself, reproduced everywhere from Tarkovsky's films to postage stamps, and is often called the most famous snow scene ever painted.
