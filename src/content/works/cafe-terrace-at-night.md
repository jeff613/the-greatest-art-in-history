## The Story

On a warm September evening in 1888, Van Gogh set up his easel on a street in Arles and painted a café terrace glowing under the night sky. A great awning of yellow gaslight spills across the cobbles and the huddle of little tables; above the rooftops the deep blue night is pricked with stars, each one a burst of white and yellow. A few dark figures move down the street, and the warm light and the cool dark press against each other with no black anywhere in the picture. It is alive and quietly magical — an ordinary café made into something close to a vision.

This was the first canvas in which Van Gogh painted a starry sky, a subject that would obsess him and lead, a year later, to The Starry Night. He was thrilled by the challenge of working at night, outdoors, by the light of the gas lamps and the stars themselves. "I enormously enjoy painting on the spot at night," he wrote. To his sister Willemien he described the picture with obvious pride — a night painting, he told her, without any black in it, made with nothing but beautiful blue and violet and green, in which the lit terrace glows a pale sulphur and greenish-yellow. Banishing black from a night scene was, for him, a small revolution.

The café was real — it still stands on the Place du Forum in Arles, kept up today for the visitors who come to stand where he stood. And here is the odd thing about this most reproduced of images: Van Gogh never signed it. It is one of his unsigned canvases, its authorship known not from any mark on the paint but from the letters in which he described it, again and again, to the people he loved. The evidence that it is his is the story itself.

## The World Behind It

Arles was a small town in Provence, in the far south of France, and Van Gogh had come there in February 1888 from grey, exhausting Paris, hungry for strong sun and stronger color. He rented the Yellow House on the Place Lamartine and dreamed of turning it into a "studio of the south," a brotherhood of painters. That autumn of 1888 was the great flood-tide of his career: in a matter of months he produced the Sunflowers, the Yellow House, the night café, the portraits of the Roulin family, and this glowing terrace — some of the most beloved paintings ever made, all in one sustained burst of southern light.

He was fascinated, too, by the newly bright artificial lighting of the modern town. Gas lamps had spread through French cities and were reaching provincial squares, and their yellow glare against the natural blue of the night gave Van Gogh exactly the collision of warm and cool he was chasing. The café terrace, humming with gaslight beneath real constellations, let him set the man-made glow of modern life against the ancient, indifferent stars — the same contrast that would soon carry him, and painting itself, toward The Starry Night.

## The Artist at This Moment

In September 1888 Van Gogh was thirty-five and, for once, happy. Arles had given him everything Paris lacked: sun, cheap rent, and subjects everywhere he turned. He was painting at a furious pace, sometimes finishing a canvas a day, and writing to Theo with plans for the artists' colony he was certain would take root in the Yellow House. Gauguin, after months of coaxing, had finally agreed to come south and join him. The future looked golden — as golden as the gaslit terrace he was painting.

He could not have known how little time was left before it all came apart. Gauguin would arrive in October; the two would paint side by side, then quarrel bitterly; and on 23 December, in the depths of the crisis, Van Gogh would cut off part of his own ear. But the Café Terrace belongs to the weeks of pure hope beforehand, when Arles was still a paradise and the studio of the south still seemed possible. Its confident, joyous light is the light of a man who believed, however briefly, that his life was finally coming right.

## Interesting Facts

- It is the first painting in which Van Gogh depicted a starry night sky — the theme that would culminate a year later in The Starry Night.
- Van Gogh proudly described it to his sister Willemien as a "night painting without any black," built entirely from blues, violets, greens, and glowing yellows.
- Astronomers have matched the arrangement of stars to the real sky over Arles around 16–17 September 1888, allowing the picture to be dated almost to the hour.
- The painting is unsigned; it is known to be his only through the several letters in which he described it — to Theo, to Willemien, and to a fellow painter.
- The café still exists on the Place du Forum in Arles, repainted yellow and renamed the Café Van Gogh for the tourists who now flock to it.
- He painted it outdoors at night, working directly under the gas lamps and stars rather than from memory in daylight.
