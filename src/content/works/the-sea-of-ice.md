## The Story

In January 1821 the Elbe froze hard and then broke up, and Friedrich went down to the riverbank at Dresden with oil paints to watch what the floes did — how they cracked, rode up over each other, and stacked. Two or three years later he used those studies to build a place he had never been within two thousand miles of.

*The Sea of Ice* is a heap. Green-blue slabs have been driven up into a jagged pyramid in the middle of the canvas, tilted and stacked like a cairn or a collapsed tomb, on a frozen sea that runs back to a bar of pale sky. It takes a moment to find the ship. Off to the right, half-swallowed, there is a piece of a stern, a snapped mast, a scrap of sail, some rope. The ice did not sink her; it took her apart and closed over the pieces. There is no crew, no body, no bird, nothing alive anywhere in the picture.

He had painted the subject once before. In 1822 the Dresden collector Johann Gottlob von Quandt commissioned him to produce the northern half of a pair, the counterpart to a warm southern landscape by another painter. Friedrich delivered a polar wreck with the ship's name legible on her: *Hoffnung* — Hope. That painting was auctioned in 1868 and has not been seen since.

This second, bleaker version went on show in Prague in 1824 under a title that reads like a museum label written by a lawyer: an ideal scene of an Arctic sea, with a wrecked ship among heaped masses of ice. It travelled to Dresden, Berlin and Hamburg. Four exhibitions in three years, and no buyer. It was still unsold when he died in 1840, and it stayed in the hands of friends and their descendants for two more generations.

Its rescue was almost accidental. In 1905 Alfred Lichtwark, director of the Hamburger Kunsthalle, bought it for three thousand marks from the widow of Johann Siegwald Dahl, the son of Friedrich's old friend and housemate. The following year Lichtwark helped mount the Berlin exhibition that put Friedrich back into art history. The picture nobody wanted became one of the most famous images of the nineteenth century.

## The World Behind It

Europe had gone Arctic-mad. In 1819 the British Admiralty sent Lieutenant William Edward Parry west in search of the Northwest Passage; he pushed further into the ice than anyone before him, then had his two ships frozen in for the winter at Melville Island, where the crews spent months in the dark waiting for a thaw. Parry's published journal was a sensation, and Friedrich very likely read the accounts, though no document proves it.

Behind the fashion sat something colder. Under Metternich's settlement the hopes of 1813 had been shut down: censors, informers, dissolved student societies, a Germany of princes again. Friedrich, who had put patriotic hopes into pictures for years, painted a ship being dismantled by something enormous, blind and patient. Given that his first attempt at the subject had a vessel called Hope written on the hull, the reading has never seemed far-fetched. He himself, as usual, explained nothing.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was fifty. In 1824 the Dresden Academy made him an associate professor — a title, a little more money, and not the landscape chair he had wanted, which the academy never gave him. Johan Christian Dahl, nine years younger, Norwegian, and by then living in the same house on the Elbe, had that ground. They stayed friends anyway; Dahl was one of the few people who could get into the studio.

His health had begun to slip, with recurring illnesses that sent him to spas and off the easel for months. Taste was slipping too. The generation coming up wanted the fresh outdoor naturalism Dahl represented, not silent allegories built from memory in a bare room, and Friedrich's kind of picture was beginning to look like a mood that had passed. He worked more in sepia and watercolour as the years went on, sold less, and grew more suspicious of the world.

He had sixteen years left, and would spend most of them disappearing.

## Interesting Facts

- The ice is real: Friedrich made oil studies of floes on the frozen Elbe at Dresden in the winter of 1820–21 and built the Arctic out of them.
- He painted the subject once before, in 1822, on commission — that version showed a ship named *Hoffnung*, Hope, and was sold at auction in 1868 and lost.
- The wreck here is anonymous. All that survives above the ice is a section of stern, a broken mast, a piece of sail and some rope.
- Shown four times between 1824 and 1826, it never found a buyer and was still unsold at Friedrich's death.
- Alfred Lichtwark bought it for the Hamburger Kunsthalle in 1905 for three thousand marks, a year before the Berlin exhibition that made Friedrich famous again.
- The popular English title *The Wreck of Hope* is borrowed: in 1965 the historian Wolfgang Stechow showed that this painting had long been confused with the lost 1822 one, and that the name Hope belongs to the other ship.
- Friedrich never saw polar ice. The furthest north he ever got was the Baltic coast where he was born.
