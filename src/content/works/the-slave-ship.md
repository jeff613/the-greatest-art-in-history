## The Story

A ship flees across a heaving, blood-red sea under a sky that seems to be on fire. In the foreground the water is full of horror: shackled black limbs thrust up from the waves, chains and manacles still on them, while fish and gulls swarm to feed. The slave ship itself is already small and distant, its masts leaning as it runs before an oncoming typhoon, leaving its human cargo to drown behind it. Turner hands us the atrocity not as reportage but as a kind of cosmic judgement, written across sea and sun.

There is a real event behind the picture. In 1781 the British slave ship Zong, having lost its way and run low on drinking water, threw around a hundred and thirty enslaved Africans overboard alive. The reason was money: the ship's insurance would pay for "cargo" lost at sea, but not for captives who died of thirst or disease aboard. The massacre became notorious only through the lawsuit that followed — an insurance dispute, argued as if over jettisoned goods, in which no one was ever charged with murder. Half a century later the story still burned in the abolitionist cause, and Turner, exhibiting the painting in 1840 as Britain hosted a great anti-slavery convention, made it his subject.

He showed it at the Royal Academy under the long title "Slavers Throwing Overboard the Dead and Dying — Typhoon Coming On," pinned to a verse from his own unfinished poem, *The Fallacies of Hope*. Critics were divided; some found the churning colour incomprehensible, others the subject too ghastly to look at. But it found one passionate champion. The young critic John Ruskin was given the painting by his father in 1844 and wrote that if Turner's immortality had to rest on a single work, this would be his choice.

Ruskin kept it for nearly thirty years — and then let it go. The subject, he admitted, was finally too painful to live with, and in 1872 he sold it. After passing through a New York collection it was bought by a Bostonian, and in 1899 it entered the Museum of Fine Arts, where it hangs today.

## The World Behind It

By 1840 Britain had abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery itself across most of its empire in 1833, and a confident, righteous abolitionism had become part of the national self-image. That June, London hosted the first World Anti-Slavery Convention, and reformers were pressing other nations to follow. Turner, who read widely and followed politics, is thought to have been reading Thomas Clarkson's history of abolition — reissued in 1839, with its account of the Zong — when the picture took shape.

He timed it to that moment, but refused to let his countrymen feel comfortable. The slave trade had been a British industry as much as a British target, and the wealth of ports like Bristol and Liverpool had been built on it. Turner's blazing sea is beautiful and appalling at once, and the beauty is the accusation: nature itself, in his vision of a punishing sunset and a gathering storm, seems to recoil from the crime. It was among the first times a great painter had turned the full force of Romantic landscape onto a modern political atrocity.

## The Artist at This Moment

Turner was sixty-five in 1840, long famous and long rich, but drifting further from public taste with every year. His paintings were dissolving into storms of light and colour that baffled reviewers, and The Slave Ship — where the ship and its victims almost vanish into the flaming sea — was among the most extreme things he had yet shown. He no longer painted to be liked.

He had always been drawn to the sea and to catastrophe, and to the idea, threaded through his private poem *The Fallacies of Hope*, that human ambition ends in ruin. The Slave Ship gave that pessimism a moral edge sharper than usual. It is not a calm elegy like his Fighting Temeraire of the year before but a howl. Ruskin, who would spend years championing Turner as the supreme painter of nature, saw in it the summit of the old man's power — even if, in the end, he could not bear to keep so terrible a picture on his own wall.

## Interesting Facts

- The painting refers to the 1781 Zong massacre, when the crew of a British slave ship threw around 130 enslaved Africans overboard to claim insurance money for "cargo" lost at sea.
- Turner exhibited it at the Royal Academy in 1840, the year London hosted the first World Anti-Slavery Convention, and attached lines from his own unfinished poem, *The Fallacies of Hope*.
- The critic John Ruskin owned it for nearly thirty years and called it the one work on which he would rest Turner's immortality — then sold it in 1872, having found its subject too harrowing to keep.
- After passing through a New York collection, it was bought by a Bostonian and entered the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, in 1899, where it remains.
- In the foreground, chained hands and legs rise from the water among feeding fish and birds — a deliberately gruesome detail that many contemporary viewers found unbearable.
- Its full exhibited title was "Slavers Throwing Overboard the Dead and Dying — Typhoon Coming On"; the short name "The Slave Ship" came later.
