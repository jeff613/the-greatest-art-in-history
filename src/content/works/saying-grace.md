## The Story

A woman in a white cap leans over a little round table, one hand steadying a plate, the other lifting the lid off a bowl. Two small children are waiting. The older one, seated in a high-backed chair, is not looking at the food; she is looking sideways at the younger, in a pink cap and a white pinafore, who has got his hands half-joined and is somewhere in the middle of the blessing and clearly losing his place. The mother has stopped mid-serve. Everything in the room — the soup, the spoons, the whole evening — is on hold until the child finds the end of the sentence.

The print made after this painting spelled the joke out in four lines of verse: the sister is laughing on the sly at her little brother, who stammers his prayer, while he, quite unbothered, rushes it — his appetite is his reasoning. Nothing is being taught here. A family is simply waiting for a small boy to finish.

Chardin showed it at the Salon of 1740, and on 27 November of that year he was taken to Versailles by Philibert Orry, the controller-general of finances, and presented to Louis XV. He arrived with two pictures for the king: this one and its companion, *The Diligent Mother*. A cabinetmaker's son who had been filed by the Académie under animals and fruit handed the King of France a painting of a bourgeois supper table, and the king took it.

He knew what he had. Chardin painted the composition again and again — versions of it appeared at the Salons of 1740, 1746 and 1761 — which was less a lack of invention than a working method: he made few pictures, sold them dearly, and repeated the ones that landed.

What survives in the Louvre canvas is how little Chardin does to make you care. There is no moral, no lesson, no gesture pointing at virtue. The room is dim, the tablecloth is white, the light is used up by the time it reaches the corners. He gives an ordinary family the compositional seriousness that his colleagues reserved for Roman generals, and then he lets nothing happen.

## The World Behind It

The France of 1740 was not comfortable. The winter of 1739–40 was one of the bitterest of the century across Europe, grain prices climbed, and parts of the country went hungry into the following year. A picture of a warm room and a full bowl of soup was not a neutral image.

It was also a country whose official art still ran on the Académie's ladder of subjects, with history painting at the top and everyday life far down. Chardin never argued with the ladder. He simply painted so well at the bottom of it that Paris came to look.

The engine of his fame was the print shop. In 1744 François-Bernard Lépicié engraved *Le Bénédicité*, and the plate became one of the most admired prints of the century. Chardin's compositions sold by the thousand as engravings, each with its little verse underneath, and he is generally said to have made more money from the prints than from the paintings. A generation later Greuze would take the same domestic material, add tears and pointing fingers, and become the most talked-about painter in France. Chardin never raised his voice.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was forty-one and his own table had emptied. Marguerite Saintard, the wife he had waited eight years to afford, died in 1735; their small daughter Marguerite-Agnès died in 1737. He was left in his rooms in the parish of Saint-Sulpice with his son Pierre-Jean and not much money.

So the run of paintings that made him famous — mothers with children, governesses buttoning coats, boys sent off to school, a family waiting on a blessing — came out of a household that had been taken apart. Nothing in the pictures says so. That is characteristic.

Things were about to improve. In 1744 he married Françoise-Marguerite Pouget, a widow with money, and for the first time in his life he stopped being poor. In 1743 the Académie made him a councillor; by 1752 he had a royal pension, and by 1757 an apartment and studio in the Louvre itself. The man who could not be promoted out of animals and fruit had quietly become one of the most secure painters in France.

## Interesting Facts

- Chardin was presented to Louis XV at Versailles on 27 November 1740 and gave him this painting together with its pendant, *The Diligent Mother*.
- The child stammering through the blessing is a small boy — the verse under Lépicié's print calls him the sister's "petit frère" — dressed, as small boys then were, in skirts.
- Lépicié's 1744 engraving after the picture was one of the most celebrated prints of the eighteenth century, and Chardin is generally said to have earned more from engravings after his work than from the paintings themselves.
- He painted the subject repeatedly; versions were exhibited at the Salons of 1740, 1746 and 1761.
- The canvas is small — under fifty centimetres high — for a picture that carries this much weight.
- Twenty years on, Diderot would use Chardin as the stick he beat other painters with, calling his pictures nature itself.
