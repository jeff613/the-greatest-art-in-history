## The Story

A man and a woman stand in a darkening potato field, heads bowed, tools set down, as the distant church bell rings the evening Angelus. Millet said he painted it remembering his grandmother, who would stop the family's work in the fields at the sound of the bell to pray for the dead. It is a small canvas, barely more than half a meter tall, and it became one of the most famous, most fought-over, and most obsessed-about paintings on earth.

It began as a private commission. The Boston collector Thomas Gold Appleton ordered the picture — Millet first called it Prayer for the Potato Crop — but never came to collect it. So Millet reworked it, adding the church steeple on the horizon that turned a scene of rural piety into something universal, and renamed it The Angelus. It sold for a trifle and then began climbing through private collections as its reproductions — engravings, postcards, plates, devotional prints — spread into practically every parlor in France.

The climax came on 1 July 1889, at the auction of Eugène Secrétan's collection in Paris. The French state, through a consortium fronted by Antonin Proust, was determined to keep The Angelus; the American Art Association was determined to take it. The bidding war electrified the room, and when the hammer fell around 553,000 francs the painting was briefly France's — until the government balked at paying, and the Americans carried it off to tour the United States before adoring crowds. National humiliation lasted barely a year: in 1890 Alfred Chauchard, the department-store magnate behind the Grands Magasins du Louvre, bought it back for 750,000 francs, then the highest price ever paid for a modern picture, and bequeathed it to the nation.

Then came Salvador Dalí, who had been unaccountably terrified of the image since childhood. He insisted the couple were not praying over a potato basket but mourning over their buried child, and at his urging the Louvre X-rayed the canvas in 1963. The scan revealed a painted-over geometric shape near the basket — one Dalí triumphantly read as a small coffin. Scholars remain unconvinced that is what it is, but the shape is undeniably there.

## The World Behind It

The Angelus was painted in the late 1850s, but its real story belongs to the Gilded Age. By the 1880s, France had canonized the once-suspect Millet as the painter of national piety, soil, and toil — precisely as new American fortunes began buying up French art wholesale. The 1889 auction fell in the year of the Exposition Universelle and the new Eiffel Tower, with patriotic feeling at full boil; losing The Angelus to America was reported like a lost battle, and deputies debated the fiasco in parliament. The affair became a founding scandal of French cultural-patrimony politics, still cited when governments block art exports today.

The painting's fame also fed on religion. In a France bitterly split between clerical and secular camps, this image of unforced, working-class prayer was claimed by both sides — proof of the peasantry's eternal faith, or a dignified portrait of a vanishing world. Millions of reproductions hung in farmhouses, rectories, and schoolrooms. By 1900 The Angelus was less a painting than a piece of folklore, which is exactly why the Surrealists later found it so deliciously disturbing.

## The Artist at This Moment

Millet painted The Angelus between 1857 and 1859, in the lean middle years at Barbizon. He was in his mid-forties, supporting Catherine and their many children on erratic sales, and still smarting from the critical beating The Gleaners had taken at the 1857 Salon. The Angelus was, by comparison, a small private job for an American client — he never exhibited it at the Salon at all, and when Appleton failed to collect it, Millet let it go for a few hundred francs to keep the household running.

He gave the picture his deepest personal material: the memory of his grandmother Louise, the most devout person in his life, halting the harvest to pray. Millet was not conventionally religious as an adult, but he was steeped in the Bible and fatalistic about peasant life, and he insisted the painting was about the sound of a bell across the fields at dusk. He died in 1875 with no idea that this modest, uncollected commission would become the most reproduced painting in France and the most expensive modern picture in the world.

## Interesting Facts

- The painting was commissioned by Bostonian Thomas Gold Appleton and originally titled Prayer for the Potato Crop; when he never collected it, Millet added the church steeple and renamed it The Angelus.
- At the 1889 Secrétan sale the American Art Association outlasted a French consortium led by Antonin Proust, driving the price to about 553,000 francs — and took the painting to America when the French government refused to pay.
- Alfred Chauchard bought it back for France in 1890 for 750,000 francs, a record for a modern painting, and his bequest carried it into the Louvre in 1910 and the Musée d'Orsay in 1986.
- Salvador Dalí was obsessed with the image for decades and wrote an entire book about it, The Tragic Myth of Millet's Angelus, published in 1963.
- A Louvre X-ray made at Dalí's urging revealed a painted-over geometric shape near the couple's feet; Dalí declared it the coffin of their child, though scholars have never accepted the identification.
- In 1932 a disturbed visitor slashed the canvas with a knife at the Louvre; it was repaired and returned to view.
