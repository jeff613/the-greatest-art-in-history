## The Story

Six itinerant circus performers stand in a barren, dust-colored waste that seems to stretch nowhere: a burly harlequin in his diamond costume, a fat jester in red, two boys, a small girl with a basket of flowers, and off to the right a seated woman who looks away from all of them. They are a troupe — saltimbanques, the traveling acrobats and clowns who scraped a living entertaining French crowds — but nobody here is performing, and nobody quite connects. They stand together and utterly alone, marooned between acts in a landscape with no horizon, no town, no audience. It is the largest and greatest picture of Picasso's Rose Period, the gentler phase that followed the icy blue years, and its warmth is deceptive: beneath the terracotta tones lies one of the loneliest images he ever made.

The canvas hides a long, restless history. When conservators at the National Gallery of Art X-rayed it in 1980, they found that Picasso had painted the composition at least twice before, over roughly nine months, wiping out entire earlier scenes as he went — an outdoor circus setting in one version, a horse-race in another, figures shifted, added, and erased. The final group was assembled from studies of harlequins and acrobats he had been making for a year. The saltimbanques were partly a self-portrait of his own circle: the harlequin is often read as Picasso himself, the wanderers standing in for the poor, rootless artists of Montmartre.

The painting later cast a strange spell over one of the great poets of the century. Rainer Maria Rilke lived for several months in 1915 in a Munich apartment where the canvas hung on the wall, and he studied it obsessively. Seven years on it surfaced in his Fifth Duino Elegy — a meditation on the acrobats as figures of human transience and effort, dedicated to Hertha Koenig, the picture's then-owner. Few paintings have written themselves so deeply into another art form.

## The World Behind It

In the Paris of 1905, the traveling circus was everywhere in the imagination of poets and painters. The saltimbanques — cheap, mobile, half-glamorous, half-destitute — were a natural mirror for the avant-garde, who saw in these outsiders a version of their own precarious lives on the margins of respectable society. Picasso and his friends, including the poet Guillaume Apollinaire, haunted the Cirque Médrano near Montmartre, and the clowns and harlequins spilled straight from its ring into their work.

Picasso himself had just begun to climb out of poverty. The gnawing hunger of the Blue Period was easing; he had a lover, Fernande Olivier, and a leaky studio in the Bateau-Lavoir tenement, a warren of artists' rooms where the roof shook in the wind. Saltimbanques belongs to that hinge moment — no longer desperate, not yet famous — when the melancholy of the outsider was becoming, in his hands, something monumental.

## The Artist at This Moment

Picasso was twenty-three or twenty-four, newly settled in Paris for good and increasingly sure of his powers. The Rose Period was a happier chapter than the one before it: the palette had warmed, and so, a little, had his life. He was in love with Fernande Olivier and surrounded by a tight band of poets and painters — Apollinaire, Max Jacob, André Salmon — who treated his cramped Montmartre studio as a headquarters.

Yet the restlessness that would define him is already visible under the paint, in the buried compositions the X-rays exposed: he could not leave the picture alone, reworking it for the better part of a year. Within two years he would abandon this wistful, figurative manner entirely and tear into the savage geometry of Les Demoiselles d'Avignon. Family of Saltimbanques is the last great statement of the young Picasso who still wanted to move you before he decided he would rather astonish you.

## Interesting Facts

- X-rays taken by the National Gallery in 1980 revealed at least two entirely different compositions underneath, reworked over about nine months.
- The poet Rainer Maria Rilke lived with the painting on his wall in Munich in 1915, and it inspired his Fifth Duino Elegy, dedicated to the picture's owner, Hertha Koenig.
- The tall harlequin at the left is widely read as a stand-in for Picasso himself.
- It is the masterpiece of the Rose Period, the warm-toned phase that followed the melancholy Blue Period.
- The banker and collector Chester Dale bought it in 1931 and bequeathed it to the National Gallery of Art in 1963.
- The saltimbanques were drawn from the real traveling performers of the Cirque Médrano, which Picasso frequented near his Montmartre studio.
