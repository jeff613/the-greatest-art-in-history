## The Story

The most charming picture of the eighteenth century began as a piece of pure smut. The story survives in the journal of the playwright Charles Collé, who heard it soon after — so it should be held a little loosely — but it is far too good to leave out. A wealthy gentleman, long assumed to be the Baron de Saint-Julien, receiver-general for the French clergy, approached the painter Gabriel-François Doyen with a proposition. He wanted a portrait of his mistress on a garden swing. He wanted a bishop to be the one pushing the swing. And he wanted himself painted into the scene, placed exactly where he could gaze up at his lover's legs as she rose into the air. Doyen, a serious history painter, was, by his own account, so appalled that he could barely reply — and he steered the commission toward a younger man with fewer scruples and a lighter touch: Jean-Honoré Fragonard.

Fragonard took the job with delight, and softened only the details. The pushing bishop became a shadowy older man in the foliage — perhaps an unsuspecting husband — while the young lover reclines in the bushes below, staring straight up the billowing froth of the lady's petticoats. She is the sun of the whole picture, a burst of pink silk against a churning green bower, kicking one dainty shoe off into the air in a spasm of abandon. Fragonard packed the garden with sly commentary: a statue of Cupid raises a finger to his lips, sworn to secrecy, while stone cherubs watch the flirtation with knowing looks.

What could have been merely crude became, in Fragonard's hands, an explosion of joy — weightless, sunlit, and irresistibly alive. The whole giddy spirit of the *ancien régime*, its pursuit of pleasure with the ground already trembling beneath it, is somehow caught in a single swinging girl and a flying slipper. It is at once the frothiest painting imaginable and, in hindsight, a kind of portrait of a world about to fall.

## The World Behind It

Fragonard painted for a narrow, glittering slice of French society: the aristocrats, tax farmers, and financiers of the *ancien régime* who had money, leisure, and an appetite for private amusement. Pictures like this were never meant for the public Salon, where reputations were made on lofty historical and religious subjects. They were made for the boudoir and the private cabinet, to be enjoyed among friends who understood the game. A commission like the Baron's — frankly erotic, personal, a little scandalous — was exactly the sort of work the official art world frowned upon and wealthy patrons quietly craved.

That taste for elegant naughtiness was the essence of Rococo. It had flourished under Louis XV, whose own mistresses, Madame de Pompadour and Madame du Barry, were great patrons of exactly this fizzing, sensual style. Beneath the frivolity, though, France was sliding toward crisis — debt, hunger, and resentment that would erupt in revolution within a generation. Fragonard's swinging lovers belong to the last golden afternoon of that doomed leisure class, painted by the man who captured its pleasures better than anyone.

## The Artist at This Moment

In 1767 Fragonard was about thirty-five and had just made the most consequential decision of his life. Two years earlier he had been received into the Royal Academy as a history painter, the most prestigious rank an artist could hold, and a glorious official career lay open before him. He turned his back on it. The grand, laborious world of state commissions bored him, and he found he could make a better living — and have far more fun — painting intimate, sensual pictures for private patrons.

*The Swing* is the emblem of that choice. It shows Fragonard at the height of his powers, working fast and brilliantly in the mode that suited him: light, witty, sumptuous, and just a little wicked. He was riding high, the darling of a pleasure-loving elite that seemed as if it would last forever. He could not have guessed that the very world buying his work — and the taste that prized it — would be swept away, taking his fortune and his fame down with it.

## Interesting Facts

- The commission's saucy origin comes from the journal of the playwright Charles Collé, who recorded that the patron first asked Doyen for a bishop to push the swing so he could see his mistress's legs.
- The flying shoe is loaded with meaning: a lost slipper was an old symbol of lost virtue, making the kicked-off shoe a wink at the lady's abandon.
- The statue on the left, finger to his lips, is a Cupid modeled on a famous sculpture by Étienne-Maurice Falconet — a god of love sworn to keep the lovers' secret.
- Between August and November 2021 the Wallace Collection cleaned the painting on site — the first time any work there had been conserved in public view — stripping away a century of yellowed varnish to reveal startlingly fresh pinks and greens.
- The picture can never travel: the terms of Lady Wallace's 1897 bequest forbid any object in the collection from being lent, so *The Swing* has not left the building in over a century.
- Its French title, *L'Escarpolette*, simply means "the swing" — the innocent name for one of art's most knowing pictures.
