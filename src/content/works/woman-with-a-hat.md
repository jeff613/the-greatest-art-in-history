## The Story

In the autumn of 1905 the Salon d'Automne gave over one room to a group of young painters who used colour like a weapon, and near its centre hung Henri Matisse's portrait of his wife Amélie in a huge, flowered hat. It should have been a conventional society picture. Instead Matisse had painted her face in slabs of green, lilac, and vermilion, her gloved hand a smear, the whole surface slashed on with a speed that looked, to 1905 eyes, like contempt for craft. Visitors were scandalised. Crowds gathered to jeer, and one persistent account has spectators trying to scratch the paint from the canvas — though the story may have grown in the telling. What is certain is that this room of raw colour provoked the critic Louis Vauxcelles to describe a delicate marble bust stranded among the lurid canvases as "Donatello chez les fauves" — Donatello among the wild beasts. The insult stuck, and Fauvism, the century's first art movement, had its name.

For Matisse the reception was agony. He was thirty-five, little known, and broke, and the mockery fell on a painting of the woman he loved. Amélie later insisted she had in fact worn sober black that day; the riot of colour was entirely his invention. Then rescue arrived in the form of two Americans. Leo and Gertrude Stein, hunting bargains among the derided moderns, bought *Woman with a Hat* — for around 500 francs, by the usual account, though the exact figure is disputed. The sale steadied Matisse's nerves and his finances, and it drew him into the Steins' circle, where he would soon meet a young Spaniard named Picasso.

The picture that had made him a laughing-stock became a badge of honour. It hung in the Steins' apartment on the rue de Fleurus, a pilgrimage site for the avant-garde, and it announced that a painter could treat colour as an independent force — free of the object, free of "correct" appearances — years before abstraction. What looked like vandalism in 1905 is now seen as one of the doors through which modern painting walked.

## The World Behind It

The Salon d'Automne, founded only in 1903, existed precisely because the official Salon was too timid for work like this. It was the place where Paris came to be shocked, and in 1905 it obliged. The city was the undisputed capital of Western art, crowded with ambitious foreigners and rival factions, and a generation raised on Impressionism and the fierce colour of Van Gogh and Gauguin was pushing towards something more extreme. Matisse and his friends André Derain and Maurice de Vlaminck had spent the summer painting on the Mediterranean coast in blazing, unmixed hues, and they brought the results to the autumn show as a deliberate provocation. The public read it as either a joke or an assault on decency, and the word *fauve* — beast — captured the sense that these men had abandoned civilisation itself. Yet within a few years the scandal had curdled into influence, and colour liberated from description became one of the founding facts of twentieth-century art.

## The Artist at This Moment

Matisse in 1905 was not a young firebrand but a cautious, anxious man of thirty-five who had come to art late and worked at it grimly. He had a wife and children to support and almost no money; Amélie ran a millinery shop to help keep the household going, which lends a private irony to the fashionable hat in the portrait. He had gambled everything on painting against his father's wishes, and years of poverty had followed. The Salon d'Automne of 1905 was, in a real sense, his trial. The derision that greeted the picture wounded him deeply — he was temperamentally the opposite of a provocateur, a man who longed for order and calm — but the Stein purchase told him that someone, at least, understood what he was doing. It was the hinge of his career: the moment the struggling clerk-turned-painter became the leader of the Paris avant-garde, and the rival against whom the young Picasso would measure himself for the next half-century.

## Interesting Facts

- The model was the painter's wife, Amélie; she later said her real outfit that day was sober black, and the wild colour was entirely Matisse's invention.
- "Fauvism" comes from the critic Louis Vauxcelles' remark about the exhibition room — "Donatello chez les fauves," Donatello among the wild beasts — though Vauxcelles later suggested he was echoing a bystander's quip.
- Some accounts claim outraged visitors tried to scratch the paint off the canvas; the tale is often repeated but hard to verify.
- Leo and Gertrude Stein bought the painting for roughly 500 francs, a purchase that rescued Matisse's morale and pulled him into their famous salon.
- In the Steins' apartment the picture hung near works they were also acquiring by the young Picasso, seeding the two artists' lifelong rivalry.
- Elise S. Haas bequeathed the painting to the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art in 1990, where it hangs today.
