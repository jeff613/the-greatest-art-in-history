## The Story

It is nearly four and a half metres tall, painted in distemper on wood, and it was made for the Franciscans of Santa Croce to hang high in their church where the whole congregation could see it. Cimabue gave them a Christ who is not reigning from the cross in the old Byzantine way, upright and open-eyed and victorious, but sagging — the body pulled into a long curve, the head fallen onto the shoulder, the weight of a dead man hanging on nails. For Franciscans, who built their whole spirituality on the physical suffering of Christ and of the poor, that was the point.

For roughly seven centuries it hung in Florence and nothing much happened to it. Then, on 4 November 1966, after days of rain, the Arno broke into the city in the dark. Santa Croce sits low, in one of the worst-hit quarters. The water came through carrying heating oil from ruptured tanks, mud, and naphtha, and inside the church it rose until it reached the height of Christ's halo. It stayed for hours. The wood swelled. The oil soaked in. When the flood drained away, roughly sixty per cent of the painted surface was simply gone.

The photographs of it — the ruined face, the great gaps in the torso — went around the world, and the crucifix became the image of what Florence had lost. Volunteers who came from everywhere to dig books and paintings out of the mud were nicknamed the *angeli del fango*, the mud angels.

A restoration team under Umberto Baldini and Ornella Casazza worked on it for ten years. They made a decision that is still argued over: rather than repaint what was missing, they filled the losses with a fine hatching of colour in the same tonal range, so that the eye reads the whole shape from a distance while every inch of loss stays visible up close. The crucifix went back on display in 1976, and it now hangs in the museum in the convent's old refectory.

What you look at, in other words, is not a painting that survived. It is a painting that mostly did not, held together and honestly labelled as a ruin.

## The World Behind It

Santa Croce was the Franciscan headquarters in Florence, and the Franciscans were the radical order of the thirteenth century — founded by a man who had given away everything, preaching to crowds in the vernacular, and enormously popular in exactly the merchant city that was making its money in ways Francis had renounced. The rich of Florence poured funds into Franciscan building, which is how a mendicant order ended up with one of the largest churches in Italy.

That contradiction shaped the art. The order wanted images that were direct, physical, and emotionally legible to ordinary people, not the remote gold-ground hierarchies of the imperial Byzantine tradition. A crucifix in which Christ visibly hurts is a piece of Franciscan argument.

## The Artist at This Moment

Cimabue was at the height of whatever reputation he had. He had been documented in Rome in 1272, had painted a huge enthroned Virgin now in the Louvre, and was probably at work on or about to begin the frescoes at Assisi — the other great Franciscan site, where he covered the upper church with scenes that have since deteriorated so badly that the white lead has oxidised to black and the paintings read like photographic negatives.

This was also, on the usual reckoning, his second attempt at a monumental crucifix; an earlier one survives at Arezzo. The Santa Croce version is the more advanced of the two, the body heavier and more convincingly dead.

Within a generation Giotto would be working in the same church, and Dante would be writing the line that fixed Cimabue's reputation as the man Giotto replaced.

## Interesting Facts

- The 1966 Arno flood put water up to the height of Christ's halo and destroyed about sixty per cent of the painted surface.
- The restoration took ten years; the crucifix returned to public view in 1976.
- The restorers deliberately did not repaint the losses, filling them with fine hatching so the damage stays visible up close — a decision still debated in conservation.
- The flood volunteers who saved Florence's art became known as the *angeli del fango*, the mud angels.
- Its dating is genuinely unsettled: proposals run from the 1260s to the late 1280s, and the church itself says only "before 1288".
- Cimabue's other surviving monumental crucifix, at Arezzo, is generally reckoned the earlier of the two.
