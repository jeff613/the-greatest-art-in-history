## The Story

A dense bed of irises fills the frame, their long leaves twisting and swording upward, their blooms a deep violet-blue crowded so tightly they seem to jostle one another — and among them, off to the left, stands a single white iris, alone in all that purple. Van Gogh painted it in May 1889, in the walled garden of the asylum at Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, within days of committing himself there. It was one of the very first things he made inside the institution, and he leaned on painting like this as a defense against the madness he felt closing in.

He treated it, characteristically, as a "study" rather than a finished picture. He knelt in the garden and painted the flowers from life, close up, cropping them so hard that they spill off every edge, with no sky and barely any horizon. Each iris is drawn with a firm, sinuous outline — a habit he had absorbed from the Japanese prints he loved — and no two blooms are quite the same. The lone white flower has tempted a century of viewers to read it as a hidden self-portrait, the outsider, the odd one out, though Van Gogh himself never said any such thing.

What he did say was that work was saving him. He had arrived at Saint-Rémy shattered, after the breakdown in Arles and the severing of his ear, and he found that painting steadily in the garden kept the terror at bay. His brother Theo saw the picture's power at once and sent it to the Salon des Indépendants in Paris that September, telling Vincent it struck the eye from clear across the room. Neither brother could have dreamed what it would one day be worth: in 1987 Irises sold at auction for $53.9 million, then the highest price ever paid for a painting, and the first ever to break fifty million dollars.

## The World Behind It

Van Gogh painted Irises inside Saint-Paul-de-Mausole, a former monastery near Saint-Rémy that had become a private asylum for the mentally ill. He had checked himself in voluntarily in May 1889, after the people of Arles petitioned to have him confined; the ear-cutting the previous December had frightened his neighbors badly. Behind the walls he was one of only a handful of patients rattling around a half-empty institution, granted an extra room to use as a studio and, when he was well enough, the freedom to paint the garden and, later, the wheat fields beyond.

The nineteenth century's treatment of mental illness was crude and often hopeless — rest, isolation, cold baths, close confinement — and Van Gogh was clear-eyed about where he had landed. Yet the year at Saint-Rémy, punctuated by devastating attacks that could stop him working for weeks at a time, became one of the most productive of his life: some 150 paintings, The Starry Night among them, poured out of that confinement. Irises came first, in the relative calm of his opening days, before the worst of the attacks returned — a burst of pure looking from a man bracing himself for the dark.

## The Artist at This Moment

In May 1889 Van Gogh was thirty-six, and had been an artist for barely nine years — all of his art still crammed into a single frantic decade. He was also at his lowest ebb. The dream of an artists' colony in Arles had collapsed with Gauguin's flight and his own breakdown; he had mutilated himself, been hospitalized, and finally concluded that he could not live safely on his own. Committing himself to the asylum was at once an admission of defeat and an act of self-preservation.

He clung to work as the one thing that steadied him. He needed, he wrote to Theo, a fixed line of conduct — and that line was painting, daily, whatever his state. He was terrified the attacks would rob him of the ability to paint at all, and he raced to make the most of every lucid stretch. Irises belongs to one such stretch, at the very start of his time inside, when the garden was in bloom and his hand was still sure. Just over a year later he would be dead. This quiet, teeming bed of flowers is the sound of a man fighting for his life with a paintbrush.

## Interesting Facts

- Van Gogh painted Irises in his first week inside the Saint-Rémy asylum, in May 1889, working from the flowers growing in the hospital garden.
- He regarded it as a study rather than a finished work, and, unusually for him, made no preliminary drawing — he painted it straight onto the canvas.
- A single white iris standing among the purple has long tempted viewers to see it as a self-portrait of the outsider, though Van Gogh left no such explanation.
- The strong, curving outlines around each flower reflect the Japanese woodblock prints Van Gogh collected and revered.
- Theo entered the painting in the 1889 Salon des Indépendants, where it hung among the work of the Paris avant-garde.
- In 1987 Irises sold at Sotheby's for $53.9 million — a world record, and the first painting ever to pass fifty million; the buyer, Alan Bond, could not pay, and the Getty Museum acquired it in 1990.
