## The Story

Three people crouch in a dark room and none of them is looking at any of the others. A naked man squats at the back, knees drawn up, arms folded over the woman in front of him; he is all shins and ribs and sockets, and his eyes are wide open and fixed somewhere past the edge of the picture. The woman sits between his legs, also naked, softer, her head tipped down and her gaze turned inward. Between her feet a child in a patterned smock peers out from under the shelter of her body. They are three figures stacked into a single pyramid, touching everywhere and connected nowhere.

Schiele painted this canvas — a metre and a half square — in 1918, and he did not call it *The Family*. His title was *Kauerndes Menschenpaar*, "Crouching Couple." The name it carries now was applied by the Viennese critic Berta Zuckerkandl, and it changed the picture permanently: from a study of two bodies into a portrait of a household, which it never was. The man has Schiele's face. The woman is not his wife Edith, who was pregnant that year and did not pose for it; she was a model. And the child was an afterthought in the most literal sense: Schiele painted flowers into that space first and then covered them over with a small boy, said to have been modelled by his young nephew.

Look closely and the canvas is not finished. The man's left hand is barely resolved; passages of the background are laid in and left. He had months in hand and did not use them here, because 1918 had suddenly buried him in work.

Then the mourners got hold of it. Edith died of Spanish influenza on 28 October 1918, six months pregnant. Schiele died three days later, on 31 October, aged twenty-eight. A painting of a man, a woman and a child by a man whose wife and unborn child had just died was never going to stay a study of two bodies. It became his epitaph, whether or not he meant it as one.

## The World Behind It

Nineteen eighteen was the year Austria-Hungary came apart. The empire was starving — Vienna's bread and fat rations had collapsed, coal ran out, and the army was disintegrating in the field — and by the end of October the Habsburg state that had governed central Europe for six centuries had ceased to exist in anything but name. The armistice came on 3 November. Schiele had been dead three days.

The other catastrophe arrived that autumn without warning. The influenza pandemic of 1918 killed somewhere in the tens of millions worldwide, and unlike ordinary flu it took the young and strong. In Vienna it moved through a population already worn down by four years of hunger. Klimt had died that February, after a stroke.

For Schiele personally it was, absurdly, the best year of his life. In March the Vienna Secession gave him its main hall for the forty-ninth exhibition, hung with dozens of his paintings and drawings. He designed the poster himself, arranging his fellow artists around a table like the Last Supper with himself in Christ's place and one chair conspicuously empty, where Klimt would have sat. The show sold. Prices for his drawings jumped, portrait commissions arrived, and after a decade of scandal he was, at twenty-eight, the leading painter in Vienna.

## The Artist at This Moment

He had just inherited a city. With Klimt gone in February, the succession was obvious to everybody including Schiele, who took a larger studio that year. Edith was expecting their first child. The war was clearly ending. For the first time since he left the Academy there was money coming in and no scandal attached to it.

He kept painting large, ambitious, deliberately awkward canvases of people who cannot reach each other. That was simply what he did, and he had been doing it since he was twenty; the biographical coincidence that made this one look prophetic was still months away. He was also, at last, drawing bodies that were allowed to be soft — the late nudes are heavier and warmer than anything he made before the war — without ever letting the figures in them close the gap between one person and the next.

Edith fell ill in the last week of October. In the three days between her death and his own, sick himself, Schiele drew her — a few sketches of his wife's face, made from her deathbed. They are the last things he did.

## Interesting Facts

- Schiele's own title was *Kauerndes Menschenpaar* — "Crouching Couple." The name *The Family* was given by the critic Berta Zuckerkandl, and it recast the picture as autobiography.
- The woman is not Edith Schiele. She was a model; Edith was pregnant at the time and did not sit for it.
- The child was painted last, over a bunch of flowers Schiele had put in that space first, and is said to have been modelled by his nephew.
- Parts of the canvas were never finished, including the man's left hand.
- In March 1918 the Vienna Secession gave Schiele its main hall; he designed the poster as a Last Supper with himself at the head and an empty chair for Klimt, who had died weeks earlier.
- Edith died on 28 October 1918 and Schiele on 31 October, both of Spanish influenza. He was twenty-eight, and he spent the days between drawing her.
- The painting stayed with the collector Hans Böhler from 1918 until the Belvedere bought it from him in 1948, by which time he was living in New York.
