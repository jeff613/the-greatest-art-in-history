## The Story

A young peasant strides downhill across a plowed field at dusk, his arm flung wide, scattering grain with every step. He is huge — he fills the canvas the way a general fills an equestrian portrait — and his face is lost in shadow, which makes him less a man than a force: sowing itself, painted life-size.

Millet unveiled him at the Salon of 1850–51, the same exhibition where Courbet's Burial at Ornans detonated. Together the two pictures announced that Realism had arrived, and the reaction split cleanly along political lines. Republicans and radicals hailed the sower as a modern hero, the laborer finally given his monument. Conservatives saw something else entirely: barely two years after the 1848 revolution, here was a dark colossus hurling handfuls of something across France — and more than one commentator suggested that what he was sowing was revolution. Millet, characteristically, said he was painting what he had grown up watching on his father's land in Normandy, and that the gesture of the sower — the same gesture in the Gospel parable — was simply eternal.

Then the painting did something almost unheard of for a French masterpiece: it left. William Morris Hunt, a young Boston painter who had attached himself to Millet as student and friend, bought The Sower around 1851–52, when Millet's prices were still pitiful, and took it home to America. It passed in 1874 to the Boston collector Quincy Adams Shaw, whose heirs gave it to the Museum of Fine Arts in 1917. France's great image of the peasant has spent nearly its whole life in Massachusetts.

Its strangest afterlife belongs to Vincent van Gogh, who worshipped Millet and called copying him "translating into another language." Van Gogh never saw this canvas — he knew the composition only from prints and photographs — yet he returned to The Sower compulsively for a decade, producing well over a dozen drawings and paintings after it, from his earliest student exercises to the blazing versions painted at Arles and Saint-Rémy, where the sower strides beneath a huge yellow sun Millet never painted.

## The World Behind It

The Salon of 1850–51 opened onto a France still trembling from 1848. The Second Republic was two years old and visibly dying; Louis-Napoléon would strangle it with his coup in December 1851. In that atmosphere, art about the rural poor was read as electoral politics by other means — the countryside held the votes, and both left and right claimed to speak for the peasant. A monumental laborer, painted dark and faceless and in motion, could not be neutral no matter what his maker intended.

The deeper anxiety was that Millet painted work itself, without anecdote or charm. Academic tradition allowed peasants as decoration — dancing at fairs, dozing among sheep. Millet gave the sower the striding pose of classical heroes and the scale of history painting, and stripped away everything pretty. Critics who hated it understood it exactly: it claimed that a man scattering seed on cold ground deserved the space in art that had belonged to kings. Within a generation, that claim had won — and had crossed the Atlantic, where American collectors, Boston's first among them, made Millet one of the most beloved painters in the United States.

## The Artist at This Moment

The Sower was Millet's first great statement from Barbizon. He had arrived in the village only the year before, in June 1849, fleeing the cholera epidemic in Paris with Catherine and the children, and the move completed his transformation: the failed portraitist and reluctant painter of rococo nudes was gone, and the painter of peasants had taken his place. He was thirty-six, desperately poor, and finally certain of what he was for.

He built the figure from memory — the sowers of his childhood on the family farm at Gruchy, where he had done the work himself — and he labored over the pose through repeated studies and at least one earlier version until the stride and the flinging arm locked into place. The picture earned him notoriety rather than money; selling it cheaply to Hunt within a year or two was simple necessity. But Millet kept returning to the motif in drawings, pastels, and prints for the rest of his life, as if he knew the striding figure was his signature. He was right: when the world thinks of Millet, it sees this man's silhouette.

## Interesting Facts

- The Sower debuted at the Salon of 1850–51 alongside Courbet's A Burial at Ornans — the one-two punch that announced Realism to the French public.
- Conservative critics, jittery so soon after the 1848 revolution, read the dark striding figure as a revolutionary sowing unrest rather than wheat.
- Millet's American pupil William Morris Hunt bought the canvas around 1851–52 for very little; it reached Quincy Adams Shaw in 1874 and entered Boston's Museum of Fine Arts in 1917.
- Van Gogh never saw the original — it was already in America — yet he made well over a dozen drawings and paintings after The Sower, and twenty-one painted copies after Millet in the Saint-Rémy asylum alone.
- Millet repeated the sower motif in paintings, pastels, and prints across two decades; another painted version now hangs in the Yamanashi Prefectural Museum of Art in Japan.
- The sower's flinging gesture deliberately echoes the Gospel parable of the sower — Millet, raised on nightly Bible readings, painted farm labor with the weight of scripture.
