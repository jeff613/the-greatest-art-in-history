## The Story

A bareback rider stands balanced on a white horse in mid-gallop. A clown in the foreground turns his back on us, holding up a curtain of orange hair like a stage flat. A ringmaster cracks a whip, an acrobat cartwheels through the air, and the whole lower half of the picture flies upward on a set of rising curves. Above them, in tiered rows, sits the audience — and the higher the rows go, the stiffer and duller the spectators become, until the back row is a wall of grey identical faces watching without expression.

Seurat was working out a theory. He had absorbed the ideas of the mathematician Charles Henry, who argued that the direction of a line carried a predictable emotional charge — lines rising to the right read as gaiety, falling lines as sadness — and *The Circus* is that argument made visible, all whipping upward arcs in yellow, red, and blue. It is his most deliberately engineered painting, and also his liveliest, which is a joke he might have enjoyed. The venue was real: the Cirque Fernando in Montmartre, a permanent circus building that Degas and Toulouse-Lautrec had both painted.

He never finished it. Sections remain thinly covered, the paint stopping short of the painted blue border he had begun. He hung it anyway at the Salon des Indépendants in March 1891, alongside the other Neo-Impressionists, and during the run of the exhibition he fell ill. What he had is still not settled — meningitis, pneumonia, an infectious angina, diphtheria have all been proposed. He died on 29 March, thirty-one years old, at his parents' home.

Two weeks later his infant son, Pierre-Georges, died of the same illness. Madeleine Knobloch, the woman almost none of Seurat's friends had known existed, was pregnant with a second child, who did not survive either. The painter had been at work for about a decade; the movement he founded outlived him by a generation, and this unfinished circus was the last thing he had to say.

## The World Behind It

Montmartre in 1890 was the entertainment engine of Paris — cabarets, dance halls, the Moulin Rouge in its first year, and the Cirque Fernando pulling crowds into a purpose-built ring on the boulevard. Popular spectacle had become the great subject for advanced painters, who found in it both modern life and a set of shapes nobody had painted before.

Seurat's circus, though, is not a celebration. Look at the price of the seats: the cheap high rows are painted as a compressed, stupefied mass, the expensive lower boxes as individuals. Critics have read the picture as a diagram of a class system watching itself be entertained, which fits a painter who moved in the anarchist-sympathising circle around Félix Fénéon — though Seurat, as usual, explained nothing.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was thirty-one and had spent five years defending pointillism against people who thought it a gimmick, including former allies. He had become guarded to the point of paranoia about his methods, convinced that other painters were taking his discoveries, and had fallen out with several of them over who had invented what.

His private life was still entirely private. Madeleine Knobloch had been living with him; their son had been born in February 1890; he had told almost no one, and only introduced Madeleine to his mother days before his death.

He had also become more theoretical, moving from optics into Charles Henry's psychology of line, and there were friends who worried the system was tightening around him. He was, by every account, working with total confidence. He had nine days left when the exhibition opened.

## Interesting Facts

- The painting is unfinished — Seurat died during the exhibition where he first showed it.
- Its structure follows Charles Henry's theory that upward-tilting lines produce a sensation of gaiety; almost every major line in the lower half rises.
- The cheap seats at the top are painted as an anonymous grey mass while the expensive seats below hold distinct individuals.
- Seurat painted a border directly onto the canvas edge, as he had on the Grande Jatte, and it too was left incomplete.
- The venue is the Cirque Fernando in Montmartre, the same ring Degas and Toulouse-Lautrec painted.
- Seurat died on 29 March 1891 of an illness never conclusively identified; his baby son died of it two weeks later.
