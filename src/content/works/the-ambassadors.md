## The Story

There is something wrong with the floor. Between the two men, across the beautiful inlaid pavement, lies a pale grey smear, tilted and stretched like a spillage the painter forgot to wipe out. Step to the right of the picture, get close to the wall, and look back along its surface. The smear stands up and becomes a human skull.

Everything else in the painting is the opposite of a trick. Two men stand life-size in front of a green damask curtain, either side of a two-tiered stand loaded with the most expensive equipment of the age. The one on the left is Jean de Dinteville, seigneur de Polisy, sent to London by Francis I of France; his dagger gives his age as twenty-nine. The one on the right, in the sober scholar's gown, is his friend Georges de Selve, Bishop of Lavaur; his book gives his age as twenty-five.

Dinteville hated the posting. He wrote home that he was "the most melancholy, weary and wearisome ambassador that ever was seen." De Selve came over briefly in the spring of 1533 to keep him company, and the painting is a record of that visit — a monument to two young men who were only in the same room for a few weeks.

The objects between them are not decoration. The upper shelf holds instruments for measuring the heavens: a celestial globe, sundials, a quadrant, a torquetum. The lower shelf holds the world below: a terrestrial globe marked with Dinteville's estate at Polisy, a merchants' arithmetic book by Peter Apian lying open at pages of long division, a case of flutes, a Lutheran hymn-book showing Luther's translation of *Veni Sancte Spiritus* beside the Ten Commandments — and a lute with one string broken.

The historian of science John North argued that the shadows fix the scene precisely at four in the afternoon on Good Friday, 11 April 1533. Whether or not that reading holds, the message of the skull is plain enough, and Dinteville wore it as a badge on his cap: his personal motto was *memento mori*. Half hidden by the curtain in the top left corner, almost invisible, is a small silver crucifix.

## The World Behind It

Dinteville was standing in the wreckage of Christendom, and he knew it. During his embassy Henry VIII married Anne Boleyn, had Thomas Cranmer declare the marriage to Catherine of Aragon void, crowned Anne at Westminster on 1 June, was excommunicated by the pope that summer, and became the father of the future Elizabeth I in September. England was leaving Rome, and the French ambassador's job was to keep Henry from wrecking Francis I's own delicate arrangements with the papacy.

Georges de Selve was not a bystander either. He was a young bishop who had already argued in print for reconciliation between the Church and the reformers, and the two open books on the lower shelf — a Lutheran hymnal, a manual of division — set the argument out on the shelf like exhibits. The lute's broken string was a standard emblem of discord. In 1533 nobody in the picture yet knew how bad the discord would get: within two years Thomas More would be beheaded, and within four Anne Boleyn would be dead too.

## The Artist at This Moment

Holbein had been back in London for about a year and was rebuilding a career from scratch for the second time in his life. His first English patron, Thomas More, had resigned as Lord Chancellor in 1532 and was sliding towards ruin; that circle was no longer safe or useful. So Holbein took work where a foreigner could get it — from the German merchants of the Steelyard, and then from the people around Anne Boleyn and Thomas Cromwell who were assembling the new England.

The Ambassadors was the biggest and most ambitious thing he ever painted, and it was made for foreigners like himself: two Frenchmen far from home, in a country that was mid-revolution. It went back to France and hung in the great hall of Dinteville's chateau at Polisy, where an inventory records it in 1589. The National Gallery bought it in 1890, and it has been the most stared-at picture in the building ever since.

## Interesting Facts

- The grey shape on the floor is an anamorphic skull: painted so distorted that it only resolves into a skull when the viewer stands off to the right of the picture and looks across its surface.
- Jean de Dinteville's motto was *memento mori*, and he wears a small skull badge on his cap — so the skull on the floor is also, in effect, his signature.
- The globe on the lower shelf is marked with Polisy, the ambassador's own estate in Champagne, putting his house at the centre of the world.
- The arithmetic book lies open at pages beginning with the word *Dividirt* — divide — and the lute below it has a snapped string, both standard emblems of discord in a year when Europe was splitting apart.
- A small silver crucifix hangs at the top left, almost entirely hidden behind the green curtain and missed by most viewers.
- The painting stayed in the Dinteville family chateau at Polisy for generations; the National Gallery bought it in 1890.
