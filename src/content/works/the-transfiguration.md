## The Story

The *Transfiguration* is Raphael's final work, and it is split down the middle into two worlds. Above, on a mountaintop washed in unearthly light, Christ rises into the air between the prophets Moses and Elijah, robes blazing white, arms open, lifted clear of the earth while three apostles fall back dazzled below him. Beneath that radiance, the lower half plunges into darkness and turmoil: a crowd of desperate people gathers around a boy in the grip of a fit, whom the remaining apostles cannot heal. Serenity above, human helplessness below — the divine and the earthly held in a single frame, joined by the pointing hands of the crowd who seem to gesture up toward the only source of help.

Raphael took the commission around 1516 from Cardinal Giulio de' Medici, the future Pope Clement VII, who had ordered a second painting from Raphael's rival Sebastiano del Piombo to hang beside it — a deliberate contest between the two workshops. Raphael labored on the panel through the last years of his life and left it not quite finished; parts of the lower group were completed by his assistants after his death. It is often called the summit of his art and the picture in which he reached for something darker and more dramatic than the serene harmony that had made him famous, as if at the very end he were straining toward the storms of the coming age.

Then it became something more than a painting. When Raphael died suddenly on Good Friday, 6 April 1520, at the age of thirty-seven, the unfinished *Transfiguration* was set at the head of the room where his body lay in state. According to Giorgio Vasari, the sight of that glowing, living work of art beside the dead master was more than the mourners could bear — the contrast of the radiant picture and the still corpse, he wrote, made the hearts of all who saw it burst with grief. Rome mourned as though a prince had fallen, and Raphael was buried, as he had asked, inside the ancient Pantheon.

## The World Behind It

Raphael died at the height of an extraordinary career, and it left a hole. He had been the golden favorite of the papal court under Julius II and Leo X, the most sought-after artist in Europe, and the head of a workshop so large — some fifty assistants — that it functioned like a factory of images. He was also architect of the new St. Peter's and overseer of Rome's ancient ruins. When he was gone, an entire machine of art ground to a halt, and the men he had trained scattered to carry his manner across Italy.

Legends gathered around the death at once. Vasari reported that Raphael, who loved women and pleasure, had fallen ill after a night of excess, hidden the cause from his doctors, and been bled fatally by physicians who misjudged the sickness — a story impossible to confirm and easy to doubt, but irresistible to a Rome that wanted the beautiful painter's end to be as romantic as his life. What is certain is that he died young, on the day he was believed to have been born, and that his passing was felt as the close of a golden age.

## The Artist at This Moment

These were Raphael's final years, and they were crushingly busy. Buried under commissions from Pope Leo X, running his vast workshop, designing buildings and cataloguing antiquities, he was stretched thin across more work than any one man could finish. The *Transfiguration* was his most ambitious painting, and he was pouring into it a new intensity — deeper shadows, wilder emotion, a reaching for drama beyond the calm perfection of his youth — when his life was cut off.

He did not live to sign it off as complete. He worked on the panel almost to the end, and it was still in his studio, its lower reaches unfinished, when the fever took him. That is the painting's unbearable poignancy: it is the last thing the most fortunate artist of the Renaissance was making, left forever on the edge of completion, and it stood watch over him as he lay dead at thirty-seven.

## Interesting Facts

- It was Raphael's last painting, left unfinished at his death and completed in part by his workshop.
- The commission was a contest: Cardinal Giulio de' Medici ordered a rival altarpiece from Sebastiano del Piombo to be judged against it.
- Raphael died on Good Friday, 6 April 1520, at thirty-seven — the same day of the year on which he was believed to have been born.
- Vasari recorded that the unfinished panel was placed at the head of Raphael's deathbed, and that the sight of it beside his body overwhelmed the mourners.
- He was buried, at his own request, inside the Pantheon in Rome, where his tomb can still be visited.
- Napoleon's troops carried the painting off to Paris in 1797; it was returned to the Vatican after his fall.
