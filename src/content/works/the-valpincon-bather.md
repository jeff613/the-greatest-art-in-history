## The Story

She is turned away. That is the whole event. A woman sits on the edge of a bed in a curtained room, a striped cloth over one arm, a turban wound round her head, and shows us nothing but her back — the long unbroken sweep from shoulder to hip that Ingres would still be thinking about when he was an old man. There is no story attached, no myth, no name. Somebody is about to bathe.

Ingres painted it in Rome in 1808, aged twenty-eight, as part of the work he was required to send back to Paris to justify his Prix de Rome scholarship. The Academy had a clear idea of what those submissions should be: heroic male nudes, classical subjects, proof that the student had mastered the grand manner. Ingres sent bathing women instead. The judges' verdict was that the figures were not sufficiently idealised — which, translated, meant they were not sufficiently Greek. Too particular. Too much like a real body in a real room.

What he had actually made is one of the quietest pictures in French art. The light is even and cool, the space shallow, the greens and creams almost nothing. Every incident is in the drawing: the crease at the waist, the weight of the far shoulder, the small dark opening of the bath at the left. Kenneth Clark thought this back one of the great achievements of European painting, and painters have agreed for two centuries.

The title has nothing to do with the picture. It comes from a later owner, the Valpinçon family — friends of the young Degas, who saw it hanging in their house. The painting entered the Louvre in the nineteenth century and has been there ever since.

And Ingres could not leave her alone. He put the same figure, from the same angle, into a harem scene in 1828. Then, in 1862, at eighty-two, he set her at the very centre of *The Turkish Bath*, seen from behind exactly as here, now playing a long-necked lute among two dozen other women. Fifty-four years, one back.

## The World Behind It

Ingres was in Rome because Napoleon's France had a system: the state trained its painters, sent the best of them to Italy at public expense, and expected them to come home and glorify the regime in the correct classical style. His own teacher, Jacques-Louis David, had turned that style into the visual language of the Revolution and then of the Empire.

Rome in 1808 was under French control, full of French officials and French artists, and awash with the antiquities that were supposed to supply the models. Ingres looked at the antiquities and also at Raphael, who mattered more to him than any Greek, and quietly went his own way. The Academy's complaint that his nudes were not idealised enough is the first sign of the argument that would follow him for sixty years: whether a line that is beautiful is allowed to be anatomically wrong.

## The Artist at This Moment

He was twenty-eight, had waited five years for the French state to fund the scholarship he had won in 1801, and had arrived in Rome to discover that Paris critics had already savaged him. His response was to announce that he would stay in Italy and reform French painting from there. He meant it: he did not live in France again for eighteen years.

He was poor, largely unsold, and supporting himself on portrait drawings of tourists — small, exquisite pencil likenesses of visiting English and French gentry, done for a fee, which he regarded as beneath him and which are now treasured. He had not yet met his wife; he would marry her in 1813, sight unseen, after a courtship conducted entirely by letter.

He was, in short, an unsuccessful young man being told by every authority that his instincts were wrong, and continuing exactly as before.

## Interesting Facts

- It was a student exercise — part of the work Ingres owed the French state for his Prix de Rome scholarship — and the Academy judged the figure insufficiently idealised.
- The name comes from a later owner, not the subject; the Valpinçon family were friends of Degas, who studied the painting in their home.
- The same back, at the same angle, reappears at the centre of *The Turkish Bath*, which Ingres signed fifty-four years later at the age of eighty-two.
- The turban is a studio prop of a kind Ingres borrowed from Raphael's *La Fornarina*.
- Nothing in the picture identifies the woman, the place, or the moment — extremely unusual for a large nude in 1808.
- Ingres was in Rome only because the French state, having awarded him the prize in 1801, could not afford to send him until 1806.
