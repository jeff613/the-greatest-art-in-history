## The Story

The sitting lasted from one o'clock to four in the afternoon on 12 March 1538, in Brussels. Henry VIII's painter had been sent across the Channel to look at a girl. She was sixteen, a duchess, a widow, and dressed in black for a husband who had died when she was thirteen. Holbein had three hours to get her face; he made drawings, went back to London, and turned them into this.

She stands almost life-size against a plain blue-green wall, in a black satin mourning gown lined and edged with dark fur, her hands loosely folded, one glove off. There is no jewellery, no throne, no coat of arms, nothing in the room at all. Everything is in the face — the slight forward lean, the just-suppressed smile, the direct look. It is one of the most alive portraits of the sixteenth century, and it was made as a sales document.

The king was delighted. The imperial ambassador reported that after seeing the likeness Henry "has been in much better humour than he ever was, making musicians play on their instruments all day long." Negotiations for the marriage went on through 1538 and then quietly died. Christina, for her part, is supposed to have said that if she had two heads, one would be at the King of England's disposal — the most repeated line she ever spoke, and one nobody has traced to a contemporary source. What is documented is duller and just as clear: English agents in Brussels advised Cromwell that the king should look elsewhere.

Henry never met her. He married Anne of Cleves instead — another portrait commission for Holbein, and one that went far worse. He kept Christina's picture until he died in 1547.

Its second brush with disaster came in 1909. The Duke of Norfolk, who owned it, gave the National Gallery a chance to match an offer he had received; the Gallery could not raise the money, and the painting went to a dealer who lined up the American collector Henry Clay Frick at £72,000. A public appeal by the newly founded National Art Collections Fund saved it at the last moment, thanks to an anonymous £40,000 from an Englishwoman who insisted her name never be revealed.

## The World Behind It

Jane Seymour had died in October 1537, days after giving Henry the son he had spent twenty years and two marriages trying to get. At forty-six the king was a widower with an heir and a bad reputation, and Thomas Cromwell went shopping in Europe for the next queen. Portraits were how the shopping was done: you could not send a princess on approval, so you sent a painter.

Christina was an unusually good catch and an unusually awkward one. Daughter of the deposed Christian II of Denmark and niece of the Emperor Charles V, she had been married off to Francesco II Sforza, Duke of Milan, at eleven and widowed at thirteen. She was also a great-niece of Catherine of Aragon, which meant the marriage needed a papal dispensation from a pope who had just excommunicated the groom. When Charles V and Francis I made peace at Nice in June 1538, the emperor no longer needed English friendship, and the negotiation lost its point. Christina went on to rule Lorraine as regent and outlived Henry by more than forty years.

## The Artist at This Moment

By 1538 Holbein was at the top of his profession and travelling on the king's business. He had painted the enormous Whitehall mural of Henry VIII the year before — the swaggering, planted, hands-on-hips image that still defines the king in the popular mind, destroyed by fire in 1698 and known only from copies. Now he was being sent abroad as a kind of official eye, dispatched to Brussels for Christina and to France for other candidates, sketching women in a few hours under the watch of their households.

It was a strange, exposed job. The painter's accuracy was a matter of state, and he had to flatter a sitter enough to please her family while telling his own king the truth. Holbein got away with it here. Two years later, after Henry took one look at Anne of Cleves and declared himself deceived, Cromwell was arrested and beheaded. Holbein kept his salary and his title, but the man who had made his English career was gone.

## Interesting Facts

- The whole likeness came from a single three-hour sitting, from 1 to 4 p.m. on 12 March 1538; the finished full-length was painted back in London from drawings.
- Christina was sixteen and in mourning for Francesco Sforza, Duke of Milan, whom she had married at eleven and lost at thirteen.
- Henry VIII reportedly reacted to the likeness by keeping musicians playing all day; he kept the painting for the rest of his life.
- The famous quip that she would lend Henry one of her heads if she had two is not traceable to any contemporary source, and is best treated as legend.
- In 1909 the picture was days away from being sold to the American collector Henry Clay Frick; a public appeal and a £40,000 gift from an anonymous Englishwoman kept it in London.
- The background is bare and the sitter wears no jewels — extremely unusual for a dynastic marriage portrait, where displaying wealth was normally half the point.
