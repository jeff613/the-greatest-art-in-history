## The Story

There is no ground in this painting, and no sky — only water. Lily pads drift in floating clusters, pink and white blossoms open across the surface, and everything else is reflection: clouds, willows, and blue depths mirrored in the pond so that up and down lose their meaning. Look for a horizon and there isn't one. Monet has tipped the whole world onto its surface and asked you to fall into it.

The pond was his own. When Monet finally had money, in the 1890s, he bought the land beyond his Giverny garden, got official permission to divert a small river, and dug an ornamental water garden with a Japanese-style footbridge and beds of imported lilies. Neighbors grumbled that his exotic plants would poison the water. Monet ignored them. He hired gardeners whose whole job was to tend the pond — to dust the lily pads and keep the surface clear — and then he painted it, over and over, for the last three decades of his life.

This 1906 canvas belongs to a series he showed together in Paris in 1909 under the title "Water Landscapes." By then Monet had stopped painting the bridge, the banks, anything that placed the pond in a normal world. He cropped tighter and tighter until only the water remained, a surface that was somehow also a depth. Critics who saw the series realized he had done something strange and new: dissolved landscape into pure sensation, a shimmering field of color that pointed straight toward the abstract painting of the century to come. The mocked young radical of 1874 had, in his sixties, quietly leapt ahead of everyone again.

## The World Behind It

Monet built his water garden at the height of the Belle Époque, the glittering, confident decades before the First World War when Paris was the capital of the modern world. He also built it in the middle of a French love affair with Japan. Cheap Japanese woodblock prints had flooded Europe, and their flattened space, high viewpoints, and asymmetry reshaped how a generation of painters saw. Monet collected them by the hundreds — his Giverny walls were covered in them — and his arched green footbridge and lily pond were, in part, a Frenchman's dream of a Japanese garden.

Giverny itself had become a kind of shrine. As Monet's fame spread, young painters, especially Americans, flocked to the village to live near him, forming an art colony in his shadow. He mostly kept them at arm's length. The garden was not a public attraction but a private engine for his work — a landscape he had designed, planted, and controlled specifically so that he could spend the rest of his life painting one thing in every possible light.

## The Artist at This Moment

Monet was sixty-five and, at last, secure. The poverty was long behind him; his paintings sold for high prices, his household at Giverny ran to gardeners and servants, and he presided over it as the grand old man of French art. His second wife, Alice, was at his side, and the endless family of their combined children filled the pink house by the garden.

But contentment never made Monet stop pushing. He worked on the water landscapes with the intensity of a much younger man — and with a mounting frustration that shadowed his final decades. His eyesight was beginning to fail; within a few years cataracts would fog his vision and shift the colors he saw toward muddy reds and yellows. He would destroy canvases in fits of dissatisfaction, delay exhibitions, and complain that the water and light escaped him. Out of that struggle would come his last great project: the enormous, room-filling Water Lilies murals he painted nearly blind and gave to the French nation as a monument to peace. This luminous 1906 pond is that obsession still in its prime, before the darkness closed in.

## Interesting Facts

- Monet engineered the pond himself, petitioning the local authorities to divert the river Ru so he could feed his water garden — over the objections of neighbors who feared his strange plants would foul the stream.
- He employed a full-time gardener just for the pond, whose duties included rowing out to wash dust and soot off the lily pads before Monet painted them.
- The Art Institute of Chicago's canvas is one of a series of about forty-eight water-landscape paintings Monet exhibited together in Paris in 1909.
- In his final years Monet painted the vast Water Lilies murals for the state while his sight was ruined by cataracts; they were installed in the Orangerie in Paris in 1927, a few months after his death.
- The water surface is the whole subject: there is no bank, no sky, and no fixed viewpoint — a radical erasure of landscape that later abstract painters treated as a founding gesture.
