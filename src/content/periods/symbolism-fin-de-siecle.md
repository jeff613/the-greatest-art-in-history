name: "Symbolism & Fin de Siècle"
order: 10
years: "c. 1886–1918"
---

By the 1890s, the great empires of Europe had never looked richer, or felt more uneasy. Vienna glittered — coffee houses, chandeliers, a court still dressed for a century that was ending — while two streets from the palace, Freud was excavating the unconscious. Gustav Klimt caught that contradiction better than anyone: women wrapped in real gold leaf, erotic and imperial, painted for patrons who would be stateless within a generation. Symbolism was Impressionism's opposite instinct. Where the Impressionists chased the visible world, the Symbolists distrusted it, certain that the real subject — desire, death, dread, the soul — lived somewhere the eye could not reach. In Oslo, Edvard Munch turned a walk along a fjord into a shriek that has never stopped echoing, sky bleeding red over a figure clutching its own skull. Odilon Redon painted eyeballs floating like balloons and spiders wearing human faces. These were the last great pictures made before the guns of August 1914 ended the century's nerves for good — art that sensed catastrophe coming and painted the dream just before the world woke into the trenches.
