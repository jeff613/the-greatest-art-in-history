---
name: "Romanticism"
order: 6
years: "c. 1780–1850"
---

Europe was on fire. Revolution in France sent a king to the guillotine; Napoleon marched armies from Madrid to Moscow; every old certainty — throne, church, the tidy clockwork universe of the Enlightenment — was cracking. Painters stopped taking orders from academies and started taking dictation from their own nerves. Reason had promised progress and delivered the Terror, so artists went looking for truth in everything reason could not tame: storms, madness, nightmares, God, the ocean at night. Géricault hauled a raft of dying castaways onto a canvas the size of a wall and made a government scandal of it. Goya, stone-deaf in a Spain gutted by war, painted atrocities no official history would admit, then covered the walls of his own house with visions of witches and devouring gods. Friedrich put lone figures on cliff edges, staring into fog. The Romantics made one discovery that still governs art: a painting could be less a window onto the world than a confession.
