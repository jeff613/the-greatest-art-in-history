For centuries, the grand scale of history painting had been reserved for kings, gods, and battles. Gustave Courbet decided a village funeral deserved the same treatment. His Burial at Ornans filled a canvas twenty feet wide with ordinary mourners — a gravedigger, a dog, red-nosed peasants in black — painted with the same gravity once given to saints. Critics were appalled; this was supposed to be ugliness, not art. Jean-François Millet stirred the same outrage painting gleaners bent double in a field, their poverty rendered too plainly to be picturesque. Then Édouard Manet took the fight straight into the Salon and lost it spectacularly: his Olympia, a naked Parisian courtesan staring back at the viewer without apology, provoked jeers and guards posted to protect the canvas from walking sticks. Manet had lifted the pose from Titian and the composition from the old masters, then dropped it into contemporary Paris with all its transactions laid bare. Realism insisted that dignity was not the property of myth and monarchy — that laborers, prostitutes, and the poor belonged on the same walls as history's heroes.
