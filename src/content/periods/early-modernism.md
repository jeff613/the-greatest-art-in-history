Cézanne died in 1906 still muttering that nature was cylinder, sphere, and cone — and the painters who inherited his instructions took them apart entirely. In cramped Paris ateliers, Picasso and Braque spent years passing canvases back and forth, fracturing guitars and faces into planes seen from six angles at once, sometimes leaving them unsigned so no one could tell whose hand had made which shard. Matisse answered with color let off its leash — a woman's face split green and orange down the nose, by a critic's sneer, "wild beasts," fauves. Photography could already do likeness faster and truer than any brush; painting stopped competing with the camera and started asking what a picture was even for. By 1912 Kandinsky was making canvases with no subject at all, just color arguing with itself. In barely two decades the result was a wholesale demolition of five hundred years of picture-making — the flat plane winning, once and for all, its argument with the window. Here the museum's chronology stops: the artists who came after remain within living copyright, not yet free to hang on these walls.
