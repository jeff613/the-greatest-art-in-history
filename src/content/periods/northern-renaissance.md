While Florence chased marble gods, the cities of Flanders were solving a different problem: how do you paint devotion so exact it looks like a miracle? Jan van Eyck and his generation perfected a new medium — oil, layered thin as glass, glazed again and again until a fur collar or a brass chandelier seemed to hold real light. Every thread, every reflection, every drop of water was rendered as if God were watching through a magnifying glass, because for these painters, He was. And this art did not answer to popes and princes alone. Bruges and Antwerp were trading cities, thick with wool merchants and bankers who wanted their own faces, their own rooms, their own dogs immortalized with the same reverence as saints. Then a goldsmith's son from Nuremberg, Albrecht Dürer, crossed the Alps, studied what Italy was doing with proportion and perspective, and carried it home in his sketchbooks — fusing northern precision with southern ambition. The result was a Renaissance built not on ruins, but on ledgers, workshops, and unbearably close attention.
