---
name: "Gothic & Proto-Renaissance"
order: 1
years: "c. 1290–1420"
---

For a thousand years, the Byzantine icon had been the only acceptable way to paint a saint: flat, golden, timeless, staring out from a world with no weather and no weight. Then, in the hill towns of Tuscany, painters started asking a heretical question — what if holiness could look like a person? Cimabue's Madonnas still floated on gold leaf, remote as empresses. His pupil Giotto broke the spell. He gave his figures shoulders that carried weight, faces that grieved, robes that fell the way cloth actually falls under gravity. In Padua, a banker named Enrico Scrovegni hired him to cover an entire chapel — commissioned, tradition says, to buy his father's soul out of hell, the old man having grown rich lending at interest, a sin the Church did not forgive lightly. Giotto filled its walls with a Last Judgment and a Christ so plainly a suffering human that pilgrims wept before it. Gold ground did not vanish — it lingered for another century, dazzling and flat — but something had cracked open. Painting had learned to breathe, and it would not stop.
