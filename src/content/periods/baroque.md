The Catholic Church, bleeding followers to the Reformation, made a calculated bet: if argument could not hold the faithful, awe would. Rome commissioned art as overwhelming as thunder — saints caught mid-ecstasy, light slashing through darkness, ceilings that seemed to open onto heaven itself. Its greatest painter, Caravaggio, pulled his models from taverns and brothels, painted them as apostles, and spent his last years on the run for killing a man. Meanwhile, in the Protestant north, a republic of merchants was inventing a different kind of picture. The Dutch had no pope and no king to please, so they bought paintings of themselves: their cheese, their ships, their windswept skies, their own well-fed faces. A prosperous cobbler might own a dozen canvases. Out of this first mass art market rose Rembrandt, who made fortunes, lost them, buried nearly everyone he loved, and kept painting his own aging face with a honesty that still feels almost too private to look at.
