It began in a city of bankers. Florence's merchants had money, ambition, and a nagging suspicion that the ancients had known something everyone since had forgotten. So they dug — literally, pulling Roman statues out of the ground — and what they found rewired the European imagination. Man, not just God, could be the measure of things. Painters who had been anonymous craftsmen became celebrities courted by popes and princes. Brunelleschi raised a dome no one believed could stand. Leonardo dissected corpses by candlelight to learn how a smile works. Michelangelo and Raphael worked within shouting distance of each other in the Vatican, seething with rivalry. It was not a gentle golden age — plague swept the cities, Savonarola burned paintings in the public square, and the Medici bought glory with blood money. But for two centuries, art carried the full weight of human ambition, and it has never entirely put that weight down since.
