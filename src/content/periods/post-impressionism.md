The Impressionists had won their war — and their heirs almost immediately declared it insufficient. Catching the light was marvelous, but what about everything light cannot show: feeling, memory, faith, the structure hiding under the surface of things? They were never a movement, barely a generation on speaking terms — a handful of stubborn loners, each pushing color and form toward a private obsession. Cézanne, alone in Provence, painted one mountain until it became architecture; Van Gogh — poor, unsold, feverish between breakdowns — twisted brushstroke into pure emotion. And as the century died, others turned the same instinct inward, toward desire and dread. In Vienna, Klimt wrapped women in real gold leaf, erotic and imperial, painted for patrons who would be stateless within a generation. In Oslo, Munch turned a walk along a fjord into a shriek that has never stopped echoing. However far apart they worked, they were making the same discovery: that a painting could be about what happens inside a head. Everything modern traces back to them.
