---
name: "Post-Impressionism"
order: 8
years: "c. 1885–1910"
---

The Impressionists had won their war — and their heirs almost immediately declared it insufficient. Catching the light was marvelous, but what about everything light cannot show: feeling, memory, faith, the structure hiding under the surface of things? The painters who came after were not a movement; they barely tolerated one another. They were a handful of stubborn loners, each pushing color and form toward a private obsession. Seurat built shimmering scenes out of scientific dots. Cézanne, alone in Provence, spent decades painting one mountain until it became architecture. Gauguin abandoned a bank career, a wife, and five children, and sailed for Tahiti chasing a paradise that did not exist. And Van Gogh — poor, unsold, working at feverish speed between breakdowns — twisted color and brushstroke into pure emotion. Nearly all of them died before the world caught up. When it finally did, everything that followed — Matisse's wild color, Picasso's shattered forms, modern art itself — traced straight back to these few isolated, half-mad, wholly convinced painters.
