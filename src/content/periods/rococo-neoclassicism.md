---
name: "Rococo & Neoclassicism"
order: 4
years: "c. 1700–1820"
---

For most of the eighteenth century, French painting existed to flatter a class that had nothing left to prove. Watteau invented whole worlds of picnics that never ended; Fragonard painted a girl on a swing, her shoe flying off toward an unseen lover, in colors soft as spun sugar. It was art built for boudoirs and country houses, pastel and weightless, made to be looked at while reclining. Then the Bastille fell, the king lost his head in a public square, and pastel silk suddenly looked like evidence. A new generation wanted paintings that argued for something — sacrifice, discipline, republican virtue carved in the hard clean lines of ancient Rome. Jacques-Louis David, who had once painted for the doomed court, remade himself as the Revolution's chief image-maker, staging his figures like statues and his stories like civic oaths. He would go on to crown Napoleon on canvas as coolly as he had once mourned the Revolution's martyrs — the same hand serving crown, republic, and empire in turn. One century held two entirely opposed dreams — pleasure without consequence, then consequence without pleasure — and both still shape how we picture power today.
